{{< drg/requirement number="5a3" >}}
Make two objects out of 24- or 26-gauge sheet metal. Use patterns either provided by your counselor or made by you and approved by your counselor. Construct these objects using a metal that is appropriate to the object's ultimate purpose, and using cutting, bending, edging, and either soldering or brazing.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This is where tinsmithing becomes real fabrication. You are taking your approved plan and turning it into finished objects with accurate cuts, square bends, and secure joints. The exact objects may differ, but the core lesson is the same: every step affects the next one.

### Build in a Smart Order

A strong sequence usually looks like this:

1. transfer the pattern and dimensions
2. cut the parts cleanly
3. remove burrs and sharp edges
4. form bends and hems in the right order
5. fit parts together before joining
6. solder, braze, or rivet as required
7. clean and protect the finished piece

If you jump ahead, you can trap yourself. For example, a seam made too early may block a later bend. A bend made first may make a cut line harder to reach.

## Requirement 5a3a: Add a Riveted Component

{{< drg/inherited-requirement number="5a3a" req_path="5.a.3.a" topic="One object also must include at least one riveted component." />}}

A rivet is more than a fastener. It teaches alignment, hole placement, and tight mechanical joining. For a riveted part to work well, the pieces must fit before you set the rivet. Sloppy holes or shifting parts usually lead to weak or ugly results.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Drill or punch rivet holes only after checking that the pieces line up exactly as they will be assembled. A small offset can twist the whole project.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Requirement 5a3b: Protect Against Oxidation

{{< drg/inherited-requirement number="5a3b" req_path="5.a.3.b" topic="If you do not make your objects from zinc-plated sheet steel or tin-plated sheet steel, preserve your work from oxidation." />}}

Some sheet metals need extra protection after fabrication. If your metal is not already protected by plating, your counselor may have you clean it, coat it, wax it, paint it, or otherwise finish it so moisture and air do not attack the surface quickly.

This is an important real-world lesson: a project is not truly finished just because it holds together. A good finish helps it survive handling, weather, and time.

{{< drg/image src="images/sheet-metal-joining-sequence.avif" alt="A step-by-step sequence showing a sheet metal edge being cut, deburred, bent, fitted, riveted, and then finished for corrosion protection" >}}

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Freshly cut sheet metal is sharp enough to slice skin badly. Deburr edges early, handle parts carefully, and never slide your fingers along a cut edge to “check” it.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

The best sheet-metal objects look simple when they are done. That usually means the maker planned carefully, worked in order, and corrected small errors before they became big ones.
