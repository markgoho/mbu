Do the following: Each piece is to be painted, glazed, or otherwise decorated by you.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This requirement is where pottery becomes real. You are not just talking about clay anymore. You are shaping it in several different ways, finishing the surface yourself, and learning what happens before a piece is ready for the kiln.

The parent instruction matters here: every piece needs your own decorative choices. That does not mean every surface has to be fancy. It does mean you should think intentionally about color, texture, pattern, carving, or glaze rather than leaving the finish as an afterthought.

## Requirement 5a

{{< drg/inherited-requirement number="5a" req_path="5.a" topic="Make a slab pot, a coil pot, and a pinch pot." />}}

These three forms teach the core hand-building methods. Each one helps you understand clay structure a little differently.

### Slab pot

A slab pot starts with rolled-out sheets of clay. You cut shapes, score the edges, add slip, and join the pieces into a box, cup, tray, or other form. Slab work rewards careful measuring and clean edges.

### Coil pot

A coil pot is built from rope-like coils stacked one row at a time. Coils can stay visible for texture or be smoothed together into a continuous wall. This method is great for building taller forms gradually.

### Pinch pot

A pinch pot starts as a ball of clay with your thumb pressed into the center. You pinch and rotate the clay to open it evenly. It is one of the oldest pottery methods and one of the fastest ways to learn wall thickness by feel.

{{< drg/checklist title="What makes hand-built pots stronger" subtitle="Use the same habits on all three forms" >}}

- **Keep wall thickness even** so one side does not dry or fire differently from another.
- **Score and slip joins well** so seams do not split apart.
- **Compress the clay** with fingers, ribs, or paddles to strengthen the surface.
- **Dry slowly** under light plastic if parts seem to be drying at different speeds.
  {{< /drg/checklist >}}

## Requirement 5b

{{< drg/inherited-requirement number="5b" req_path="5.b" topic="Make a human or animal figurine or decorative sculpture." />}}

Sculpture asks you to think less like a vessel-maker and more like a builder. The goal is shape, gesture, balance, and character.

Start with the biggest forms first. If you want to make an owl, dragon, or face, block in the body mass before fussing over eyes, feathers, or scales. Thin details can come later when the main structure is stable.

### Strong sculpture habits

- Build around a solid form, not a pile of tiny details.
- Watch for weak overhangs like arms, ears, wings, or tails.
- Hollow very thick sections if your instructor recommends it so the piece dries and fires more safely.
- Add texture only after the main proportions look right.

{{< drg/tip >}}
When a figurine looks wrong, step back and check the silhouette. If the outline does not read clearly from a distance, more detail usually will not fix it.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Requirement 5c

{{< drg/inherited-requirement number="5c" req_path="5.c" topic="Throw a functional form on a potter's wheel." />}}

A functional form is something meant to be used: a cup, bowl, cylinder, or small vase. For most beginners, a straight cylinder is the best starting point because it teaches centering, opening, and pulling walls.

### Key wheel-throwing stages

1. **Center the clay** so it spins without wobbling.
2. **Open the middle** while keeping enough clay in the base.
3. **Pull the walls upward** with steady pressure.
4. **Shape the form** without making one area too thin.
5. **Compress the rim** so it stays stronger.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="If the clay keeps collapsing on the wheel" >}}
Do not assume you have failed. Most wheel problems come from a few repeat causes.

- **Check the speed**: Too fast can make the walls flare wildly.
- **Check the water**: Too much water can turn the clay weak and slippery.
- **Check your pressure**: Uneven pressure makes wobble worse.
- **Re-center if needed**: Starting over early is often faster than fighting a crooked form.
  {{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

## Requirement 5d

{{< drg/inherited-requirement number="5d" req_path="5.d" topic="Help an experienced adult to load and fire a kiln OR describe in detail how to load and fire a kiln." />}}

This part is about process and safety. Even if you do not operate the kiln yourself, you should understand the sequence clearly enough to describe it.

### Loading a kiln

Pieces must be fully dry before firing. Potters place work with enough space for heat to circulate and for glazes not to run onto other pots or kiln shelves. Kiln shelves and posts create levels, and nothing should touch a heating element or block the kiln from closing properly.

### Firing a kiln

A firing raises temperature in a controlled way. Early in the firing, leftover moisture must leave the clay safely. Later, the clay matures and the glaze melts at the right temperature range. Cones or digital controls help confirm the firing reached the intended amount of heat work.

### Cooling matters too

Opening a kiln too early can crack ware or injure someone. Cooling is part of the firing, not an optional extra step.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Never load, unload, or open a kiln without an experienced adult guiding the process. Hot shelves, hot ware, and sudden temperature changes can cause serious burns or ruin the work.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Bringing the whole requirement together

The four parts of Req 5 teach a big pottery truth: making is not one skill. It is a set of related skills. Hand-building teaches structure. Sculpture teaches form. Wheel throwing teaches control. Kiln knowledge teaches patience and process. Decoration ties all of it together by asking you to finish your work with intention.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
A piece that looks finished while it is leather hard is still only halfway through its life. Drying, bisque firing, glazing, glaze firing, and cooling all still lie ahead.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}
