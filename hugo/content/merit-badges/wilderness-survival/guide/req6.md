{{< drg/requirement number="6" >}}
Using three different methods (other than matches), build and light three fires.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This is one of the most hands-on requirements in the badge. You must actually build and light fires using three different fire-starting methods — not just describe them. Practice matters here: fire-starting without matches is a skill that takes real repetition to develop.

{{< drg/image src="images/fire-triangle-diagram.avif" alt="A triangle diagram with the three sides labeled Fuel, Oxygen, and Heat, with a flame icon in the center" >}}

## The Fire Triangle

Every fire requires three things simultaneously: **heat, fuel, and oxygen**. Understanding this triangle helps you troubleshoot when a fire won't start.

- **Heat source** — the initial ignition (your fire-starting method)
- **Fuel** — tinder, kindling, and larger wood
- **Oxygen** — airflow through your fire lay

If your fire won't catch, one of these three is missing or insufficient. Most beginners fail on tinder — they try to light wood that's too large or too damp.

## The Fire Lay: Before You Strike the Spark

The most common fire-starting failure has nothing to do with the ignition method — it's a poorly prepared fire lay. No fire-starting method creates enough sustained heat to catch large wood directly.

**The three fuel tiers:**

1. **Tinder:** Catches the initial spark or coal. Must be bone dry, fine, and easily combustible. Examples: dried grass, cattail fluff, shredded dry bark (cedar, birch), dry leaves, commercial cotton ball + petroleum jelly, char cloth.

2. **Kindling:** Small dry sticks, 1/4 inch to 1/2 inch in diameter. Feeds the ember from your tinder bundle until you have sustainable flame.

3. **Fuel wood:** Wrist-thick and larger. Added once kindling is burning well.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Make a tinder bundle in the shape of a bird's nest — cupped hands-size, with the finest material in the center. This is where you'll place your coal or ember, fold the bundle around it, and blow gently from the bottom to nurse it into flame.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Method 1: Flint and Steel (or Ferro Rod)

A ferro rod (ferrocerium rod) is the most reliable modern non-match fire starter. It works when wet, at altitude, and in temperatures well below freezing.

**What you need:** Ferro rod + striker (can be the back of a knife blade)

**How it works:** Scraping the striker across the ferro rod produces sparks at approximately 3,000°F — hot enough to catch char cloth or fine tinder instantly.

**Process:**
1. Prepare your tinder bundle
2. Hold the ferro rod 1–2 inches above the tinder bundle, angled into it
3. Press the striker firmly against the rod and push the rod *away* from the tinder (not the striker across the rod — this controls where sparks land)
4. Repeat until a spark catches
5. Gently blow from below to develop the coal into flame

**Common mistakes:** Striking too fast (scatters sparks widely), holding the rod too far from the tinder (sparks cool before landing), tinder that's too large.

## Method 2: Magnifying Lens (Solar)

A magnifying glass, binocular lens, or even a polished water bag can focus sunlight into a spot hot enough to ignite tinder.

**What you need:** Any convex lens or reflective bowl + sunlight

**How it works:** Focusing the sun's light concentrates its energy into a tiny point, generating enough heat to start smoldering.

**Process:**
1. Prepare a dark-colored, very dry tinder (dark materials absorb heat better — char cloth is ideal)
2. Angle the lens to create the smallest, sharpest possible dot of light on the tinder
3. Hold still — the dot must stay in one spot; moving it disperses the heat
4. Watch for a wisp of smoke, then a glowing coal
5. Transfer to your tinder bundle and blow gently

**Limitations:** Only works in direct, bright sunlight — useless in cloud cover, rain, or at night.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
In a pinch, a clear water-filled plastic bag or balloon pinched into a lens shape can focus enough sunlight to start a fire. A polished tin can bottom works as a parabolic reflector. People have started fires with ice lenses carved from clear ice and polished smooth. The physics works wherever light concentrates.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Method 3: Friction Fire (Bow Drill)

The bow drill is the most ancient human fire-starting technology and the most physically demanding. It takes significant practice to succeed, but it's also the most rewarding — because it works with just wood and a few basic tools.

**What you need:**
- **Fireboard** (also called a hearth board): Dry, soft-to-medium wood. Cedar, cottonwood, willow, mullein, or basswood work well. Hardwoods don't work.
- **Spindle:** Round, straight, dry, slightly harder than the fireboard
- **Bow:** A curved stick strung with a shoelace, paracord, or cordage
- **Handhold:** A smooth rock, shell, or hardwood piece with a depression to hold the spindle tip
- **Catch board or bark:** A small piece under the notch to catch the ember

**How it works:** Rotating the spindle rapidly against the fireboard generates heat through friction, producing a fine black powder (wood dust). With sustained friction, this powder reaches combustion temperature and becomes a glowing coal.

**Process:**
1. Cut a small depression in the fireboard where the spindle tip will sit, then cut a V-shaped notch from the depression to the edge of the board. Place catch material under the notch.
2. Loop the bow string once around the spindle.
3. Kneel with one foot on the fireboard, holding the handhold firmly above the spindle.
4. Draw the bow back and forth in long, even strokes — use your full arm, not your wrist.
5. Apply consistent downward pressure through the handhold.
6. Stroke at a medium-fast, steady pace until you see smoke, then stroke faster with heavier pressure.
7. When you stop and check the notch, a glowing coal should be present.
8. Carefully transfer the coal to your tinder bundle and blow gently from below.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Always build fires in a cleared area with a water source or dirt nearby for extinguishing. Build away from dry brush, overhanging branches, and tent materials. Never leave a fire unattended. Fully extinguish every fire — stir the ashes with water until cold to the touch, even if you think it's out.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Other Accepted Methods

Your three fires must use three *different* methods. Other accepted options:
- **Battery + steel wool** — touching steel wool to a battery terminal ignites it
- **Hand drill** — friction fire without a bow (harder than bow drill, requires specific wood combinations)
- **Fire piston** — compressed air generates heat
- **Commercial fire starters** (except matches) — ferrocerium lighters, plasma lighters, piezoelectric lighters

{{< drg/checklist title="Pre-Practice Checklist" subtitle="Set yourself up for success before your counselor meeting" >}}
- Practice each method at least three separate times before your demonstration
- Gather your tinder, kindling, and fuel before beginning
- Test your fireboard/spindle wood combination (not all wood pairings work for bow drill)
- Have water or a dirt pile nearby for safe extinguishing
- Choose a weather-appropriate location (wind and moisture affect every method)
{{< /drg/checklist >}}
