{{< drg/requirement number="6" >}}
Fire Building. Using three different methods (other than matches), build and light three fires.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Fire is the one survival skill that touches nearly every other priority. It warms you, dries your clothes, purifies your water, cooks your food, signals rescuers, lights your camp, and lifts your spirits. Knowing how to build a fire without matches means you can create fire from the materials around you — no matter what has gone wrong with your gear.

## Fire Fundamentals

Before you learn the ignition methods, understand the fire triangle: fire needs **heat**, **fuel**, and **oxygen**. Remove any one and the fire dies. Your job as a fire builder is to introduce heat to fuel while ensuring adequate airflow.

### The Three Stages of Fuel

Every successful fire follows the same progression. Trying to light a large log with a spark is like trying to start a car in fifth gear — it will not work.

**Tinder** — The first material to catch. Tinder must ignite from a small spark or ember. It burns hot and fast.
- Cotton balls coated in petroleum jelly (the best — burns for 3–5 minutes)
- Dryer lint
- Dry grass bundled loosely
- Fine wood shavings (feather sticks)
- Birch bark (contains flammable oils, ignites even when damp)
- Fatwood (resin-saturated wood from pine stumps)
- Char cloth (cotton fabric burned in a sealed tin until charred)

**Kindling** — Small sticks and twigs, pencil-thickness to finger-thickness. Kindling catches from burning tinder and builds enough heat to ignite larger fuel.

**Fuel wood** — Wrist-thickness to arm-thickness pieces that sustain the fire. Start with smaller fuel wood and add larger pieces as the fire grows.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Gather all three fuel stages before you attempt ignition. Nothing is more frustrating than getting a tinder bundle lit and then scrambling to find kindling while your fire burns out. Have a fist-sized pile of tinder, a double handful of kindling, and an armload of fuel wood ready before you strike your first spark.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Fire Lay Structures

How you arrange your fuel matters. These are the most effective structures:

**Tepee** — Stand kindling upright in a cone shape around your tinder bundle. As tinder ignites, flames rise into the kindling. Easy to light but can collapse; rebuild if needed.

**Log cabin** — Stack kindling in alternating layers (like Lincoln Logs) around a tinder core. Burns evenly and creates a good coal bed. More stable than a tepee.

**Lean-to** — Push a green stick into the ground at an angle. Lean kindling against it over a tinder bundle. Good in windy conditions because the stick acts as a windbreak.

## Three Matchless Fire Methods

You need to demonstrate three different ignition methods. Here are the most practical options:

### 1. Ferrocerium (Ferro) Rod

A ferro rod is a metal alloy rod that produces extremely hot sparks (over 5,000°F) when scraped with a steel striker. It is the most reliable matchless fire-starting method and works when wet.

**How to use:**
1. Prepare your tinder bundle — cotton balls with petroleum jelly or fine wood shavings work best.
2. Hold the ferro rod close to the tinder, almost touching.
3. Place the striker against the rod and push the rod backward (away from the tinder) while holding the striker still. This prevents you from scattering the tinder.
4. Direct the shower of sparks onto the tinder. It should catch within a few strikes.
5. Gently blow on the ember to grow the flame, then transfer to your fire lay.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Ferrocerium was invented in 1903 by Austrian chemist Carl Auer von Welsbach — the same inventor who created the modern gas mantle for lanterns. The same alloy has been used in cigarette lighter flints for over a century.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

### 2. Flint and Steel

The traditional method used by frontiersmen and early Scouts. You strike a piece of high-carbon steel against a sharp edge of flint (or other hard stone like jasper, chert, or quartz) to produce sparks.

**How to use:**
1. Hold a piece of char cloth or tinder fungus on top of the flint, just above the sharp edge.
2. Strike the steel against the flint in a downward glancing blow. Sparks should land on the char cloth.
3. When a spark catches the char cloth (you will see a glowing ember spread), transfer it to a tinder bundle of dry grass or bark fibers.
4. Gently blow on the bundle until it bursts into flame.

Flint and steel requires practice. The sparks are cooler than a ferro rod, so you need excellent tinder — char cloth is almost essential.

### 3. Magnesium Fire Starter

A magnesium block with an embedded ferro rod. You shave magnesium flakes (which burn at over 5,000°F) into a pile and then ignite them with sparks from the ferro rod.

**How to use:**
1. Scrape magnesium shavings from the block onto your tinder using the back of your knife blade. Create a pile about the size of a quarter.
2. Keep the shavings sheltered from wind — they are lightweight and scatter easily.
3. Strike the ferro rod side of the block to throw sparks onto the magnesium shavings.
4. The magnesium ignites in a brilliant white flash. Have your tinder bundle ready to catch.

### Other Methods Worth Knowing

- **Bow drill** — a friction method that creates an ember by spinning a wooden spindle against a fireboard. It works, but requires specific wood types, significant practice, and considerable physical effort. Most survival instructors consider it a last-resort method.
- **Fire piston** — compresses air rapidly in a cylinder to ignite tinder (same principle as a diesel engine). Effective but requires a specialized tool.
- **Magnifying lens** — focuses sunlight to a point on tinder. Only works on sunny days and takes patience, but uses equipment you might already have (glasses, binocular lens, water-filled clear bag).

## Wet Weather Fire Building

Starting a fire in the rain is one of the hardest outdoor skills — and one of the most important, because wet conditions are exactly when you need a fire most.

**Finding dry fuel:**
- Look for dead standing wood — trees that died upright. The bark sheds rain while the inner wood stays dry.
- Split larger pieces of wood to expose the dry interior.
- Birch bark contains oils that burn even when soaked.
- Check under rock overhangs, dense conifers, and fallen logs for protected dry materials.

**Protecting your fire:**
- Build a fire platform from green logs to keep your fire above wet ground.
- Create a windscreen and rain shield from a tarp, bark slab, or large piece of wood propped at an angle.
- Start with your most reliable tinder (petroleum jelly cotton balls ignite even when damp) and build slowly.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Build fires in safe locations: away from dry grass, overhanging branches, and tent materials. Clear a 10-foot diameter area around your fire. Keep water or dirt nearby to extinguish it. Never leave a fire unattended. When you are done, drown the fire, stir the ashes, drown again, and feel with the back of your hand — if it is too hot to touch, it is too hot to leave.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}
