{{< drg/requirement number="6" >}}
Using three different methods (other than matches), build and light three fires.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

You'll build three separate fires using three different fire-starting methods. This demonstrates mastery of fire-building fundamentals and proves you can start a fire even if your primary method fails.

## The Three Fire Methods

### Method 1: Lighter or Ferrocerium Rod

**Using a lighter (butane or Zippo-style):**
1. Gather tinder (dry bark, leaves, cotton, or firestarter)
2. Gather kindling (small twigs, pencil-thick branches)
3. Gather fuel wood (arm-thickness branches and logs)
4. Arrange tinder in a small pile
5. Light the tinder with the lighter
6. Add kindling progressively as the fire grows
7. Add larger fuel wood once the kindling is burning well

**Using a ferrocerium rod (ferro rod):**
1. Prepare tinder, kindling, and fuel like above
2. Hold the ferro rod against the tinder at a 45-degree angle
3. Strike the rod with a metal striker or the spine of a knife, directing sparks toward the tinder
4. Once the tinder catches, add kindling and larger wood

The advantage of a ferro rod: **it works when wet.** The sparks are hot enough to ignite tinder even in damp conditions.

### Method 2: Friction (Bow Drill or Hand Drill)

Friction creates heat through rapid wood-on-wood movement. This is slow and difficult, but it demonstrates genuine fire-starting skill.

**Bow Drill:**
1. **Gather materials:**
   - Bow: a curved branch with a string (paracord or natural fiber)
   - Spindle: a straight stick about pencil-thickness and 10–12 inches long
   - Fire board: a flat piece of wood with a shallow groove along one edge
   - Socket: a rock or bone with a slight depression to hold the top of the spindle
   - Tinder bundle: very dry, fine material (cedar bark, dandelion fluff, char cloth)

2. **Create a notch:** Cut a V-shaped notch in the edge of the fire board where the spindle will rotate

3. **Set up:** Place tinder under the notch. Position the spindle in the groove. Place the socket on top of the spindle.

4. **Operate:** Move the bow back and forth with steady pressure on the spindle. Friction creates heat and fine wood dust that accumulates in the notch. After 1–2 minutes of steady work, the dust becomes hot enough to glow.

5. **Transfer to tinder:** Carefully move the hot dust to your tinder bundle and blow gently to encourage flames.

This method is slow and requires significant effort, but it's the most reliable if you have the right materials.

**Hand Drill:**
- Similar principle but without the bow
- Hold the spindle vertically in a fire board and rotate it rapidly between your hands
- Requires strong hands and significant effort
- Less effective than bow drill but possible

### Method 3: Solar (Sun & Magnifier)

If you have clear sunlight and a magnifying glass (or glasses), you can use concentrated sunlight to start a fire.

1. **Focus sunlight:** Use a magnifying glass, lens from glasses, or even a clear water bottle to focus sunlight into a tight point
2. **Apply to tinder:** Aim the focal point at fine, dry tinder (black tinder works best—it absorbs more heat)
3. **Wait:** In strong sunlight, the tinder will smoke and then flame after 30 seconds to a minute
4. **Nurture the flame:** Blow gently and add kindling

This method only works on clear, sunny days and is slow, but it's a useful skill.

## The Build Sequence

Build each fire at least an hour apart (or in different locations) to safely complete three separate fires. Choose a safe location with:
- No overhanging branches
- Clear ground around the fire (at least 10 feet)
- No dry grass or leaves nearby
- Access to water for extinguishing

**The proper fire structure for all three:**

1. **Tinder bundle:** Dry leaves, bark, cotton, commercial firestarter
2. **Kindling:** Pencil-to-pinky-sized sticks, arranged in a "tepee" formation
3. **Fuel:** Wrist- to arm-thickness branches, added as the fire grows
4. **Air:** Leave gaps so oxygen can reach the burning wood

As each fire burns:
1. Light the tinder
2. Once it catches, add kindling in a tepee formation
3. Once kindling burns, add progressively larger fuel
4. Build the fire to a sustainable burn
5. Let it burn for 5–10 minutes to demonstrate it's stable
6. Extinguish completely and safely

## Extinguishing Safely

After each fire:
1. Let the fire burn down to coals
2. Spread the coals and ash
3. Stir everything with a stick, breaking up any wood pieces
4. Pour water over the entire fire area (use plenty)
5. Stir again and pour more water
6. Touch the ground to verify it's cool

The test: if you can comfortably hold your hand 3 inches above the ashes, it's cool enough to leave.

## Common Challenges

**Tinder won't light:** Your tinder is too wet or too large. Use finer, drier material (feather sticks—splitting a stick into thin curls—work well). Smaller is better.

**Kindling won't catch:** The tinder isn't hot enough, or there's not enough of it. Add more tinder before moving to kindling. Ensure kindling is dry and small enough.

**Fire smolders but won't flame:** Increase airflow. Blow gently on the base of the fire. Add smaller, drier kindling.

**Ferrocerium rod sparks aren't catching:** Aim the sparks directly into the tinder. Create a "tinder nest" so sparks land in the middle. Ensure the rod is held at the right angle.

**Bow drill won't create heat:** Increase pressure on the spindle. Move faster. Use materials with less friction resistance (cedar works better than oak). Practice takes dozens of attempts.

## Tips for Success

{{< drg/tip >}}
Prepare all materials before lighting anything. Gather tinder, kindling, and fuel wood in piles next to your fire site. Once you light it, you won't want to stop to gather wood.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

{{< drg/tip >}}
"Feather sticks" are game-changers. Split a stick from the end into thin curls without separating them completely. These catch fire easily and provide a bridge from tinder to larger kindling.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The bow drill was the most common fire-starting method for thousands of years, used by cultures worldwide. It requires no special materials—just wood and a string—which is why it's so valuable to learn.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Demonstrating to Your Counselor

Have your counselor present (or take photos) of each fire:
- Fire 1: Lit with a lighter or ferro rod
- Fire 2: Friction method (bow drill preferred)
- Fire 3: Another method (solar, different friction technique, etc.)

Be able to explain:
- Why you chose these methods
- How each method works
- What challenges you encountered and how you overcame them
- How you'd start a fire in wet conditions
- Fire safety and suppression
