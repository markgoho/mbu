Using three different methods (other than matches), build and light three fires.
{{</* /drg/requirement */>}}

This is the hands-on fire requirement. You'll demonstrate three different methods to start a fire—*without using matches*. This pushes you to master fire-starting techniques that work when your lighter dies or you lose your matches. By the end, you should be confident starting a fire under difficult conditions.

## Method 1: Ferrocerium Rod (Ferro Rod)

A ferrocerium rod is the most reliable backup fire starter. It creates sparks even when wet and works in almost any condition.

### **How It Works**

A ferro rod is a metal rod that produces sparks (3000°F+) when struck sharply. The sparks ignite tinder directly.

### **Required Materials**

- Ferrocerium rod (cost: $5–15)
- Striking surface (rough metal or the back of a knife)
- Tinder (cotton balls with vaseline, dry leaves, inner bark of dead trees, char cloth)
- Kindling (small dry twigs, pencil-thickness)
- Fuel wood (larger branches, progressively thicker)

{{</* drg/image src="images/ferro-rod-fire-starting.avif" alt="Sequence showing ferro rod fire-starting: hand holding ferro rod and striker, sparks flying, tinder glowing, flames in kindling" */>}}

### **Step-by-Step Fire Building**

1. **Gather tinder, kindling, and fuel wood.** (Critical: without all three sizes of material, you'll fail.) Arrange them within arm's reach.
2. **Create a tinder nest.** Gather the fluffiest, driest material into a nest shape (about the size of your fist).
3. **Strike the ferro rod sharply downward** over the tinder, pointing sparks into the nest. You need multiple strikes—20–30 is normal.
4. **As the tinder glows,** gently blow on it to increase oxygen and ignite flames.
5. **Add kindling immediately.** Place small twigs on top of the burning tinder in a teepee shape.
6. **Blow gently** to establish the kindling fire. Once kindling is burning well, gradually add larger twigs.
7. **Add fuel wood.** Lay progressively larger branches on top, allowing airflow between pieces.

### **Pro Tips**

- **Prepare tinder before striking.** Don't start striking with tinder not ready—you'll waste sparks.
- **Dry tinder is non-negotiable.** Wet tinder won't catch sparks. Find the driest material available.
- **Practice multiple times.** Ferro rods require technique. Your first fire might take 10 minutes. With practice, 2–3 minutes is normal.
- **Angle matters.** Strike at 45 degrees, pointing sparks downward into the tinder.

## Method 2: Flint and Steel (or Simplified Version: Char Cloth)

Flint and steel is a traditional method. A harder metal striking flint creates sparks that ignite char cloth (fabric pre-burned to be highly flammable).

### **Simplified Version: Lighter + Char Cloth**

For the Wilderness Survival badge, use a lighter to light char cloth—char cloth is a pre-prepared material that ignites from a tiny flame. It's less "primitive" than flint-and-steel but accomplishes the same goal and is more reliable.

### **Required Materials**

- Lighter (or matches, though the requirement says "other than matches" so a lighter is better)
- Char cloth (pre-burned cotton fabric—buy it or make it)
- Tinder, kindling, fuel wood (same as Method 1)

### **Step-by-Step Fire Building**

1. **Prepare your fire lay.** Arrange tinder, kindling, and fuel wood within reach.
2. **Light the char cloth with your lighter.** Char cloth ignites instantly and burns hot for 10–15 seconds.
3. **Immediately place the burning char cloth into your tinder nest.**
4. **Blow gently on the tinder.** It should catch quickly given the heat from the char cloth.
5. **Add kindling in a teepee shape** once the tinder is burning well.
6. **Gradually add larger fuel wood.**

### **Alternative: Flint and Steel (Historical)**

If you want to use the traditional flint-and-steel method:
1. Strike flint sharply against a steel tool (or specialized flint-and-steel kit).
2. Direct sparks onto char cloth.
3. Once char cloth is burning, proceed as above.

Flint-and-steel is harder and requires more practice, but it's impressive and requires no fuel (lighter/batteries).

## Method 3: Bow Drill or Hand Drill (Friction Fire)

Friction fire is the most primitive method—creating heat through friction until tinder ignites. It's challenging and requires technique, but it demonstrates deep fire-building knowledge.

### **What You Need**

- Wooden bow (or a curved branch)
- Cord for the bow (paracord, leather, or vine)
- Drill stick (straight, dry wood, pencil-thickness)
- Board or base (flat wood with a notch for the drill)
- Socket (something to hold the top of the drill—a stone or bone with a depression, or your hand holding a stick)
- Tinder (very dry, fine material—char cloth or finely shredded bark)

### **How Bow Drill Works**

The bow drill creates friction between a rapidly rotating drill and a wooden board. The friction generates heat (800°F+). The heated wood particles become embers in the tinder bundle.

### **Step-by-Step (Simplified)**

1. **Create a notched board:** Cut a shallow groove in a flat piece of wood. At the edge, cut a notch opening downward (this is where the ember will form).
2. **Secure the drill stick** in a vertical position at the beginning of the groove.
3. **Use the bow** to rapidly move back and forth, spinning the drill stick like a dentist's drill.
4. **Maintain downward pressure** while bowing. Too little pressure = no heat. Too much pressure = the drill stops.
5. **After 20–30 seconds of vigorous bowing,** an ember should form in the notch.
6. **Catch the ember** in your tinder bundle (char cloth or fine bark).
7. **Blow the ember** until it ignites into flames.
8. **Add tinder nest, kindling, and fuel wood** as in other methods.

### **Why It's Hard (And Why You're Demonstrating Skill)**

- Requires dry materials (friction fire doesn't work with damp wood)
- Demands consistent pressure and speed
- Takes 30–60 seconds of intense effort
- One mistake (losing pressure, wrong angle) and you start over

**If you master friction fire, you've demonstrated deep understanding of fire-starting principles.**

### **Alternatives to Full Bow Drill**

If full bow drill is too difficult:
- **Hand drill:** Use your hands to spin the drill instead of a bow. Harder physically but simpler to set up.
- **Simplified friction method:** Use a stick and board with a simpler setup. Less elegant but still demonstrates the principle.

## Fire Building Layout: Universal Principles

Regardless of ignition method, fire structure is the same:

**Tinder** (ignites from sparks/heat):
- Cotton with vaseline
- Dry inner bark
- Char cloth
- Fine, fluffy dry grass
- (Finely shredded cedar bark works great)

**Kindling** (burns quickly, creates heat):
- Small twigs (pencil-thickness)
- Small branches
- Arranged in a teepee or log-cabin shape to allow airflow

**Fuel wood** (larger wood that sustains fire):
- Progressively larger branches and logs
- Dry wood burns best; fresh/green wood smolders

The progression is critical: tinder → kindling → fuel. Skip steps and you fail.

## Demonstration Strategy for Your Counselor

Your counselor will want to see three *different* fires, built with three *different* methods. Here's the smart way to approach it:

1. **Fire #1 (Ferro rod):** Fastest, most reliable. Build this first to ensure success and build confidence.
2. **Fire #2 (Char cloth + lighter):** Middle difficulty. Builds on fire-starting principles but is forgiving.
3. **Fire #3 (Bow drill or hand drill):** Most challenging. If friction fire is too difficult, use a simpler method—hand drill, or even a different approach (lens-based fire if sunny).

Discuss with your counselor beforehand which three methods you'll demonstrate.

## Safety and Best Practices

- **Clear the fire site.** Remove dead leaves and debris in a 10-foot circle.
- **Have water nearby.** A bucket or extinguisher to fully extinguish fires.
- **Never leave a fire unattended.** Even a small ember can escape.
- **Fully extinguish before leaving.** Cool ashes with water, stir, and feel with your hand to confirm no heat.
- **Practice in good conditions first.** Learn on a dry day before attempting in rain or wind.

## Before You Move On

You'll demonstrate three fires with three different methods. Your counselor will observe the entire process—how you gather materials, prepare your fire, ignite it, and maintain it. Show confidence, attention to detail, and understanding of fire structure. Even if one fire struggles to start, explain what you're doing and why—demonstrating knowledge counts.
