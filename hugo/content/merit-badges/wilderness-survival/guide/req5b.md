Clothing is your first layer of shelter. Before you build a debris hut or light a fire, your clothing is already working — or failing — to keep your core temperature stable. Understanding the *why* behind outdoor clothing choices turns gear selection into a strategy rather than a shopping list.

{{< drg/image src="images/layering-system-diagram.avif" alt="Diagram showing the three-layer clothing system: base layer for moisture management, mid layer for insulation, and outer shell for weather protection" >}}

## Cold Weather Clothing: The Layering System

In cold conditions, the gold standard is three distinct layers, each with a specific function:

### The Base Layer (Next to Skin)

The base layer's job is moisture management. When you exert yourself, you sweat — and wet material against skin conducts heat away from your body about 25 times faster than dry material.

**Good materials:** Merino wool, polyester (Capilene, CoolMax), nylon. These wick moisture away from skin and continue insulating even when slightly damp.

**Bad materials:** Cotton. Cotton absorbs moisture and holds it against your skin. Wet cotton in cold conditions is genuinely dangerous — it earned the backcountry saying "cotton kills."

### The Mid Layer (Insulation)

The mid layer traps warm air near your body and provides the bulk of your insulation. This is your fleece jacket or puffy synthetic/down layer.

**Key consideration:** Compressibility. In a survival situation, you might need to remove or add this layer frequently as your activity level changes. A layer you can stuff into your pack is more useful than one you can't.

**Synthetic vs. down:** Down insulation is warmer by weight but useless when wet. Synthetic insulation continues insulating when damp — a critical advantage in backcountry conditions where staying completely dry is unlikely.

### The Outer Shell (Weather Protection)

The outer shell blocks wind and rain without trapping moisture inside. Modern shells are both waterproof and breathable, letting water vapor escape while blocking incoming rain and wind.

In a survival situation, even a non-breathable rain poncho is better than nothing — it creates a windproof, waterproof barrier that dramatically reduces heat loss.

{{< drg/tip >}}
The most important layer you can add in cold or wet conditions isn't a fancy jacket — it's a hat. You don't lose most of your body heat from your head (that's a myth), but your head *is* heavily vascularized and doesn't thermoregulate the way your torso does. A hat makes a measurable difference.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Hot Weather Clothing

In extreme heat, clothing has two jobs: **protect you from UV radiation** and **allow your cooling system (sweat evaporation) to work**.

**Cover up, but with the right materials.** Long sleeves and pants in hot dry conditions prevent sunburn and reduce water loss through the skin. Loose, light-colored, breathable fabrics (linen, lightweight nylon, or merino wool) allow airflow and wick moisture.

**Protect your extremities.** A wide-brimmed hat protects your face, neck, and ears — areas where sunburn causes serious fluid loss. UV-blocking sunglasses prevent snow blindness and reduce the squinting headache common in bright desert light.

**Avoid dark colors when possible.** Dark clothing absorbs radiant heat; light colors reflect it.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
In hot conditions, the temptation is to remove clothing to feel cooler. In a survival situation, resist this. Bare skin in direct desert sun loses water through evaporation faster than covered skin, and sunburn adds to your problems. Stay covered with loose, light layers.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Wet Conditions

Wet conditions combine the challenges of cold *and* wet simultaneously — and they're where clothing failure causes the most backcountry emergencies.

**Waterproofing before setting out:** Treat footwear with water repellent. Make sure your rain layer is clean and seam-sealed — dirt and oils break down the durable water repellent (DWR) coating that causes water to bead off a shell jacket.

**Managing wet clothing in the field:** If your insulating layers get wet, change into dry backups if you have them. If you don't, wring out as much water as possible from any wet layer. A damp-but-wrung fleece insulates at 30–40% of its dry capacity; a soaking-wet one insulates at almost nothing.

**Gaiters and waterproof socks:** For stream crossings or rain-soaked grass, gaiters keep your feet dry far longer than any boot can. Dry feet maintain fine motor control and prevent blisters in wet conditions.

## The "Would You Wear This Tonight?" Test

When you're packing for any outdoor trip, ask this about every item of clothing: "If I'm stranded overnight in the worst weather this region can produce this time of year, would this keep me alive?"

A cotton T-shirt on a warm day hike fails this test in most temperate environments. A lightweight merino base layer, a fleece mid layer, and a packable rain shell passes it — and together weighs just a few pounds.
