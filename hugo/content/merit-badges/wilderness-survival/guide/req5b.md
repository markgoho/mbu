{{< drg/requirement number="5b" >}}
Show that you know the proper clothing to wear while in the outdoors during extremely hot and cold weather and during wet conditions.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Your clothing is your first line of shelter — it is the layer between your body and the environment that is always with you. Wearing the wrong clothing in extreme conditions can make a comfortable outing miserable or turn a challenging one dangerous. The right clothing system adapts to changing conditions throughout the day.

## The Golden Rule: Cotton Kills

In cold or wet conditions, cotton is your enemy. Cotton absorbs water readily, takes forever to dry, and loses all insulating value when wet. A cotton T-shirt soaked with sweat or rain will pull heat from your body relentlessly.

The outdoor community has a saying: **"Cotton kills."** It is not an exaggeration. Hypothermia caused by wet cotton clothing is one of the most common preventable outdoor emergencies.

**Instead of cotton, choose:**
- **Synthetic fabrics** (polyester, nylon) — wick moisture, dry quickly, insulate when wet
- **Merino wool** — insulates even when wet, resists odor, wicks moisture naturally
- **Silk** — lightweight base layer option that wicks well

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Wet cotton loses approximately 90% of its insulating ability. Wet wool, by contrast, retains about 80% of its warmth. This is why wool has been the preferred outdoor fabric for centuries — long before synthetic alternatives existed.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Cold Weather: The Layering System

In cold conditions, the layering system gives you flexible temperature control. You add layers when you are cold and remove them when you warm up — which is especially important during physical activity, when overheating and sweating can be as dangerous as being cold.

### Base Layer (Next to Skin)

**Purpose:** Wick sweat away from your skin.

Wear a snug-fitting synthetic or merino wool layer on top and bottom. This layer should move moisture from your skin to the outer layers where it can evaporate. A wet base layer defeats the entire system.

### Mid Layer (Insulation)

**Purpose:** Trap warm air close to your body.

Fleece, down, or synthetic insulation jackets work here. You can add or remove mid layers as activity levels change. On a cold hike, you might start with just a base layer and add insulation at rest stops.

### Outer Layer (Shell)

**Purpose:** Block wind and rain.

A waterproof, breathable jacket and pants protect your insulating layers from the elements. Look for sealed seams and adjustable closures at cuffs, hem, and hood. If your shell is not breathable, moisture from sweat gets trapped inside — recreating the wet-clothing problem you are trying to avoid.

### Accessories

Do not underestimate heat loss from your head, hands, and feet. Carry:
- **Warm hat** (wool or fleece) — you lose significant heat through your head
- **Insulated gloves** (with waterproof shell for wet conditions)
- **Wool or synthetic socks** (carry extras — changing into dry socks is an instant morale boost)
- **Neck gaiter or balaclava** — protects face and neck from wind

{{< drg/image src="images/cold-weather-layering.avif" alt="Infographic showing the three-layer cold weather clothing system: base layer, mid layer, and outer shell" >}}

{{< drg/tip >}}
The key to layering is adjustment. Start cold — if you are comfortable standing still, you will overheat within minutes of hiking. Add layers at rest stops when your body cools down. Managing sweat is just as important as managing cold.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Hot Weather Clothing

Heat demands the opposite approach: your clothing should help your body cool itself through evaporation while protecting you from the sun.

### What to Wear

- **Lightweight, loose-fitting, light-colored clothing** — light colors reflect sunlight; dark colors absorb it. Loose fits allow air circulation.
- **Long sleeves and long pants** — counterintuitive, but they protect from sunburn, scratches, and insect bites while allowing airflow if the fabric is light enough.
- **Wide-brimmed hat** — shades your face, ears, and neck. A baseball cap leaves your ears and neck exposed.
- **Breathable, moisture-wicking fabrics** — synthetic athletic fabrics help sweat evaporate, which is your body's primary cooling mechanism.
- **Sunglasses** — protect against UV damage and reduce squinting fatigue.

### What NOT to Wear

- Dark-colored heavy fabrics that trap heat
- Tight-fitting clothing that prevents air circulation
- Cotton (it holds sweat against your skin, which can cause chafing and heat rash)
- Nothing at all — exposed skin sunburns, which impairs your body's ability to regulate temperature

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
In hot conditions, dehydration and heat exhaustion can develop within a few hours of exertion. Wear a hat, apply sunscreen, and drink water consistently. By the time you feel thirsty, you are already mildly dehydrated.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Wet Weather Clothing

Rain is the bridge between uncomfortable and dangerous. The goal is to keep your insulating layers dry while managing the sweat that builds up inside waterproof gear.

### The Strategy

- **Waterproof shell on the outside** — a quality rain jacket and rain pants are non-negotiable for any trip where rain is possible.
- **Moisture-wicking layers underneath** — same as cold weather; avoid cotton.
- **Gaiters** — cover the gap between your boots and pants, keeping water, mud, and debris out.
- **Waterproof boots or at least quick-drying footwear** — wet feet lead to blisters, which lead to mobility problems.

### Managing Moisture from Inside

Even the best waterproof jacket traps some moisture inside. Open armpit zippers (pit zips) and loosen the collar when working hard. Remove your rain jacket during short dry spells to ventilate. Carry your rain gear accessible — not buried at the bottom of your pack where you will not bother digging it out when the first drops fall.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Caught Without Rain Gear" >}}
You left your rain jacket at home and an unexpected storm rolls in. What now?

- **Step one:** If you have a large garbage bag, cut holes for your head and arms. It is not breathable, but it will keep rain off your core.
- **Step two:** Get under natural cover — dense conifers shed rain effectively from their lower branches.
- **Step three:** Keep moving at a moderate pace to generate body heat, but do not push so hard that you sweat heavily.
- **Step four:** When you stop, change into the driest clothing you have and wring out everything wet.
- **Step five:** Prioritize drying out near a fire at your next camp. Lesson learned — rain gear stays in the pack every trip, regardless of the forecast.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}
