{{</* drg/requirement number="5b" */>}}
Show that you know the proper clothing to wear while in the outdoors during extremely hot and cold weather and during wet conditions.
{{</* /drg/requirement */>}}

Clothing is your first line of defense against exposure. In wilderness survival, clothing *is* portable shelter. A Scout dressed wrong can suffer hypothermia, heatstroke, or frostbite. A Scout dressed right can survive conditions that would otherwise be deadly. This requirement asks you to demonstrate knowledge of appropriate clothing for three extreme environments.

## Cold Weather Clothing System

In cold weather, layers are everything. The goal is to trap warm air while allowing moisture (sweat) to escape.

{{</* drg/image src="images/clothing-layering-system.avif" alt="Cross-section showing three layers of clothing: base layer wicks moisture, insulation layer traps warm air, outer shell blocks wind and rain" */>}}

### **Layer 1: Base Layer (Wicking)**

**Material:** Wool or synthetic (polyester, nylon). **Never cotton.**

**Why:** Cotton absorbs moisture and stays cold when wet. Wool and synthetic fibers wick moisture away from your skin, keeping you dry and warm.

**Fit:** Tight to your skin so moisture transfers efficiently to outer layers.

**Examples:**
- Wool long underwear
- Synthetic thermal shirt
- Merino wool base layer

### **Layer 2: Insulation Layer (Warmth)**

**Material:** Fleece, down, or synthetic insulation (PrimaLoft, etc.)

**Why:** Traps warm air. Down is extremely warm but fails when wet. Synthetic insulation is less compressible but works even if damp.

**Fit:** Loosely fitting, allowing air circulation and layering underneath.

**Examples:**
- Fleece jacket
- Down vest
- Synthetic puffy jacket

### **Layer 3: Outer Shell (Wind/Water Protection)**

**Material:** Waterproof/windproof fabric (Gore-Tex, or similar).

**Why:** Blocks wind (which strips away your insulating air layer) and water (which destroys insulation).

**Fit:** Should fit over insulation layers without being so loose it traps wind underneath.

**Examples:**
- Waterproof rain jacket
- Windproof shell

### **Extremity Protection**

- **Hat:** Lose 40% of your body heat through your head. A wool or fleece hat is essential in cold weather.
- **Gloves:** Wool or synthetic insulated gloves. Keep extras—wet gloves need to be replaced.
- **Socks:** Wool hiking socks (not cotton). Wet feet lose heat rapidly. Carry extra socks.
- **Face covering:** Balaclava or neck gaiter protects your face and ears from wind-chill.

### **Cold Weather System Demonstration**

Your counselor might ask: "It's 15°F and windy. What are you wearing?" Be ready to describe:
- Base layer: wool thermal underwear
- Insulation layer: fleece jacket
- Shell layer: waterproof jacket
- Hat, insulated gloves, wool socks, possibly a neck gaiter

And explain: *"The base layer wicks moisture away. The fleece traps warm air. The shell blocks wind. Together, they keep me warm even if I sweat or it gets wet."*

## Hot Weather Clothing System

In hot weather, the goal is to shed heat while protecting yourself from sun.

### **Sun Protection First**

**Light-Colored Clothing:**
- Light colors (white, khaki, light blue) reflect the sun's heat.
- Dark colors absorb heat.
- Wear light-colored long sleeves and pants if possible—they protect from sunburn and allow sweat evaporation.

**Coverage:**
- Long sleeves protect your arms from sun exposure (risk of skin cancer, sunburn).
- Long pants protect your legs.
- Sounds counterintuitive, but light-colored, loose long clothing is actually cooler than exposed skin because it reflects heat instead of absorbing it.

### **Ventilation and Moisture Management**

**Loose Fit:**
- Baggy, loose clothing allows air circulation underneath, which promotes evaporative cooling.
- Tight clothing traps heat against your body.

**Moisture-Wicking Fabric:**
- Synthetic or wool fabrics wick sweat away from your skin, allowing evaporative cooling.
- Cotton absorbs sweat and stays wet, reducing cooling efficiency.

### **Head Protection**

- **Wide-brimmed hat:** Protects your face, ears, and neck from direct sun.
- **Light color:** Reflects heat instead of absorbing it.

### **Footwear**

- **Closed-toe hiking shoes:** Protects from desert plants, rocks, and insects.
- **Light-colored:** Reflects heat from the ground.
- **Breathable:** Allows sweat evaporation from your feet.

### **Hot Weather System Demonstration**

Your counselor might ask: "It's 95°F and dry desert. What are you wearing?" Be ready to describe:
- Light-colored loose long-sleeve shirt
- Light-colored pants (not shorts—sun protection and plant protection)
- Wide-brimmed hat (light color)
- Hiking shoes (closed-toe, light color, breathable)
- Sunscreen on exposed areas

And explain: *"Light colors reflect heat instead of absorbing it. Loose fit allows air to circulate and sweat to evaporate. Long sleeves and pants protect from sun exposure and desert plants."*

## Wet Weather Clothing System

In wet weather, the challenge is staying warm while staying dry. Water (especially cold water) dramatically increases heat loss.

### **Priority: Stay Dry**

**Waterproof Outer Layer:**
- Waterproof rain jacket and pants (Gore-Tex or similar).
- Blocks rain so your insulation layers stay dry.
- Must have taped seams or sealed zipper sliders to prevent water from entering at weak points.

**Avoid Cotton:**
- If you get wet, cotton stays wet and cold.
- Use synthetic or wool for all layers.

### **Layering Under Waterproofs**

**Base layer:** Synthetic or wool moisture-wicking layer
**Insulation layer:** Fleece or synthetic insulation (NOT down, which fails when wet)
**Shell:** Waterproof jacket/pants

### **Additional Wet Weather Gear**

- **Waterproof bag for gear:** Keep sleeping bag, extra clothes, and first aid kit dry.
- **Extra socks:** Wool. If your feet get wet, change immediately.
- **Hat:** Water-resistant or waterproof. Keeps rain out of your face and off your neck.
- **Gloves:** Waterproof if possible. Wet hands lose heat rapidly.

### **Wet Weather System Demonstration**

Your counselor might ask: "It's rainy, 40°F, and you're hiking. What are you wearing, and why?" Be ready to describe:
- Base layer: synthetic thermal underwear (wicks moisture)
- Insulation layer: fleece jacket (provides warmth, synthetic material dries quickly)
- Shell: waterproof rain jacket and pants (blocks rain)
- Waterproof gloves or regular gloves (depending on activity)
- Hat (water-resistant, keeps rain off)
- Wool socks and hiking shoes

And explain: *"Synthetic base layer wicks away sweat. Fleece provides warmth even if damp. Waterproof shell keeps rain out. Together, even if I get wet, I won't become hypothermic because the layers still insulate."*

## Clothing Mistakes to Avoid

| Mistake | Why It's Bad | Correct Choice |
|---------|-------------|-----------------|
| Wearing cotton in cold or wet weather | Absorbs moisture and stays cold | Wool or synthetic |
| Tight clothing in hot weather | Traps heat and sweat | Loose-fitting |
| Dark colors in hot weather | Absorb the sun's heat | Light colors (white, khaki, tan) |
| Leaving extremities unprotected in cold | Head, hands, feet lose heat fastest | Hat, gloves, warm socks |
| Down insulation in wet weather | Down loses all insulation when wet | Synthetic insulation |
| Wearing a heavy winter coat in hot weather | Won't cool down even with layers underneath | Dress in layers you can remove |

## Practical Packing for Variable Conditions

When hiking or camping, pack for multiple scenarios:

**Base kit (all seasons):**
- Light-colored long-sleeve shirt (synthetic or wool)
- Lightweight pants (synthetic)
- Wool socks (multiple pairs)
- Hiking shoes

**Cold season addition:**
- Heavy fleece jacket
- Waterproof/windproof shell jacket
- Insulated gloves
- Wool hat
- Extra socks

**Hot season adjustment:**
- Switch heavy fleece for lightweight fleece or remove insulation layer
- Keep shell jacket (provides UV protection even if you don't expect rain)
- Wide-brimmed hat instead of wool hat

**Wet season addition:**
- Waterproof rain pants
- Waterproof gloves
- Waterproof bag for gear

{{</* drg/tip */>}}
The Scout Essentials include "proper clothing." This means *appropriate clothing for the expected weather plus one season's worth of backup*. On a day hike in summer, bring a light jacket (in case weather changes). On a winter hike, bring a spare fleece and extra socks. Plan for the weather to get worse than the forecast predicts.
{{</* /drg/tip */>}}

## The Layer System: Why It Works

Layers work because they:
1. **Allow customization:** Remove layers if you're warm, add layers if you're cold.
2. **Trap air:** Multiple layers trap dead air space between them, which insulates.
3. **Manage moisture:** Base layer wicks, outer layer protects, and you can remove layers if they get damp.
4. **Distribute weight:** Three lightweight layers are warmer than one heavy coat and more flexible.

A Scout who understands layering can adapt to almost any weather condition with gear that fits in a backpack.

## Before You Move On

Your counselor will likely show you different clothing items and ask: "Would you wear this? Why or why not?" Be ready to discuss fabric type, fit, and appropriateness for the weather. Show that you understand the *logic* behind clothing choices, not just that you memorized a list.
