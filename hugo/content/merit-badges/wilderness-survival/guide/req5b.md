Show that you know the proper clothing to wear while in the outdoors during extremely hot and cold weather and during wet conditions.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Clothing is your first line of defense against every environmental exposure scenario from Req 5a. Getting it right means you may never need to build an emergency shelter. Getting it wrong means the shelter is just the beginning of your problems.

{{< drg/image src="images/layering-system-diagram.avif" alt="Three clothing layers displayed side by side — a moisture-wicking base layer shirt, an insulating mid-layer fleece, and a waterproof shell jacket — each labeled with its function" >}}

## The Layering System

All-weather outdoor clothing is built around three layers, each with a specific job:

### Layer 1: Base Layer (Moisture Management)

The base layer sits against your skin. Its job is to move moisture away from your body — not trap it. A wet base layer is the beginning of hypothermia.

**Good base layer materials:**
- **Merino wool:** Wicks moisture, stays warm when damp, naturally odor-resistant. The gold standard for backcountry use.
- **Synthetic (polyester, nylon):** Dries very fast, inexpensive, durable. Slightly less comfortable than wool.

**Avoid:** Cotton. Cotton absorbs and holds moisture against your skin, losing all insulation value when wet. "Cotton kills" is a serious saying, not an exaggeration.

### Layer 2: Insulating Layer (Heat Retention)

The mid layer traps warm air close to your body. In cold conditions, you may wear multiple mid layers.

**Good insulating materials:**
- **Fleece:** Lightweight, fast-drying, retains some warmth when damp. Great for active use.
- **Down insulation:** Unmatched warmth-to-weight ratio when dry. Becomes nearly useless when wet.
- **Synthetic insulation (PrimaLoft, Thinsulate):** Retains most of its insulation value when wet. Better choice for wet environments than down.

### Layer 3: Shell Layer (Protection)

The outer shell blocks wind and precipitation while allowing water vapor from sweat to escape. Without a shell, rain and wind defeat every layer beneath it.

**Types:**
- **Hard shell:** Fully waterproof and windproof. Best in heavy rain, snow, sustained wet weather.
- **Soft shell:** Stretchy, breathable, some water resistance. Best in light rain and wind with high physical activity.
- **Rain poncho:** Emergency option. Lightweight but can be cumbersome to move in.

## Cold Weather Clothing Strategy

In cold conditions, your goal is to trap warm air and eliminate moisture.

{{< drg/checklist title="Cold Weather Layering Checklist" subtitle="Adjust layers as your activity level changes" >}}
- **Head:** 20–40% of body heat is lost through an uncovered head. Wear a fleece or wool hat. In extreme cold, a balaclava covers the neck and lower face.
- **Hands:** Thin liner gloves under waterproof mittens are warmer than thick gloves alone (mittens keep fingers together, sharing warmth).
- **Feet:** Moisture-wicking liner socks + wool or synthetic hiking socks. Gaiters keep snow from entering boot tops.
- **Core layers:** Base (moisture-wicking) → insulating mid layer → shell as needed.
- **Avoid overheating:** When active, remove layers before you start sweating. Sweat in cold weather accelerates heat loss — "vent early, not late."
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

## Hot Weather Clothing Strategy

In hot and dry conditions, clothing serves a different purpose — protecting from UV, slowing sweat loss, and reflecting heat.

{{< drg/checklist title="Hot Weather Clothing Checklist" subtitle="Protect yourself from UV and heat without overheating" >}}
- **Loose-fitting, light-colored, long-sleeved shirt:** Reflects sunlight. Slows sweat evaporation to maintain cooling longer.
- **Lightweight convertible pants or long shorts:** Protects legs from UV and ground abrasion (important in desert terrain).
- **Wide-brim hat:** Shades the face, neck, and ears — areas prone to UV damage and overheating.
- **Bandana or neck gaiter:** Wet it and wear it to cool the neck (one of the body's primary heat-exchange sites).
- **Ventilated footwear:** Breathable mesh boots or trail runners allow air circulation, reducing foot temperature.
- **UV-protective sunglasses:** Polarized, wraparound style. In desert or snow, essential.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

## Wet Weather Clothing Strategy

Rain takes the cold-weather risks and makes them worse. Wet clothing loses nearly all its insulation — and wet skin loses heat 25 times faster than dry skin.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
In wet conditions at temperatures below 60°F, hypothermia risk is real. A soaked cotton shirt and jeans combination is genuinely dangerous. Modern rain gear and moisture-wicking layers are not luxury items — they are safety equipment.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

**Key principles for wet weather:**
- Put on your rain gear *before* you get wet — not after. Once your insulating layers are soaked, they take far longer to dry than it takes rain gear to prevent the problem.
- Carry a dry change of base layer in a waterproof bag inside your pack, even in light rain. If everything else fails, this is your emergency warmth reserve.
- Gaiters prevent water from running into boots from above.
- Choose synthetic or wool over cotton in any forecast that includes rain.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Merino wool has been worn by sailors and polar explorers for centuries precisely because it maintains insulation even when saturated. Unlike synthetic fibers, wool's protein structure traps air in microscopic crimps — and those crimps don't collapse when wet.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}
