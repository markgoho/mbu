{{< drg/requirement number="5b" >}}
Show that you know the proper clothing to wear while in the outdoors during extremely hot and cold weather and during wet conditions.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Your clothing is your first shelter. Long before you build a lean-to or start a fire, the layers on your body are either keeping you alive or helping the environment kill you. Cotton in cold rain absorbs water and steals your heat. Dark synthetic in desert sun traps heat against your body. Choosing the right clothing for conditions isn't about fashion — it's survival engineering.

## The Layering System for Cold Weather

Cold weather clothing works on one principle: **trap warm air, block wind and moisture.** A three-layer system does this efficiently:

### Base Layer (Moisture Management)

This layer sits against your skin and has one job: move sweat away from your body. Wet skin loses heat rapidly, so your base layer must wick moisture outward to dry.

- **Wear:** Synthetic (polyester, nylon) or merino wool
- **Avoid:** Cotton — it absorbs moisture and holds it against your skin. In cold weather, wet cotton can kill you. Outdoor instructors call it "death cloth."
- **Fit:** Snug but not tight. Compression restricts blood flow, which reduces warmth.

### Mid Layer (Insulation)

This layer traps warm air around your body. The thicker the layer, the more air it traps, the warmer you stay.

- **Wear:** Fleece, down, or synthetic fill jacket
- **Down:** Lightest, most compressible, warmest for its weight — but useless when wet
- **Synthetic fill:** Heavier, but retains some insulating ability even when wet
- **Fleece:** Durable, breathable, dries fast, affordable

### Outer Layer (Wind and Rain Protection)

This layer blocks wind and repels water. Without it, wind blows through your insulation and rain soaks it.

- **Wear:** Waterproof/breathable shell jacket and pants (Gore-Tex or similar)
- **Look for:** Sealed seams, adjustable hood, pit zips for ventilation
- **Avoid:** Cheap ponchos that trap moisture inside — they make you wet from your own sweat

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
"There's no such thing as bad weather, only bad clothing" is a Scandinavian saying — and it's mostly true. Norwegian postal carriers, who work outdoors year-round in Arctic conditions, rarely get cold-related injuries because their layering systems are precise and practiced.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

### Extremities

Your body prioritizes keeping your core warm. When cold, it reduces blood flow to your hands, feet, and head — which is why these areas get cold first.

- **Head:** A warm hat prevents up to 10% of total body heat loss. Bring a balaclava for extreme cold.
- **Hands:** Insulated gloves plus waterproof shell mittens. Mittens are warmer than gloves because fingers share heat.
- **Feet:** Moisture-wicking liner socks under insulating wool socks. Boots should be waterproof with room for thick socks — tight boots restrict circulation and make feet colder.

## Hot Weather Clothing

Hot weather clothing works on the opposite principle: **let heat escape and protect from sun.**

- **Wear:** Lightweight, loose-fitting, light-colored clothing made from breathable fabrics
- **Long sleeves and pants:** Counterintuitive, but covering your skin in dry heat actually keeps you cooler. Fabric blocks direct UV radiation and slows moisture loss from your skin. Desert peoples worldwide have worn loose, flowing, full-coverage clothing for millennia.
- **Wide-brimmed hat:** Shades your face, neck, and ears. A hat can lower your perceived temperature by 10–15°F.
- **Ventilation:** Look for shirts with mesh-backed vents. Roll sleeves when in shade, cover up in direct sun.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Light colors reflect sunlight; dark colors absorb it. A black shirt in direct sun can be 20°F hotter on the surface than a white shirt. Choose khaki, tan, or white for hot-weather outings.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Footwear in Heat

- Lightweight hiking boots or trail shoes with good ventilation
- Moisture-wicking socks to prevent blisters (feet sweat more in heat)
- Gaiters in sandy terrain to keep debris out of boots

### Sun Protection

- Sunglasses with UV protection (sunburn on your corneas — called snow blindness or photokeratitis — is incredibly painful and can cause temporary blindness)
- Sunscreen (SPF 30+) on exposed skin, reapplied every two hours
- Neck gaiter or bandana that can be soaked with water for evaporative cooling

## Wet Weather Clothing

Rain, mist, stream crossings, and morning dew all get you wet. Wet clothing in cool or cold temperatures is the fastest path to hypothermia.

### The Rules

1. **Waterproof outer layer is mandatory.** A quality rain jacket and rain pants are non-negotiable for any trip where rain is possible.

2. **No cotton underneath.** Wet cotton takes forever to dry and pulls heat from your body the entire time. Synthetics and wool dry faster and retain insulating ability when damp.

3. **Bring extra dry layers.** Store them in a waterproof stuff sack inside your pack. If you get soaked, you have dry clothing to change into.

4. **Manage sweat.** In rain gear, your own sweat can soak you from the inside. Ventilate by opening pit zips, loosening cuffs, and removing your rain jacket during uphill sections when you're working hard.

5. **Protect your feet.** Waterproof boots keep your feet dry during light rain. For stream crossings, consider bringing lightweight water shoes and changing back to dry boots on the other side.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Always pack rain gear, even if the forecast says clear skies. Mountain weather changes in minutes. An unexpected afternoon thunderstorm without rain gear can turn a pleasant hike into a hypothermia emergency.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## The Bottom Line

| Condition | Principle | Key Fabrics | Avoid |
|-----------|-----------|-------------|-------|
| **Cold** | Trap heat, stay dry | Wool, synthetic, down | Cotton |
| **Hot** | Release heat, block sun | Lightweight synthetic, linen | Dark colors, heavy fabrics |
| **Wet** | Block water, wick moisture | Waterproof shell, synthetic base | Cotton, non-waterproof layers |

Your clothing choices can buy you hours — or cost you your life. The right system for the conditions is the most accessible form of shelter you have. Pair smart clothing choices with the shelter-building skills coming in [Req 5d](/merit-badges/wilderness-survival/guide/req5d/), and you'll handle just about anything the backcountry throws at you.
