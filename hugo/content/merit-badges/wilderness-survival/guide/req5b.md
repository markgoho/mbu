{{< drg/requirement number="5b" >}}
Show that you know the proper clothing to wear while in the outdoors during extremely hot and cold weather and during wet conditions.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Clothing is your **first defense against exposure.** The right outfit can mean the difference between a comfortable hike and hypothermia. Yet many Scouts wear whatever they grabbed from the closet, rather than planning based on the conditions they'll encounter.

The key concept: **layer, and dress for the conditions you might face, not just the conditions that exist right now.** The weather will change. Your activity level will change. You need flexibility.

## The Layer System

Most outdoor experts teach the three-layer system: base layer, insulation layer, and outer shell.

**Base Layer (Moisture Management)**
- Purpose: moves sweat away from your skin
- Material: synthetic (polyester, nylon) or merino wool
- NOT cotton (cotton absorbs sweat, stays wet, causes heat loss)
- Fit: snug but not restrictive
- Examples: thermal underwear, rashguard

**Insulation Layer (Warmth)**
- Purpose: traps warm air close to your body
- Material: fleece, down, synthetic insulation, or wool
- Fit: loose enough to layer underneath and over it
- Examples: fleece jacket, wool sweater, down parka

**Outer Shell (Wind & Rain Protection)**
- Purpose: blocks wind and water while allowing moisture to escape
- Material: Gore-Tex, ripstop nylon, or similar breathable waterproof fabric
- Fit: roomy enough to fit over everything else
- Examples: rain jacket, windbreaker, puffy jacket

## Dressing for Cold Weather

**The outfit:**
1. Base layer: thermal underwear (synthetic or merino wool)
2. Mid layer: fleece jacket
3. Outer shell: waterproof, windproof jacket and pants
4. Hat (wool or fleece): most heat loss happens through your head
5. Gloves or mittens: protects fingers and hands
6. Insulated socks: not cotton
7. Insulated boots: keep feet warm and dry
8. Consider: neck gaiter or balaclava for extreme cold

**Why this works:**
- Each layer traps warm air
- Moisture (sweat) moves outward through the layers instead of staying against your skin
- You can remove a layer if you get too warm, preventing sweat accumulation

**Key principle:** **Avoid cotton.** Cotton holds moisture and loses all insulating value when wet. Use synthetics or wool instead.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Bring a beanie or winter hat even on mild-weather hikes. A wool or fleece hat weighs almost nothing but can prevent hypothermia if you need to spend an unplanned night outside.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Dressing for Hot Weather

**The outfit:**
1. Lightweight, moisture-wicking base layer: keeps sweat from pooling on your skin
2. Light-colored clothing: reflects heat instead of absorbing it
3. Hat with a brim: protects your face and neck from sun
4. Long sleeves and pants: protect your skin from sun and bug bites (counterintuitive, but sun damage and dehydration are bigger killers than heat)
5. Sunglasses: reduces eye strain and squinting (which causes heat exhaustion)
6. Lightweight socks: synthetics that wick moisture

**Why this works:**
- Light colors don't trap heat
- Moisture-wicking moves sweat away so it can evaporate (cooling you)
- Loose fit allows air circulation around your body
- Covering your skin reduces sun exposure

**Key principle:** **Light-colored, light-weight, and loose-fitting.** Think "desert robes," not "t-shirt and shorts."

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Desert guides wear long sleeves and pants in the hottest conditions. It looks uncomfortable, but it reduces water loss and sun damage. Exposed skin not only burns but also loses water through increased perspiration.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Dressing for Wet Conditions

**The outfit:**
1. Base layer: synthetic that wicks moisture (not cotton)
2. Insulation layer: wool or synthetic fleece (NOT down; down loses all value when wet)
3. Outer shell: waterproof and breathable jacket and pants (Gore-Tex preferred)
4. Hat and gloves: synthetic or wool, not cotton
5. Waterproof boots with good traction (wet rocks are slippery)
6. Waterproof bag for your essentials

**Why this works:**
- Wet + cold = hypothermia. Insulation is critical.
- Synthetic or wool insulation retains warmth even when wet
- Waterproof shell keeps the insulation dry
- Down becomes useless when wet, so avoid it in rainy conditions

**Key principle:** **Assume you'll get wet.** Every piece of clothing should remain warm if soaked.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Hypothermia can occur in wet conditions at temperatures that feel "not that cold" (even 50°F). Dress with the assumption that you'll be soaked before the day ends.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## A Sample Packing List by Season

**Spring/Fall (Variable Conditions)**
- Moisture-wicking base layer
- Fleece or wool mid layer
- Waterproof jacket and pants
- Hat (can be removed if warm)
- Gloves (can be removed)
- Good hiking boots with ankle support

**Summer (Hot & Sunny)**
- Lightweight moisture-wicking shirt (long sleeves recommended)
- Lightweight pants or capris
- Wide-brimmed hat
- Sunglasses
- Sunscreen (SPF 30+)
- Lightweight socks and trail shoes

**Winter (Cold & Potentially Snowy)**
- Thermal underwear (synthetic or merino)
- Fleece or wool sweater
- Down or synthetic parka
- Waterproof shell pants
- Winter boots (insulated)
- Winter hat
- Insulated gloves or mittens
- Wool scarf or neck gaiter

## Practical Considerations

**Weight matters:** Don't over-pack layers. A lightweight fleece and rain jacket take up minimal pack space but add significantly to warmth.

**Check the forecast, but over-prepare:** Forecasts change. Weather is unpredictable. Bring one more layer than you think you need.

**Break in your boots before a trip:** Blisters are serious and avoidable. Wear new boots around home first.

**Wear your gear to know it:** Practice hikes in your planned outfit. This reveals problems (like "this jacket makes my arms too hot") before a real emergency.

**Socks matter as much as boots:** Good insulating socks prevent blisters and keep feet warm. Avoid cotton; use synthetic blends or wool.
