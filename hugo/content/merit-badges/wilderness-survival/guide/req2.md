Explain the importance of each of the seven priorities of survival in a wilderness location.

Survival is not random — it follows an order. When you're lost or in trouble, decisions that feel urgent in the moment may not be what actually keeps you alive. The seven priorities of survival give you a decision-making framework so panic doesn't drive you to do the wrong things first.

{{< drg/image src="images/seven-priorities-infographic.avif" alt="Numbered vertical list showing the Seven Priorities of Survival as icons with labels" >}}

## The Seven Priorities, In Order

### 1. S.T.O.P. — Stop, Think, Observe, Plan

Before anything else — **stop moving**. Panic drives people to wander, which makes them harder to find and more likely to be injured. S.T.O.P. is your mental reset.

- **S — Stop:** Sit down. Don't take another step. Catch your breath.
- **T — Think:** What happened? How did you get here? What do you have with you?
- **O — Observe:** Look around. What's your terrain? Weather coming? Water nearby? Landmarks you recognize?
- **P — Plan:** Decide your next steps *before* acting. What's your most urgent need?

**Why it's first:** Every other priority depends on your brain working correctly. A panicking person makes terrible decisions — they walk away from a trail they almost found, they use matches to light fires they haven't prepared for, they exhaust themselves moving instead of conserving energy.

### 2. First Aid

Treat life-threatening injuries immediately. If you or a companion is unconscious, bleeding severely, in shock, or having trouble breathing, first aid takes priority over all other survival tasks.

**Why it's second:** You can't build shelter or signal for help if you're incapacitated. The priority order assumes you've already cleared your head (S.T.O.P.) — now check the physical state of everyone in the group.

### 3. Seek Shelter

Once immediate medical needs are handled, protect yourself from the environment. Exposure — heat, cold, wind, rain — is responsible for the majority of wilderness fatalities.

**Why it's third:** A person can survive three hours in a harsh environment without shelter. You can go three days without water and three weeks without food. Shelter buys you the time you need to work the other priorities.

Good shelter does three things: blocks wind, keeps you dry, and provides insulation. In cold environments, that might mean a leaf debris hut, a snow trench, or a space blanket bivouac. In hot environments, it means shade and ventilation.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Wind at just 20 mph in 30°F temperatures creates wind chill equivalent to 17°F — cold enough to cause frostbite on exposed skin within 30 minutes. Shelter from wind is often more urgent than shelter from cold alone.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

### 4. Build a Fire

Fire does more than warm you. In a survival situation, fire provides: heat against hypothermia, a means to signal rescuers (smoke by day, flames by night), the ability to purify water by boiling, and a psychological anchor that dramatically improves morale.

**Why it's fourth:** Fire is important but not as immediately urgent as shelter. In many environments, you can reach the fourth priority quickly — but don't skip to it before confirming your shelter situation, especially in wet or cold conditions where hypothermia risk is high.

In Req 6, you'll practice building fires without matches using three different methods. Understanding *why* fire is a survival priority helps you take that practice seriously.

### 5. Find and Treat Water

A person can survive three days without water under mild conditions — but that window shrinks dramatically in heat, with exertion, or at altitude. By the time you're severely dehydrated, your decision-making is already impaired.

**Why it's fifth:** Water is more urgent than food — but less urgent than shelter in most cold-weather scenarios (since cold kills faster than dehydration). Once you have shelter and fire, finding and treating water becomes the priority.

Water in the backcountry always needs treatment before drinking. Giardia, Cryptosporidium, and bacteria can cause severe GI illness that rapidly worsens your situation. Req 8 covers three specific treatment methods.

### 6. Find Food

Food is the *least* urgent of the immediate survival priorities. Most healthy adults can survive two to three weeks without eating. In a short-term survival situation (hours to a few days), food is almost never what determines the outcome.

**Why it's sixth:** Spending energy searching for food in a survival situation often burns more calories than the food provides. More importantly, foraging for wild plants or hunting requires knowledge you may not have in an unfamiliar ecosystem — and eating the wrong thing can rapidly worsen your condition.

Req 9 explains why wild plants and wildlife are usually poor choices for survival food, especially in a short-duration emergency.

### 7. Signals

Signaling for rescue runs throughout a survival situation — it's not just the last step. But thinking strategically about signaling — setting up ground-to-air signals, maintaining a signal fire, keeping your mirror accessible — becomes more urgent once your immediate survival needs are met.

**Why it's seventh (last):** Not because it's least important, but because the other priorities need to be in place first. A well-sheltered, hydrated person near a signal fire is in a much better position than a dehydrated, hypothermic person frantically waving a mirror. The order builds survival capacity — then broadcasts it.

In Req 7, you'll learn specific signaling methods for attracting ground, air, and water rescue teams.

## Why the Order Matters

The priorities are sequential because each one creates the conditions for the next. A panicking, injured person (priorities 1 and 2 not met) cannot effectively build shelter. An exposed, hypothermic person (priority 3 not met) cannot rationally find and treat water. The order isn't arbitrary — it's built on the actual physiological timeline of wilderness threats.

{{< drg/checklist title="Remember the Priority Order" subtitle="When everything feels urgent, this framework decides what's next" >}}
- **1. S.T.O.P.** — Stop, Think, Observe, Plan. Reset your brain.
- **2. First Aid** — Treat life-threatening injuries immediately.
- **3. Shelter** — Protect from exposure. You have three hours.
- **4. Fire** — Warmth, signaling, water purification, morale.
- **5. Water** — You have three days. Treat before drinking.
- **6. Food** — You have three weeks. Don't risk eating the wrong thing.
- **7. Signals** — Maximize your chance of being found.
