Explain the importance of each of the seven priorities of survival in a wilderness location.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

When you're lost or stranded, your brain is flooded with fear. You need a **decision-making framework** that strips away panic and tells you what matters most. That framework is the Seven Priorities of Survival.

These aren't "tips." They're **listed in order** because the first one is truly the most important, the second matters next, and so on. If you memorize nothing else from this badge, memorize these seven in order.

## Priority 1: Survival Attitude & Will to Live

You can survive for weeks without food, days without water, and hours without shelter—but you can die from despair in minutes. Your **mindset** is your first and most critical priority.

People with a strong will to live do things like:
- Stay calm and think rationally
- Take action instead of sitting paralyzed
- Adapt to circumstances
- Maintain hope

People without it give up and make fatal errors (walking randomly in circles, drinking saltwater, refusing to drink at all).

**In practice:** The moment you realize you're lost, your first job is emotional: *Acknowledge the situation, remind yourself that people will search for you, and commit to surviving.* Your next job is thinking clearly.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="You Realize You're Lost" >}}
**Stay calm.** Panic clouds judgment and burns energy.

- **Step 1:** Stop moving. Take three deep breaths.
- **Step 2:** Accept the situation. You are lost. That's okay. You have skills.
- **Step 3:** Use your map and compass to figure out where you are if possible.
- **Step 4:** If you can't orient yourself, stay put. Your trip plan means someone will search for you.
- **Step 5:** Use the next six priorities to guide your actions.

## Priority 2: Shelter (Protection from Elements)

Exposure kills faster than hunger or thirst. In extreme cold, you can die of hypothermia in hours. In extreme heat, heat exhaustion and heatstroke follow.

**Why it matters:** Your body is constantly losing heat (or trying to shed heat). In cold weather, an insulating layer and protection from wind and rain are the difference between a bad night and a life-threatening one.

**In practice:** Build or find shelter before nightfall. Even a simple lean-to using branches and evergreen boughs provides insulation and wind protection. In a true emergency, the psychological boost of shelter ("I have a safe place to sleep") is as important as the physical protection.

## Priority 3: Signaling for Rescue

Don't plan to survive for weeks. Plan to be found. **Rescue is coming.** Your job is to make yourself findable.

**Why it matters:** Three things can find you:
- **Ground search teams** who follow your trip plan
- **Helicopter or small aircraft** spotting you from above
- **People you contact** via signal or communication device

All of these depend on knowing approximately where to look, which is why a trip plan is critical (Priority 1 in Requirement 1c).

**In practice:**
- Stay where you are (don't wander off)
- Create visible signals: bright clothing, reflective material, signal mirror
- Use audible signals: whistle, voice
- Set up in an open area if possible (avoid dense forest where helicopters can't see you)

## Priority 4: Water

You can survive only 3–4 days without water (less in hot, dry climates). Dehydration clouds judgment and weakens you.

**Why it matters:** Water is essential for core body function—cooling, digestion, brain function. Even mild dehydration reduces mental clarity by 10–15%.

**In practice:**
- Find water if possible (streams, collected rainwater, dew on leaves)
- Boil, filter, or treat it before drinking
- Drink regularly, even if you're not thirsty
- In cold weather, you're less aware of dehydration—force yourself to drink

## Priority 5: Fire

Fire keeps you warm, signals for rescue, boosts morale, and purifies water.

**Why it matters:** Fire is as much psychological as physical. The moment you get a fire going, your despair drops and your energy rises. You have light, warmth, and a way to signal.

**In practice:**
- Find dry tinder, kindling, and fuel wood before dark
- Start a fire if you can do so safely
- Keep it small and controlled to conserve fuel
- Use it for warmth, signaling, and psychological comfort

## Priority 6: Food

You can survive 3–4 weeks without food. It's the lowest priority because it's the slowest killer.

**Why it matters:** Food maintains energy and morale. Once you've addressed shelter, signaling, water, and fire, thinking about food prevents despair and gives you a task.

**In practice:**
- You likely have emergency rations in your survival kit
- In an extended situation, you might look for safe wild foods (nuts, berries, insects)
- **Do NOT eat unfamiliar plants or wildlife** (more on this in Req 9)
- Rationing matters: eat small amounts frequently rather than a big meal once daily

## Priority 7: First Aid

Treat injuries promptly to prevent infection and worse conditions.

**Why it matters:** A small cut that gets infected can become life-threatening. A sprain that you keep using can become permanent damage.

**In practice:**
- Keep your first-aid kit accessible
- Treat wounds immediately: clean, dry, bandage
- Immobilize sprains
- Monitor for signs of infection (increasing redness, warmth, pus)

## Remembering the Order

A helpful mnemonic is **"Survival Attitude, Shelter, Signaling, Water, Fire, Food, First Aid"** — or the acronym **SASWFFF** (though that's not as catchy as some mnemonics). The key is understanding *why* this order: **the things that kill you fastest come first**.

Cold exposure kills in hours, so shelter is #2. Rescue is your goal, so signaling comes early. Water kills in days, fire in even longer, food in weeks. First aid prevents slow decline.

## How the Priorities Guide Real Decisions

Imagine you're stranded overnight in the mountains.

**Scenario:** It's 4:00 p.m., you're in a dense forest, and you've realized you won't make it back to your car before dark.

Your decision-making:
1. **Attitude:** You accept the situation. You're lost, but you have skills. You'll spend the night and be rescued tomorrow.
2. **Shelter:** You find a relatively dry spot under a large tree or in a small depression. You gather branches and evergreen boughs to insulate yourself and block wind. Task: make shelter before dark.
3. **Signaling:** You look for an open area near your shelter where a helicopter could spot you. You place rocks in a large X pattern or arrange bright clothing visibly. Task: be findable.
4. **Water:** There's a stream 50 yards away. You'll boil water once you have a fire.
5. **Fire:** You spend an hour gathering tinder, kindling, and larger wood. Once dark, you light it. It keeps you warm and visible to searchers.
6. **Food:** You have energy bars in your pack. You eat one now and one in the morning to maintain morale and energy.
7. **First Aid:** You notice a small cut on your hand. You clean it and apply antibiotic ointment.

By 9:00 p.m., you're in your shelter, warm from a fire, and mentally strong because you've done everything right. You sleep. The next morning, a helicopter spots your signal and you're rescued.

**That's the Seven Priorities at work.**
