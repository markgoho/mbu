If you had one hour alone in the wilderness with no gear, what would kill you first? The answer changes based on the environment, but the survival priorities—ranked by how quickly they become life-threatening—guide your decision-making in any survival situation. These seven priorities are not academic; they determine what you do *first* when things go wrong.

{{</* drg/image src="images/survival-priorities-pyramid.avif" alt="Pyramid showing seven survival priorities stacked from bottom to top: Shelter, Signal, First Aid, Water, Fire, Food, Morale" */>}}

## The Seven Survival Priorities (In Order)

### **1. Shelter and Protection from the Elements**

**Why it's first:** Exposure to extreme temperature is your most immediate killer in most wilderness environments. Hypothermia can set in within hours. Heatstroke within 6–12 hours. Wind and wet conditions accelerate both dramatically.

**In practice:**
- In cold weather: Stop and build shelter immediately, even if you're only 2 miles from help. Keeping body heat is more important than hiking out.
- In hot weather: Find shade and rest during peak heat (11am–3pm). Conserve water and energy.
- In wet weather: Stop, protect yourself from rain, and prevent hypothermia.

**Key principle:** Shelter/protection is about your *immediate survival*. Everything else depends on you staying alive long enough to act on it.

### **2. Signal for Rescue**

**Why it's second:** Once you're protected from the elements, someone needs to know you need help. A signal can bring rescers to you. Without a signal—or if no one knows you're lost—you might survive for weeks but never get rescued.

**In practice:**
- Use a whistle (three blasts = distress signal). It carries further than your voice and works when you're exhausted.
- Create ground-to-air signals (bright cloth, reflective materials, debris arranged in large X or SOS on the ground).
- If you have a mirror, signal passing aircraft.
- Maintain a fire for smoke signals (which are visible for miles).
- Stay *put* in one location instead of wandering. Rescuers can't find you if you're moving.

**Key principle:** A distressed Scout in a shelter making signals will be found. A Scout wandering lost even with decent survival skills won't be.

### **3. First Aid and Injury Prevention**

**Why it's third:** After shelter and signaling, your next priority is preventing injuries from getting worse. A minor cut becomes infected. An ankle sprain becomes a mobility disaster if untreated.

**In practice:**
- Treat cuts with soap and water, then antibiotic ointment and a bandage.
- Immobilize sprained ankles and knees so they don't get worse.
- Treat blisters before they disable you.
- Prevent dehydration and heatstroke by drinking water and resting.
- Manage any existing medical conditions (wounds, illnesses, medications).

**Key principle:** In a survival situation, an injury is amplified. A Scout with an untreated blister can't hike out. A Scout with an infection gets weaker daily.

### **4. Water**

**Why it's fourth:** A human can survive 3 weeks without food, but only 3–5 days without water. In most environments, water is available (rain, snow, streams, lakes)—but it needs to be found and purified.

**In practice:**
- Find water sources (listen for running water, look for valleys, animal trails often lead to water).
- Treat water to make it safe: boil for 1 minute, use purification tablets, filter if you have one.
- Ration water only in desert environments with no water sources.
- In cold weather, melt snow with a fire before drinking (eating snow lowers body temperature).

**Key principle:** Thirst is a symptom of dehydration that appears *after* the damage has started. Drink regularly, even if you don't feel thirsty.

### **5. Fire**

**Why it's fifth:** Fire provides warmth (backup shelter), allows water purification, provides psychological comfort, and signals (smoke/flames visible for miles). But it's not *more* urgent than shelter/signal/first aid/water because you can survive a night without fire if you have shelter.

**In practice:**
- Have a reliable fire starter (matches, lighter) in your kit.
- Know how to build fire in wet conditions (feather dry wood from the inside, use tinder that burns even when damp).
- Fire size depends on goal: large fire for warmth and signaling, small fire for cooking/boiling water.

**Key principle:** Fire is a multiplier. It makes water safer, makes shelter warmer, and makes rescue faster. But only if you can make it—hence it's lower priority than the items you can do without fire (shelter, signaling, first aid).

### **6. Food**

**Why it's sixth:** Unlike water or shelter, food is not immediately life-threatening. A Scout can survive weeks without food. But starvation causes weakness, poor judgment, and collapse—eventually fatal.

**In practice:**
- Carry high-calorie emergency food in your survival kit (energy bars, nuts, jerky).
- If stranded long-term, focus on water and shelter over food foraging.
- Never eat wild plants unless you're 100% sure they're safe—many wilderness plants are poisonous.
- Conserve energy instead of expending energy hunting for food (exception: if rescue is days away and you know the food sources are safe).

**Key principle:** Hunger is uncomfortable, but starvation is slower than dehydration or exposure. If you're forced to choose, water and shelter come first.

### **7. Morale and Mental Health**

**Why it's last (but crucial):** Panic and despair kill. They make people make poor decisions (wander instead of stay put), take unnecessary risks, or give up when help is coming. Maintaining morale—mental resilience, hope, purpose—is the difference between survival and surrender.

**In practice:**
- Stay busy with purposeful tasks (improving shelter, gathering firewood, maintaining signals).
- Keep a positive mindset. Remind yourself: "Rescuers know to search my route. I have shelter and water. I just need to wait."
- Think of morale as a tool, not a luxury. Clear thinking *is* a survival skill.
- Maintain routines (meals at regular times, sleep schedule) even in survival situations.

**Key principle:** The strongest, most equipped Scout gives up under despair. The weaker Scout with hope survives. Mental toughness is not a personality trait—it's a skill you build by staying mentally engaged.

## How Priorities Change by Situation

The order of the seven priorities is general, but they shift based on your specific situation:

**In a blizzard:** Shelter jumps to absolute priority #1. Everything else is secondary to not freezing.

**In the desert:** Water becomes urgently important (maybe #2). You can't survive dehydration for long.

**With a serious injury:** First aid might jump to #2. An untreated life-threatening wound (severe bleeding, open fracture) needs immediate attention before signaling.

**If rescue is known to be coming soon:** Shelter and signaling are enough. Food is almost irrelevant if rescuers arrive in 6 hours.

The seven priorities are your *framework*, not a rigid checklist. You apply them to your specific situation.

## Why These Priorities Matter for Counselor Discussions

When your merit badge counselor asks you to explain the seven priorities, they're really asking: *Do you understand what actually matters in a survival situation?* Many inexperienced people focus on food foraging or building elaborate shelters—missing the point that shelter/signal/water are more critical. Understanding the priorities shows that you've thought deeply about what survival actually requires.

Be ready to discuss scenarios: "You're lost overnight in winter. Your shelter is done. What's your next priority?" (Signal for rescue.) "You've signaled. Now what?" (Water and fire, if you can make them.) The priorities help you make good decisions when conditions are bad.

{{</* drg/tip */>}}
Test yourself: Read a wilderness survival story (books, magazines, or documentaries). When you see a stranded person, ask yourself: Are they following the seven priorities in the right order? Often, the people who don't survive are those who focused on the wrong priority at the wrong time.
{{</* /drg/tip */>}}

## Before You Move On

By the end of this requirement, you should be able to explain why each of the seven priorities matters and give examples of how to handle each one. You don't need to memorize the list in order—but you should understand the *logic*: why does shelter come first? Why is morale important even though it's last?
