{{< drg/requirement number="2" >}}
Priorities for Survival. Explain the importance of each of the seven priorities of survival in a wilderness location.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

When a survival situation hits, your brain will want to do everything at once — build a shelter, find water, start a fire, signal for help. But trying to do everything simultaneously means doing nothing well. The seven priorities of survival give you a clear order of operations: handle the most urgent threat first, then move to the next.

These priorities are not arbitrary. They are ranked by how quickly each threat can kill you.

## 1. STOP — Stop, Think, Observe, Plan

Before you do anything physical, stop moving. Sit down. Breathe. The most dangerous minutes in a survival situation are the first ones, because panic drives people to make catastrophic mistakes: running blindly, leaving gear behind, wandering farther from the trail.

**S.T.O.P.** is an acronym that guides your first response:

- **Stop** — Physically stop moving. Sit down if possible.
- **Think** — What happened? What are my immediate threats? What resources do I have?
- **Observe** — Where am I? What time is it? What is the weather doing? What terrain surrounds me?
- **Plan** — Based on what I know, what should I do first? What is my best course of action?

This is the single most important survival priority because it prevents everything that follows from going wrong. You will learn more about managing panic in [Req 3](/merit-badges/wilderness-survival/guide/req3/).

## 2. First Aid

Address injuries immediately. An untreated wound can become infected within hours. Bleeding must be stopped. Sprains must be stabilized. Hypothermia and heatstroke must be recognized and treated before they become life-threatening. Everything you learned in [Req 1a](/merit-badges/wilderness-survival/guide/req1a/) applies here.

First aid comes second because injured people cannot effectively build shelter, gather water, or signal for help. Stabilize the medical situation before moving on to environmental threats.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Do a head-to-toe assessment of yourself and everyone in your group, even if no one thinks they are hurt. Adrenaline masks pain. Injuries discovered early are far easier to treat than injuries found hours later.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## 3. Shelter

In extreme conditions, exposure can kill in as little as three hours. Shelter protects you from wind, rain, snow, and sun — the environmental forces that cause hypothermia and heatstroke. In most survival situations, building or finding shelter should be your first major physical task after treating injuries.

Shelter does not have to be elaborate. A debris hut that blocks wind and traps body heat, a tarp strung between trees, or even crawling under a dense evergreen can be enough to survive the night. You will learn shelter-building techniques in detail in [Req 5d](/merit-badges/wilderness-survival/guide/req5d/) and spend a night in one you build in [Req 5e](/merit-badges/wilderness-survival/guide/req5e/).

## 4. Fire

Fire is the great multiplier. It provides warmth (extending your shelter's effectiveness), light (extending your working hours), a way to purify water (boiling), a means to signal rescuers (smoke by day, flames by night), and a massive morale boost.

Fire ranks below shelter because a good shelter alone can keep you alive through a cold night, but a fire without shelter will not help much in wind and rain. However, once you have basic shelter, fire should be your next priority. You will master three matchless fire-starting methods in [Req 6](/merit-badges/wilderness-survival/guide/req6/).

## 5. Water

The human body can survive roughly three days without water, but your physical and mental abilities start declining long before that. After about 24 hours of dehydration, you will have trouble thinking clearly, making good decisions, and performing physical tasks — all things you desperately need in a survival situation.

Finding and treating water is covered in [Req 8](/merit-badges/wilderness-survival/guide/req8/). In most environments, water sources exist — streams, rain, snow, dew — but they must be purified before drinking to avoid waterborne illness, which would make your situation dramatically worse.

## 6. Signaling

Getting rescued is almost always better than trying to walk out. Rescuers have helicopters, trained dogs, thermal imaging, and teams of experienced trackers. Your job is to make yourself easy to find.

Signaling ranks sixth because it requires you to be stable (not in medical distress), sheltered, and in a condition to sustain your signal over time. A signal fire needs to be maintained. Ground-to-air signals need to be large and visible. You will learn these techniques in [Req 7](/merit-badges/wilderness-survival/guide/req7/).

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The universal distress signal is any signal repeated three times: three whistle blasts, three fires in a triangle, three gunshots. Rescuers are trained to recognize this pattern. Two signals mean "I am okay," but three means "I need help."
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## 7. Food

Food is last on the list because the human body can survive three weeks or more without it. While hunger is uncomfortable and saps morale, it is not an immediate threat to life the way exposure, dehydration, or untreated injuries are.

In most survival situations, you will be rescued or walk out long before food becomes a critical concern. The energy you would spend hunting, trapping, or foraging is usually better spent on maintaining shelter, fire, water, and signals. You will explore why eating wild plants is risky in [Req 9](/merit-badges/wilderness-survival/guide/req9/).

## Putting the Priorities in Practice

The seven priorities are a framework, not a rigid checklist. In practice, you will often work on multiple priorities at once — building a fire while you have a pot of water collecting rain, for example. The key is knowing which threat is most urgent right now and addressing it first.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Lost After a Storm" >}}
Your patrol was day-hiking when a sudden thunderstorm rolled in. Heavy rain made the trail impossible to follow, and now you are in an unfamiliar part of the forest. It is 4 p.m. in October, temperature is dropping, and everyone is soaked.

Using the seven priorities:

- **STOP:** Gather the group. Sit down. Take inventory of your gear and assess your situation.
- **First Aid:** Check everyone for injuries. One Scout has a small cut from a branch — clean and bandage it.
- **Shelter:** You have about two hours of daylight. Find a spot sheltered from wind and build a debris lean-to using fallen branches and leaf litter.
- **Fire:** Gather dry tinder from under dense trees or inside dead standing wood. Start a fire to warm up and dry clothing.
- **Water:** Everyone is low on water. Set out containers to catch ongoing rain.
- **Signaling:** Your trip plan says you will be back by 5 p.m. When you do not return, your Scoutmaster will alert searchers. Build your fire large enough to be visible and have your whistle ready.
- **Food:** You have trail mix and energy bars. Ration them but eat enough to maintain energy.
