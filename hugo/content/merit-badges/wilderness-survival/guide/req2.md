{{< drg/requirement number="2" >}}
Priorities for Survival. Explain the importance of each of the seven priorities of survival in a wilderness location.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

When everything goes wrong at once — you're lost, it's getting dark, the temperature is dropping, and your pack is lighter than it should be — how do you decide what to do first? That's exactly what the seven priorities of survival answer. They're a decision framework, ordered from most urgent to least, that prevents the paralysis and poor judgment that can turn a bad situation fatal.

Think of them as a hierarchy, not a checklist. You work from the top down, addressing each layer only after the previous one is handled.

{{< drg/image src="images/seven-priorities-diagram.avif" alt="Seven numbered icons in a vertical list representing the seven priorities of wilderness survival" >}}

## Priority 1: Positive Attitude

A positive attitude isn't about being cheerful when things are terrible. It's about controlling fear and making rational decisions when everything in you wants to panic and run.

Panic is physiologically expensive. It raises heart rate, burns calories, impairs judgment, and drives people to make decisions — wandering deeper into the woods, crossing rushing water, taking shelter in dangerous spots — that make situations worse. The psychological dimension of survival is documented in the accounts of nearly every wilderness survivor: the people who make it home tend to be the ones who stayed calm enough to think clearly.

A positive attitude also means believing rescue is possible and that your actions matter — because they do. You'll explore the specific techniques for building and maintaining this mental state in Req 3.

## Priority 2: First Aid

Before you address shelter, fire, or water, address your physical condition. An untreated bleeding wound or worsening hypothermia will compromise every subsequent decision you make.

Conduct a rapid self-assessment:
- Am I bleeding? Stop it.
- Is my body temperature stable? Address it if not.
- Can I walk? If not, that changes your options significantly.
- Are there others in my group who need attention first?

Think of first aid as your "operational status check." You can't build an effective shelter if you're losing blood or your fingers have lost feeling. The conditions covered in Req 1a — dehydration, hypothermia, shock — are exactly what you're watching for here.

## Priority 3: Shelter

Body heat loss kills faster than lack of water or food. In wind and rain at 50°F — conditions not unusual in most of North America — an unprotected, underdressed person can become hypothermic within hours. Shelter is your armor against the environment.

The hierarchy within shelter is: **protect yourself from the ground first** (ground conducts cold far faster than air), then from wind, then from rain. In a desert environment, shelter means protection from sun and heat rather than cold.

You'll explore the full range of shelter strategies — from natural debris huts to improvised tarps to snow shelters — in Req 5.

{{< drg/tip >}}
The best shelter material in the backcountry is often the gear you're already wearing. Before building anything, fully use your clothing (put on every layer, close every zipper), find a windbreak, and get off the bare ground — even sitting on your pack is significantly warmer than sitting on dirt.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Priority 4: Fire

Fire serves multiple survival functions: warmth (extends shelter effectiveness), signaling (smoke by day, light by night), water treatment (boiling), and morale. The morale function is underestimated — a fire represents warmth, safety, and the confirmation that you can still make things happen.

Fire is Priority 4, after shelter, because in extreme conditions, fire takes time to build and may require dry materials you need to find. Getting into shelter first buys you the time and stability to build fire effectively. In hot, dry environments, fire's warmth benefit disappears — but its signaling and water-treatment benefits remain.

Req 6 covers fire-building in depth.

## Priority 5: Signaling

Once you're physically stable (first aid done), protected (shelter established), and warm (fire going or unnecessary), your energy turns outward: how do you get found?

Signaling is Priority 5 because it requires both your attention and, in many cases, some energy. It also assumes that help is looking, which is more likely after you've been missing for some time. Your job during signaling is to maximize your visibility — mirror flashes, smoke, whistle blasts, ground-to-air patterns — in ways that stand out from the natural landscape. Req 7 covers the full toolkit.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Search-and-rescue statistics show that most lost hikers are found within 3 miles of where they were last seen. Staying in one place ("stay put") and signaling from there is almost always more effective than attempting to walk out.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Priority 6: Water

A healthy adult can survive about three days without water in moderate conditions. In heat, that window shrinks sharply. Dehydration impairs cognitive function well before you feel seriously thirsty, which is why staying hydrated from your water bottle (Priority 1b of the Scout Essentials) comes before a survival emergency begins.

Once in a survival situation, finding and treating water becomes a serious priority — but it comes after your immediate physical stability, shelter, and ability to signal. A search that takes four hours doesn't require you to find water. A search that takes four days does.

Req 8 covers water treatment methods in detail.

## Priority 7: Food

The lowest priority in a survival situation. Most healthy people can survive three weeks without food (the body begins metabolizing fat reserves). In a search-and-rescue timeframe — typically hours to a few days — food is rarely a survival issue by itself, though eating does support morale, body temperature regulation, and decision-making energy.

The exception: people with diabetes or other metabolic conditions where blood sugar management matters. Know your group members' medical conditions.

Food also connects to a risk: Req 9 addresses why eating wild plants or wildlife is usually *not* a good idea in a survival situation, despite what TV shows might suggest.
