Wilderness survival is the set of skills that keeps you alive — and brings you home — when things go wrong in the backcountry. A wrong turn on a trail, a sudden storm, an injury three miles from the nearest road: any of these can turn an ordinary outdoor adventure into a survival situation. This merit badge teaches you to handle exactly that.

The skills you'll build here go far beyond camping tips. You'll learn to manage your own physiology in extreme heat and cold, construct emergency shelter with what's around you, signal rescuers effectively, and treat injuries when a hospital is nowhere nearby. More importantly, you'll develop the mental framework — calm, deliberate problem-solving — that separates people who survive from people who don't.

## Then and Now

### Then: Survival as Daily Life

For most of human history, wilderness survival wasn't a merit badge topic — it was Tuesday. The Indigenous peoples of North America spent thousands of years mastering the skills of reading weather, building fire without matches, finding water in arid terrain, and sheltering through brutal winters. Their knowledge wasn't theoretical; it was inherited, tested, and refined across generations. Early European explorers who survived their expeditions often did so because they learned from Native guides or adopted the same techniques.

By the 1800s, mountain men and frontiersmen carried that tradition forward. Men like Jim Bridger and Kit Carson spent months alone in wilderness that killed less-prepared travelers. Their survival equipment was minimal by modern standards — a rifle, a knife, a fire-starting kit — but their skills were encyclopedic.

### Now: A Skill Set Worth Knowing

Modern wilderness travel is far safer. GPS navigation, synthetic insulation, and satellite emergency beacons have transformed backcountry safety. But technology fails. Batteries die. Satellite communicators get lost in rivers. The core skills — building fire, finding water, reading terrain, staying calm — remain as relevant as ever.

Search-and-rescue teams respond to thousands of calls every year, many involving lost or injured hikers who had perfectly good gear but lacked the fundamental knowledge to use their surroundings. That's what this badge addresses: not the gear, but the knowledge.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The U.S. has roughly 200 search-and-rescue incidents every day. A large percentage involve people with functioning cell phones who simply didn't know what to do before help arrived — which can take hours or days in remote terrain.

## Get Ready!

The skills in this guide will make you a better outdoor traveler, a more reliable trail companion, and someone who handles crises with skill instead of panic. Most of all, they'll give you genuine confidence the next time you're deep in the backcountry — because you'll know what to do if everything goes sideways.

## Kinds of Wilderness Survival Knowledge

Wilderness survival is a broad discipline. Here's how the major categories break down:

### Immediate Threat Management

The first category covers injuries and medical conditions that can kill or incapacitate you fastest: hypothermia, heatstroke, dehydration, and trauma. This is the "stop the bleeding" layer of survival — the knowledge you need in the first few minutes of a crisis.

### Shelter and Thermoregulation

Your body maintains a core temperature of 98.6°F in environments ranging from –30°F to 110°F. Clothing, shelter, and fire are the tools you use to make that happen. Understanding how your body loses and retains heat — and what to do about it — is foundational to surviving any environment.

### Water and Fire

You can survive about three weeks without food. Without water in a hot environment, you might last three days. Without fire in the cold, even less. Acquiring safe drinking water and building fire without modern tools are two of the oldest and most essential human skills.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
All fire-building, shelter construction, and signaling activities should be done with adult supervision and in accordance with Leave No Trace principles. Wilderness survival skills are emergency tools — practice them in appropriate settings and with a plan to restore any disturbed environment.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Navigation and Signaling

Being lost and being found are two different problems. Navigation keeps you from getting lost; signaling gets you found. Knowing how to communicate your location to rescue teams — using mirrors, fire, signals, or sound — can mean the difference between a 12-hour ordeal and a multi-day one.

### Mental Survival

Possibly the most underestimated category. Research on survival situations consistently shows that psychological factors — panic, poor decision-making, giving up — kill people who otherwise had the physical resources to survive. The mental side of survival is addressed directly in Req 3 and woven throughout this guide.
