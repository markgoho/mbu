You are three miles from the trailhead when the sky turns dark. Rain hammers down, the trail vanishes under rising water, and your group realizes there is no way you are hiking out tonight. What you do in the next two hours — finding shelter, staying warm, keeping calm — determines whether this becomes a good story or a dangerous situation.

Wilderness Survival is about learning to handle exactly these moments. This badge teaches you how to prevent emergencies in the backcountry, how to respond when they happen anyway, and how to keep yourself and others safe until help arrives.

## Then and Now

**Then:** Survival skills have been part of Scouting since its founding in 1910. Early Scouts learned many of these techniques from Indigenous peoples and frontier settlers who depended on them daily. The original 1911 *Boy Scout Handbook* devoted entire chapters to fire-making, shelter-building, and reading weather signs — skills that could mean life or death for anyone traveling through unsettled country. During World War II, military survival training drew heavily on these same Scouting skills, and returning veterans brought even more refined techniques back to their troops.

**Now:** Modern wilderness survival blends time-tested bushcraft with technology and science. We understand hypothermia and dehydration at a medical level. GPS devices and satellite communicators can summon help from almost anywhere. But the core truth has not changed: when technology fails — when batteries die, signals drop, and trails wash out — the skills in your head and the gear on your back are what keep you alive. Every year, search and rescue teams respond to thousands of calls in U.S. national parks alone. The people who fare best are almost always the ones who prepared.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The U.S. National Park Service logged over 3,500 search and rescue operations in a single recent year. The number one cause? Day hikers who underestimated their trip and got lost or injured without basic survival gear.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Get Ready!

You are about to learn skills that could genuinely save your life. This badge covers everything from treating backcountry injuries to building a shelter you will actually sleep in overnight. You will start fires without matches, purify water from streams, and learn to signal rescuers from the ground. These are not hypothetical exercises — they are the same techniques used by search and rescue professionals, wilderness guides, and military personnel around the world.

## Kinds of Wilderness Survival

Survival situations vary dramatically depending on where you are. The shelter that saves your life in a blizzard could be useless in a desert. Here are the major environments you will need to understand:

### Temperate Forest

Most Scouting happens in temperate forests — the woods, hills, and valleys that cover much of the United States. These environments offer abundant shelter materials (branches, leaves, bark) and water sources (streams, lakes), but weather can shift fast. A warm afternoon hike can turn into a cold, wet night. The biggest threats here are hypothermia from exposure, dehydration from exertion, and getting lost in dense tree cover where landmarks disappear.

### Cold and Snowy

Winter survival demands the most from your skills. Snow can be both your enemy and your ally — it causes frostbite and hypothermia, but it also insulates shelters and provides a water source when melted properly. Moving through deep snow burns enormous calories, so energy conservation becomes critical. Proper layering and understanding wind chill can mean the difference between discomfort and a medical emergency.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Hypothermia can set in at temperatures well above freezing — especially when you are wet and wind is blowing. Learn to recognize the early signs: uncontrollable shivering, confusion, and slurred speech. These topics are covered in detail in Requirement 1a.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Hot and Dry

Desert and arid environments flip the survival priorities. Water becomes your most urgent need, and shade your most important shelter. The ground surface can exceed 150°F in direct sun. Travel should happen during early morning and evening hours. Dehydration can become dangerous within hours, not days.

### Wet and Tropical

Constant moisture creates unique challenges: staying dry is nearly impossible, so staying warm despite being wet becomes the goal. Fungal infections, insect-borne diseases, and waterlogged gear compound the difficulty. Building a fire requires finding dry fuel — a real skill when everything around you is soaked.

### At or On Water

Capsized boats, flash floods, and unexpected water crossings create sudden survival situations. Cold water saps body heat 25 times faster than cold air. Knowing how to float, signal, and get out of the water quickly is essential.

## Your Path Through This Badge

This guide walks you through every requirement of the Wilderness Survival merit badge. You will start with hazards and first aid, build your survival mindset, assemble your kits, master shelter and fire, and learn signaling and water treatment. By the end, you will have spent a night in a shelter you built yourself.
