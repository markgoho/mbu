{{< drg/requirement number="3" >}}
Describe ways to avoid panic and to maintain a high level of morale when lost, and explain why this is important.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Panic is a biological response to perceived threat. Your heart rate jumps, your breathing quickens, and your rational mind takes a backseat. For a few seconds of panic, you might run into the woods randomly, drink saltwater, or make other decisions that make things worse. **Panic is the #1 survival killer because it overrides good judgment.**

The good news: panic is not inevitable. You can train yourself to recognize it and respond rationally.

{{< drg/image src="images/mental-resilience-practice.avif" alt="Scout sitting calmly in forest environment with eyes closed, practicing breathing exercises, peaceful natural setting with dappled sunlight" >}}

## Why Panic Is Dangerous

When panic hits, you lose the ability to:
- Think clearly and plan
- Navigate or navigate correctly
- Assess real danger vs. imagined danger
- Use survival skills you've learned
- Conserve energy and resources

In survival situations, clear thinking is more valuable than any gear. A panicked person with a knife is in more danger than a calm person with nothing.

## Ways to Avoid Panic

**Recognize the moment you're in danger or lost.** The sooner you accept the reality ("I am lost, and that's okay"), the sooner your rational mind takes control. Denial extends panic: "This can't be happening" or "Someone will find me in five minutes" prevents you from taking action.

**Use the STOP method:**
- **S**top moving and stop talking
- **T**hink about your situation clearly
- **O**bserve your surroundings and resources
- **P**lan your next steps using the Seven Priorities

This takes 5–10 minutes and shifts you from panic to action.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Panic Hits" >}}
You realize you're lost. Your heart is pounding, you feel dizzy, and you want to run.

- **Step 1:** STOP. Sit down. Stop talking to yourself or others.
- **Step 2:** Take three deep breaths. Count slowly: in for 4, hold for 4, out for 4. Do this three times. This is called box breathing, and it lowers your heart rate immediately.
- **Step 3:** Remind yourself: "I am trained for this. My trip plan means people will search for me. I have skills."
- **Step 4:** Take out your map and try to figure out where you are.
- **Step 5:** Once you've calmed down, you can think clearly and act.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

**Build confidence through preparation.** The best panic prevention is knowing you're prepared. You've packed the Scout Essentials. You've practiced navigation. You've studied first aid. When panic starts to creep in, you remind yourself: *"I have a first-aid kit. I have a signal mirror. I know how to build a fire. I'm going to be okay."* This knowledge is armor against panic.

**Stay active and purposeful.** Sitting alone in the dark is a recipe for catastrophic thinking. Instead, work on the Seven Priorities: gather firewood, improve your shelter, practice your signaling method. Action is the antidote to despair.

**Use breathing techniques.** Box breathing (4 counts in, 4 hold, 4 out, 4 hold) is used by Navy SEALs and emergency responders to manage stress. Your nervous system responds to breathing patterns, so controlling your breath controls your panic.

**Maintain perspective.** In most wilderness emergencies, rescue is coming. You have a trip plan, so someone knows approximately where to search. Rescue teams are good at finding people. Your job is to stay calm, make good decisions, and stay where you are so they can find you.

## Maintaining Morale

Even if you've avoided panic, long hours alone in the wilderness can lead to despair and depression. Here's how to stay mentally strong:

**Set small, achievable goals.**
- "By 3 p.m., I'll have improved my shelter"
- "I'll gather a full armload of firewood"
- "I'll eat a small snack and drink water"

Completing these goals gives you a sense of control and progress.

**Maintain daily routines.**
- Eat at regular times
- Keep yourself clean if water is available
- Mark time (draw a line for each day that passes)
- Set aside time to rest and time to work

Routine provides structure when everything feels chaotic.

**Use positive self-talk.**
- "I've trained for this"
- "People know where I am"
- "I'm making good decisions"
- "I'm staying calm"

The narrative you tell yourself matters. Negative self-talk ("I'm going to die out here") is a self-fulfilling prophecy. Positive self-talk ("I've got this") builds resilience.

**Find meaning in action.**
- Improving your shelter is not just survival—it's problem-solving
- Gathering firewood is not a chore—it's taking control of your environment
- Practicing signals is not boring—it's active rescue preparation

Reframe the work as purposeful, not desperate.

**If you're with others, support each other.**
- Check on each other's morale
- Split work so no one person bears all the burden
- Celebrate small wins together
- Keep conversations moving forward, not in catastrophic loops

## Why This Matters

Rescue statistics show that **panic and despair are leading causes of death in wilderness emergencies**, more so than the actual hazards. People who stay calm and follow the Seven Priorities survive. People who panic sometimes make fatal decisions (like trying to hike out at night and falling, or drinking saltwater).

Your mindset is your most important tool.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
In the 1970s, a psychologist named John Leach studied survival disasters and found that roughly 10% of people panic and become useless, 80% function with some difficulty, and 10% become hypercompetent. The key difference? Training and mental preparedness. Scouts with survival training move from the 80% into the hypercompetent group.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Practice

Try this at home: go into a room with the lights off for 10 minutes. Feel the discomfort and fear. Use box breathing to manage it. Remind yourself that you're safe. This is exposure to fear in a controlled way, which builds resilience.

Next, practice your navigation skills on a hike. Get intentionally "off route" and use your map and compass to find your way back. This builds confidence that you *can* handle navigation challenges.

These small practices build the mental toughness you need in a real emergency.
