{{< drg/requirement number="3" >}}
Describe ways to avoid panic and to maintain a high level of morale when lost, and explain why this is important.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

People die in survival situations they should have survived — with the right gear, in survivable conditions — because their brain convinced them to make terrible decisions. Panic is not just uncomfortable. It is physiologically lethal.

{{< drg/image src="images/scout-sitting-calmly-in-forest.avif" alt="A Scout in a clean uniform sitting calmly against a tree in a forest, eyes closed, breathing steadily" >}}

## Why Panic Kills

When your brain perceives a threat, the amygdala triggers the stress response: adrenaline floods your body, heart rate spikes, breathing becomes rapid, and decision-making shifts away from the prefrontal cortex — the part of your brain responsible for planning, logic, and memory. This fight-or-flight response evolved to help you outrun predators, not to help you navigate a topo map or select the right tinder for a fire.

In the backcountry, a panicking person typically does several things that worsen their situation:

- **Keeps moving** even when stopping is the right choice
- **Misreads landmarks** because tunnel vision reduces perception
- **Exhausts their water and food supply** through unnecessary exertion
- **Makes poor shelter and fire decisions** because rational planning fails
- **Ignores or doesn't signal** because they're focused on walking their way out

The S.T.O.P. protocol from Req 2 is specifically designed to break this panic cycle. But it works best when you've practiced it before you need it.

## Techniques to Avoid Panic

### Controlled Breathing

When panic begins, your breathing gets shallow and fast. This actually makes the panic worse — rapid breathing reduces CO₂ levels, causing dizziness and increased anxiety.

The antidote is deliberate, slow breathing. The technique used by military operators and first responders is called **box breathing:**

1. Inhale slowly through your nose for **4 counts**
2. Hold your breath for **4 counts**
3. Exhale slowly through your mouth for **4 counts**
4. Hold at the bottom for **4 counts**

Repeat for 2–4 minutes. This directly counters the fight-or-flight response by activating the parasympathetic nervous system — your "rest and digest" mode.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Practice box breathing at home, when you're completely calm. The reason military and first-responder training emphasizes it so heavily is that you won't be able to *learn* it when you need it. You need it to already be automatic.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Take Inventory

After using S.T.O.P., physically take stock of what you have. Pull out your pack contents. Count your water. Check your first aid kit. List your fire-starting tools.

This serves two psychological purposes: first, it gives your hands something productive to do, which prevents the "keep moving" urge; second, it often reveals that your situation is more manageable than it felt in the first panicked moments. Discovering you have three days of food and a functional fire starter looks very different from the "I'm going to die" narrative panic creates.

### Make a Plan and Write It Down

Once you've observed your situation, make a concrete plan — and write it down if you have paper. Writing forces you to slow down and think linearly. A written plan also becomes something you can refer back to, which helps if anxiety tries to revise your memory of how good or bad your situation actually is.

Include: where you are (as specifically as you know), what you have, what your first priority action is, and a timeline for reassessment.

### Keep Busy with Productive Tasks

Idle hands and an idle mind are the panic's best friend. The survival priorities are a built-in task list. Once you've done S.T.O.P. and addressed immediate first aid, begin working on shelter. Stack the material. Count the branches. The act of building something restores a sense of control that directly counters panic.

### Maintain Morale

Morale is a long-term concern — panic is acute, but despair sets in over hours and days. High morale makes people persistent, creative, and likely to keep working survival priorities even when progress is slow.

Ways to maintain morale in a survival situation:

{{< drg/checklist title="Morale Maintenance" subtitle="For extended survival situations (hours to days)" >}}
- **Set achievable short-term goals.** "I will gather enough firewood to last the night" is achievable. "I will get rescued by tomorrow" is not within your control.
- **Celebrate small wins.** Got a fire lit? That's a genuine accomplishment. Name it, acknowledge it, let it build confidence.
- **Maintain routine.** Even a simple routine — wake, assess, work, rest — creates structure that fights helplessness.
- **Keep warm and fed.** Physical comfort has a direct impact on psychological resilience. Address hypothermia and hunger as survival priorities, not luxuries.
- **Use humor when appropriate.** Groups who maintain the ability to laugh at absurd aspects of their situation tend to fare better than those who spiral into shared despair.
- **Remember people are looking for you.** If you filed a trip plan (Req 1c!), someone will be coming.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

## Why This Matters: The Psychology of Survival

Studies of survivors — shipwrecks, plane crashes, wilderness ordeals — consistently show that survival often has less to do with physical skills than with psychological state. People with equivalent skills and gear who face similar situations can have wildly different outcomes based on how they respond mentally.

The survivors tend to have several things in common: they accept their situation rather than denying it, they focus on what they *can* control rather than what they cannot, and they maintain hope without becoming delusional about their prospects.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="First Hour Lost" >}}
You realize you haven't seen a trail marker in 30 minutes. Here's a panic-resistant response:

- **Stop.** Literally sit down. Don't take another step.
- **Breathe.** Four counts in, four hold, four out. Do this three times.
- **Check your watch.** How long since the last confirmed location? It's probably less time than panic says.
- **Look around.** What landmarks do you see? Is the sun in the expected position for this time of day?
- **Check your gear.** Map, compass, water, food, matches — confirm what you have.
- **Decide your next move.** Backtrack to the last known point. Do not try to shortcut through unmarked terrain.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}
