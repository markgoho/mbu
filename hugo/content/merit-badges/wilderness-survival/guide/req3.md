{{< drg/requirement number="3" >}}
Avoiding Panic: Describe ways to avoid panic and to maintain a high level of morale when lost, and explain why this is important.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A lost hiker who stays calm and uses good judgment will almost always be found safe. A lost hiker who panics — running blindly, abandoning gear, making rash decisions — can turn a recoverable situation into a fatal one in hours. Panic is arguably the most dangerous hazard in wilderness survival, because it causes you to ignore everything you know.

## Why Panic Is So Dangerous

When your brain detects a threat, it triggers the fight-or-flight response: adrenaline floods your system, your heart rate spikes, your breathing becomes shallow, and your higher thinking shuts down. This response evolved to help you escape predators, but in a survival situation, it works against you. You do not need to outrun anything — you need to think clearly, assess your resources, and make careful decisions.

Panic leads to predictable, dangerous behaviors:

- **Running without direction** — which takes you farther from the trail, burns precious energy, and increases injury risk
- **Abandoning gear** — dropping your pack to move faster removes the very tools keeping you alive
- **Refusing to stop** — pushing forward into the night when you should be building shelter
- **Ignoring signals from your body** — not eating, not drinking, not treating injuries because "I need to keep moving"

## Techniques to Stay Calm

### S.T.O.P.

You learned this in [Req 2](/merit-badges/wilderness-survival/guide/req2/), and it bears repeating: **Stop, Think, Observe, Plan.** The physical act of sitting down and forcing yourself through these steps interrupts the panic cycle. You cannot think clearly while running. You cannot observe while hyperventilating. Sit down first.

### Box Breathing

Box breathing is used by Navy SEALs, emergency medical technicians, and firefighters to manage stress in life-threatening situations. It works by activating your parasympathetic nervous system — the part of your brain that calms you down.

The technique is simple:

1. **Inhale** slowly through your nose for 4 seconds
2. **Hold** your breath for 4 seconds
3. **Exhale** slowly through your mouth for 4 seconds
4. **Hold** with lungs empty for 4 seconds
5. Repeat 4–6 times

Four rounds of box breathing can noticeably lower your heart rate and clear your thinking. Practice this at home so it becomes automatic when you need it.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Practice box breathing before stressful situations you already face — before a test, a sports competition, or a public speaking event. The more you use it in everyday life, the more naturally you will reach for it in an emergency.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Talk Out Loud

This sounds strange, but talking to yourself (or to your group) forces your brain into rational, verbal thinking mode instead of emotional, reactive mode. Narrate what you are doing: "Okay, I am going to sit down. I have my pack. I have water. The sun is to my west, so that direction is west. I was heading north on the trail."

Hearing your own voice describe the situation can make it feel more manageable. It also helps you notice resources and clues you might miss in silent panic.

### Stay Put

Unless you are in immediate danger (wildfire, flash flood, avalanche zone), staying in one place is almost always the right decision when you are lost. Search and rescue teams work outward from your last known position. Every step you take away from that position expands the search area and delays your rescue.

If you filed a trip plan (as covered in [Req 1c](/merit-badges/wilderness-survival/guide/req1c/)), searchers know your route and timeline. Give them the best chance of finding you by staying where you are.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
The only exceptions to "stay put" are if your current location is dangerous (unstable ground, rising water, fire) or if you are certain of the way back and have plenty of daylight. If you are unsure, stay.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Maintaining Morale

Survival situations can last hours or days. Even after the initial panic passes, boredom, loneliness, cold, and hunger can erode your willpower. Maintaining morale is not a luxury — it is a survival necessity, because people with low morale stop taking care of themselves.

### Stay Busy with Purposeful Tasks

Idle hands feed anxious minds. Work through the survival priorities systematically: improve your shelter, gather more firewood, collect water, build signals. Each completed task gives you a sense of control and accomplishment.

### Set Small Goals

Instead of thinking "I need to survive until someone finds me," think "I am going to collect enough firewood to last until midnight." Small, achievable goals keep you focused on the present and give you regular wins.

### Maintain Routines

Eat at regular intervals, even if you do not feel hungry. Drink water on a schedule. Keep your shelter organized. Routines create a sense of normalcy that fights the psychological weight of the situation.

### Lean on Your Group

If you are with others, talk to each other. Share stories, make plans, assign tasks. Taking care of someone else — encouraging a scared patrol member, sharing food, helping with shelter — is one of the most powerful morale boosters there is. Leadership in a crisis is not about being fearless; it is about keeping everyone functional.

### Positive Self-Talk

Replace catastrophic thoughts with factual ones:

- Instead of "No one will ever find me," think "My trip plan says I will be missed by 6 p.m., and searchers will know which trail I am on."
- Instead of "I cannot survive out here," think "I have shelter, I have water, and I know how to build a fire."
- Instead of "This is hopeless," think "Search and rescue teams find people every day. My job is to make myself easy to find."

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Psychological studies of survival situations consistently show that the people who survive are not necessarily the strongest or most experienced — they are the ones who maintain a positive, problem-solving mindset. Attitude is the most powerful survival tool you own.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}
