{{< drg/requirement number="1c" >}}
Explain how a trip plan could help prevent a wilderness survival situation.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Most wilderness emergencies don't start with a catastrophe. They start with a missed turn, an overly ambitious distance, or a weather change nobody checked for. A trip plan is the most powerful prevention tool you have — and it works before you ever set foot on the trail.

## What a Trip Plan Does

A trip plan serves two critical functions:

1. **It forces you to think through hazards before you leave.** Writing down your route, timeline, and emergency contacts makes you confront questions like "What if the creek is too high to cross?" and "What if someone gets hurt at mile six?"

2. **It tells people where to look for you.** If you don't come back on time, a trip plan gives search teams your route, your group size, your vehicle description, and your expected return. Without it, searchers are looking for a needle in a haystack.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
According to the National Park Service, the average search and rescue operation costs over $10,000 and involves dozens of volunteers. When the missing person left a detailed trip plan, the average search time drops dramatically — because rescuers know where to start looking.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## What Goes in a Trip Plan

A solid trip plan includes:

### Group Information
- Names of all participants
- Emergency contact for each person
- Medical conditions, allergies, and medications
- Experience level of the group

### Route Details
- Trailhead location and parking area
- Planned route with waypoints
- Alternative routes in case of closures or hazards
- Expected distance and elevation gain

### Timeline
- Departure date and time
- Expected arrival at key waypoints
- Planned return date and time
- **Overdue time** — the specific time when your emergency contact should call for help

### Vehicle Information
- Make, model, color, and license plate
- Where it will be parked

### Emergency Plan
- Nearest ranger station or emergency services
- Bail-out points along the route
- Communication plan (cell coverage areas, satellite device if available)

{{< drg/tip >}}
The most important line in your trip plan is the overdue time. Be specific: "If we haven't checked in by 6:00 PM Sunday, call the county sheriff's office at [number]." Vague instructions like "call someone if we're late" delay rescues.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Who Gets the Trip Plan

Leave copies of your trip plan with at least two people:

- **A trusted person at home** — a parent, guardian, or friend who will act on the overdue time
- **A trailhead or ranger station** — many parks have registration boxes at trailheads; sign in

Your emergency contact needs to be someone who will actually follow through. Choose someone reliable and make sure they understand what to do if you're overdue.

## How Trip Plans Prevent Survival Situations

The act of creating a trip plan often prevents emergencies by exposing bad ideas before they become bad situations:

{{< drg/be-prepared title="The Trip Plan Reality Check" >}}
You're planning a 15-mile day hike and start filling out the trip plan. As you write down the timeline, you realize:

- **Sunset is at 5:30 PM**, and at your group's pace, you won't finish until 7:00 PM.
- **The weather forecast** calls for thunderstorms after 2:00 PM.
- **One group member** has never hiked more than 8 miles in a day.

Without a trip plan, you might have discovered these problems at mile 10 in the dark during a thunderstorm. With a trip plan, you discover them at your kitchen table and shorten the route to 9 miles with an early start.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

## Weather and Conditions Check

A trip plan isn't just a form you fill out — it's a process. Part of that process is checking conditions:

- **Weather forecast** for the area (not just your hometown)
- **Trail conditions** — closures, washouts, recent bear activity
- **Water levels** for any stream crossings
- **Fire restrictions** that might affect your plans
- **Daylight hours** for the time of year

Update your trip plan if conditions change before departure. A plan written a week ago may not reflect today's reality.
