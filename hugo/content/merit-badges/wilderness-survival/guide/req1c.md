{{< drg/requirement number="1c" >}}
Explain how a trip plan could help prevent a wilderness survival situation.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Most search-and-rescue operations start with a phone call from a worried family member. Someone was supposed to be back by Sunday afternoon. It's now Tuesday morning. No one knows which trailhead they parked at, which route they planned, or who else was in the group. That's when SAR teams spend days searching the wrong area.

A trip plan — sometimes called a float plan for water trips — is a simple document that changes that scenario completely.

{{< drg/image src="images/trip-plan-on-table.avif" alt="A handwritten trip plan form on a clipboard next to a topographic map, water bottle, and compass on a wooden table" >}}

## What Goes in a Trip Plan

A complete trip plan includes:

{{< drg/checklist title="Trip Plan Essentials" subtitle="Leave this with a trusted contact before you depart" >}}
- Departure point: Trailhead name, parking lot, access road, and GPS coordinates if known.
- Planned route: A general description of your path (trail name, compass direction, alternate routes).
- Destination: Where you plan to be at the end of each day or at the trip's end.
- Return date and time: When you plan to be back — and what time to call for help if you haven't checked in.
- Party members: Full names, ages, and contact information for everyone in the group.
- Emergency contacts: Who to call if you don't return.
- Vehicle description: Make, model, color, and license plate of the vehicle at the trailhead.
- Gear summary: Type of shelter, clothing, and communication devices you're carrying.
- Medical conditions: Any relevant conditions or allergies in the group.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

## How a Trip Plan Prevents (or Shortens) a Survival Situation

A trip plan doesn't prevent you from getting lost or injured — but it dramatically reduces how long you stay in that condition.

**It triggers faster rescue.** Without a trip plan, rescuers don't know when to start looking or where to look. With a plan, your contact knows: "They were supposed to be at Barker Meadow by Saturday night. If I don't hear from them by Sunday noon, call county SAR." That's a specific trigger, not "I haven't heard from them in a while."

**It focuses the search.** SAR teams spend enormous resources searching the wrong areas when they don't have a route description. Your trip plan narrows the search zone immediately. The difference between a one-day rescue and a five-day rescue often comes down to whether responders know which trail to check first.

**It establishes your gear profile.** Knowing you're carrying a three-season tent and 72 hours of food tells rescuers you can likely self-sustain for several days. That's different from knowing you went out for a day hike with a light pack.

**It communicates medical urgency.** If one person in your group is diabetic or carries an EpiPen, that information in your trip plan helps emergency responders prepare appropriately before they even find you.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The U.S. Air Force Rescue Coordination Center estimates that having a detailed trip plan on file with a reliable contact can reduce search time by 50% or more — because responders know where to look from the start instead of beginning with a large search area.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Leaving Your Plan and Calling It In

Your trip plan is only useful if someone reliable has it. The best approach is a two-layer system:

**Leave a physical or emailed copy** with a responsible adult who will definitely notice if you don't check in. This is your "notification contact." They need to know exactly what to do if you miss your check-in time — ideally, you've talked through the steps with them in advance.

**Register with the ranger station.** Many trailheads have trail registers or ranger stations that accept trip plans. Park rangers are often first responders in wilderness emergencies, and having your plan on file with them means professional rescuers are aware of your trip from day one.

**Cancel your plan when you return.** Just as important as filing the plan is canceling it when you get back safely. This prevents a frantic, unnecessary rescue operation being launched for a group that drove home safely two days ago.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Your Contact Missed Your Check-In Window" >}}
You planned to call your parent by 6 PM Sunday. It's 8 PM and they haven't heard from you.

- **Step 1:** Don't panic. Review the plan together — do you have a route description, trailhead location, and vehicle description?
- **Step 2:** Try calling/texting first — signal can appear in unexpected places.
- **Step 3:** At a predetermined time (which you agreed on in advance), contact the county sheriff or SAR team — not just the park, and not just your friends.
- **Step 4:** Provide the full trip plan: route, vehicle, gear, last known position.
- **Step 5:** Stay near a phone and don't organize an unofficial search party that could interfere with professional responders.
