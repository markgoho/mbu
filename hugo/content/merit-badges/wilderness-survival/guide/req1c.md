{{< drg/requirement number="1c" >}}
Explain how a trip plan could help prevent a wilderness survival situation.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Most wilderness survival situations start the same way: someone went into the backcountry without telling anyone where they were going, when they planned to return, or what route they would take. A trip plan is the single most effective tool for preventing a survival emergency — and for getting rescued quickly if one happens.

## What a Trip Plan Does

A trip plan serves two critical functions:

**Before something goes wrong,** the process of creating a trip plan forces you to think through your route, timing, weather, and contingencies. You research the area, check conditions, and prepare for hazards. Many survival situations are prevented simply because the planning process revealed a problem: a trail closure, an approaching storm, or a route that was too ambitious for the group's experience level.

**After something goes wrong,** the trip plan tells someone exactly where to look for you. If you told a trusted contact that you were hiking the Lake Loop trail from the west parking lot and expected to be back by 6 p.m., and you have not returned by 8 p.m., searchers know which trail, which direction, and roughly where you might be. Without that information, a search area can span hundreds of square miles.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Search and rescue teams report that the average time to locate a missing hiker who left a trip plan is measured in hours. Without one, it can take days — and every extra hour in a survival situation increases the risk of hypothermia, dehydration, and panic.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## What to Include

A good trip plan covers five categories:

### Who

List every person in your group: full names, ages, relevant medical conditions (allergies, medications, physical limitations), and emergency contact information. If searchers find someone from your group, they need to know who else might still be out there.

### Where

Specify your planned route in detail: trailhead name and location, which trails you will take, which direction you will travel, planned campsites or stopping points, and your exit point. Include a map with your route marked.

### When

Provide departure date and time, expected return date and time, and planned timing for key waypoints along the route. Build in buffer time — trails always take longer than you think, especially in a group.

### What

List your gear, food, and water supply. Note any communication devices (cell phone, satellite communicator, radio). Include your vehicle description and where you plan to park.

### What If

Describe your contingency plans: What will you do if the weather turns bad? What if someone gets injured? What is your alternate route? At what point should your emergency contact call for help if they have not heard from you?

{{< drg/checklist title="Trip Plan Essentials" subtitle="Information your trip plan should include" >}}
- Group roster: Names, ages, medical conditions, emergency contacts for all participants.
- Vehicle info: Make, model, color, license plate, parking location.
- Route details: Trailhead, trail names, direction of travel, campsites, exit point.
- Timeline: Departure time, expected waypoint times, expected return time.
- Gear summary: Key equipment, food supply, water capacity, communication devices.
- Emergency trigger: When your contact should call for help if you do not check in.
- Alternate routes: Backup plan if primary route becomes impassable.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

## Who Gets the Plan

Leave copies of your trip plan with at least two people:

1. **A responsible adult who is not on the trip** — a parent, guardian, or Scoutmaster who stayed home. This person needs to know your exact trigger time: the specific moment when they should call search and rescue if they have not heard from you.

2. **A visible location at the trailhead** — many trailheads have registration boxes. If not, leave a copy under your dashboard (in a sealed bag so it is visible to rangers who check parked cars).

{{< drg/tip >}}
Set a specific check-in schedule with your emergency contact. For example: "I will text you from the ridge at 2 p.m. If you have not heard from me by 8 p.m., call the county sheriff's office." Be specific about the time, the method of communication, and who to call.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Common Mistakes

The best trip plan in the world fails if you do not follow it. Here are the mistakes that turn a good plan into a useless one:

- **Changing your route without telling anyone.** If you decide to take a different trail, your trip plan now points searchers to the wrong place.
- **Setting an unrealistic return time.** If your plan says 4 p.m. but you always run late, your emergency contact will stop taking the deadline seriously.
- **Not naming a specific person.** Saying "someone knows where I am" is not a trip plan. A specific person must hold the plan and know what to do with it.
- **Forgetting to close the loop.** Always notify your emergency contact when you return safely. Otherwise, they may launch an unnecessary search — or worse, stop trusting your plans for next time.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="The Trail You Didn't Plan For" >}}
Your group planned to hike the 8-mile Ridge Trail loop, but two miles in, you find the bridge over Cedar Creek is washed out. A side trail offers a detour, but it adds 3 miles and goes through unfamiliar terrain.

- **Step one:** Stop and discuss as a group. Do not push forward without consensus.
- **Step two:** Check your map. Does the detour reconnect with your planned route?
- **Step three:** Assess your resources. Do you have enough daylight, water, and energy for the extra distance?
- **Step four:** If you take the detour, send a text or leave a note at the junction explaining your change.
- **Step five:** If the detour is too risky, turn back. Reaching the trailhead safely by backtracking beats getting lost on an unfamiliar trail.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}
