{{< drg/requirement number="1c" >}}
Explain how a trip plan could help prevent a wilderness survival situation.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Most wilderness rescues don't start with a dramatic emergency — they start with nobody knowing where to look. A trip plan is the document that turns a search into a rescue rather than a recovery.

{{< drg/image src="images/trip-plan-on-table.avif" alt="A map spread out on a table with a handwritten trip plan sheet, compass, and pencil beside it" >}}

## What Is a Trip Plan?

A trip plan (also called a float plan for water travel) is a written document left with a trusted person — a parent, troop leader, or friend — before you head into the backcountry. It tells rescuers where you went, when you should be back, and how to contact authorities if you don't return on schedule.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Leaving a trip plan is one of the easiest things you can do to survive an emergency. It costs nothing, takes five minutes, and has saved hundreds of lives. A good trip plan can cut search time from days to hours — and hours matter when someone is injured, dehydrated, or hypothermic.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## What a Trip Plan Contains

A complete trip plan covers six key areas:

{{< drg/checklist title="Trip Plan Components" subtitle="Leave this with a responsible person before every backcountry trip" >}}
- **Who:** Names, ages, and contact info for everyone in the group. Any relevant medical conditions (allergies, medications, pre-existing injuries).
- **Where:** Specific trailhead, access road, and starting GPS coordinates or address. Planned route and key waypoints. Destination (campsite name/coordinates if known).
- **When:** Departure date and time. Expected return date and time. Specific check-in times if the trip includes cell coverage checkpoints.
- **What:** Gear and equipment. Vehicle description, color, and license plate (if driving to trailhead). Color and type of tents.
- **Emergency contacts:** Who to call if you don't return — county sheriff, local SAR (Search and Rescue), or Forest Service contact info.
- **Rescue triggers:** "If I don't call by Sunday at 6pm, contact [name and number] to report us overdue."
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

## How a Trip Plan Prevents a Survival Situation

The question asks how a trip plan "could help prevent" a survival situation — not just respond to one. That prevention works in two ways:

**Before the trip — forcing better decisions:** The act of writing a trip plan forces you to think through your route, identify risks, and confirm your gear. Scouts who plan carefully are more likely to notice gaps — a missing water filter, a route that goes above treeline in storm season — before they matter.

**During the emergency — shortening your time at risk:** If you're injured or lost, the clock is ticking. Dehydration, hypothermia, and shock all worsen with time. A trip plan with a clear rescue trigger means that rescuers are activated when they should be, not when a family member finally calls the ranger station three days later.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="The Overdue Hiker" >}}
Imagine your group twists an ankle and camps for an unplanned extra night. Without a trip plan, you're just "late." With a trip plan:

- **Day 0:** You file the plan — trailhead, route, campsites, return time.
- **Day 2, 6pm:** Your contact notices you haven't returned. Waits until the rescue trigger you specified.
- **Day 2, 9pm:** Rescue trigger reached. Contact calls the county sheriff.
- **Day 3, 6am:** Search and rescue begins — starting exactly where your plan says.
- **Day 3, 10am:** SAR team finds your campsite and reaches you.

Compare that to a scenario without a trip plan, where SAR might not even begin searching for 48–72 hours and would have no idea where to start.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

## Trip Plan vs. Personal Locator Beacon

A trip plan and a Personal Locator Beacon (PLB) are not the same thing — but they work well together. A PLB sends a one-way distress signal with your GPS location. That tells rescuers *something* is wrong and *where you are*, but not who you are, who's with you, or what medical issues to expect.

A trip plan gives rescuers context that technology can't: your planned route, group size, medical conditions, and the type of emergency most likely to have happened based on your terrain. That context helps rescuers arrive prepared — with the right personnel and equipment.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Leave your trip plan with someone who knows you — not just someone who will passively hold the paper. Walk them through the plan so they know what the rescue trigger means and who to call. A plan that sits in a drawer unopened until day four is not nearly as useful as one held by an attentive contact who knows exactly when to act.
{{< /drg/tip >}}
