Explain how a trip plan could help prevent a wilderness survival situation.

A trip plan is not a request for permission. It's **insurance**. The simplest trip plan has answered three questions:

1. Where are we going?
2. When will we be back?
3. Who should we contact if we don't?

{{< drg/image src="images/trip-plan-preparation.avif" alt="Scout at home reviewing detailed trail map on table with backpack, compass, and water bottle nearby, preparing for departure" >}}

If something goes wrong and you don't check in, someone knows where to find you. This is how rescue teams know where to start searching.

## How a Trip Plan Prevents Emergencies

**You'll prepare better.** Writing down your route forces you to look at the map, check the weather, and think about distance and time. Often this reveals problems before you leave: "Oh, that's a longer hike than I thought" or "Rain is forecast—should we reschedule?"

**You won't go out unprepared.** You'll know the elevation gain, water availability, and potential hazards. You'll pack the right gear instead of guessing.

**You'll tell someone the truth.** You can't lie to yourself about how hard a hike is if you've written down the route and timing. This keeps overconfident Scouts from getting in over their heads.

**You'll check in on time.** If you said you'd be back by 6 p.m., you'll make sure you are—or contact someone if you're delayed.

## What to Include in a Trip Plan

{{< drg/checklist title="Trip Plan Checklist" >}}
- **Trip leader name and contact number**: Who's in charge
- **Participants**: Names, ages, contact info for each Scout (and parent if under 18)
- **Destination**: Exact location, trailhead name, access point
- **Route**: Major waypoints, alternate routes if needed
- **Distance and elevation gain**: For time estimation
- **Expected departure time**: When you're leaving home
- **Expected return time**: When you expect to be back
- **Gear checklist**: Scout Essentials, first-aid kit, navigation tools
- **Emergency contact**: Who to call if you don't check in (usually a parent or other adult)
- **Rescue procedure**: Where to go if something happens (meet at trailhead, wait at camp, etc.)
- **Known hazards**: Weather, water crossings, wildlife, cell phone dead zones
- **Communication plan**: How will you stay in touch with the outside world (satellite messager, check-in calls, etc.)
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

## Before You Leave

1. **Obtain weather forecast** for your destination. Rain changes everything—muddy trails, stream crossings become dangerous, cold exposure increases.

2. **Check recent trip reports.** Other hikers post updates online. If they report washouts or unexpected snow, adjust your plans.

3. **Tell someone your plan.** Write it down and leave a copy with a trusted adult. They should know:
   - Where you're going
   - Your expected return time
   - Your contact info
   - What to do if you don't return (usually: wait 2 hours, then call local sheriff/park rangers)

4. **Leave earlier than you think necessary.** If you expect 4 hours, leave time to get back before dark. If something slows you down (injury, trail closure, weather), you won't find yourself scrambling in darkness.

5. **Know your turnaround time.** Before you go, decide: "If we haven't reached X by Y time, we turn around—no matter what." This prevents the panic decision to push on and end up stranded.

## During the Trip

Check your progress against your plan:
- Are we moving at the expected pace?
- Is water available where we expected?
- Are others managing the altitude/distance okay?
- Is the weather as forecast?

If something isn't matching your plan, **adjust immediately**. Turn back early if needed. Better to hike the same route next weekend in better weather than to get stuck.

## After the Trip

Check in with your emergency contact. Let them know you're back safely. This simple step closes the loop and ensures they won't be worrying or calling for help.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Many overdue hikers are found alive because someone knew their planned route. Hikers found after being truly lost usually don't have a trip plan on file—no one knew where to search.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Real-World Example: A Trip Plan in Action

Imagine your troop plans a 10-mile backpacking trip up a mountain valley. Your trip plan says:

- **Destination:** Lost Lake Trail, Cascade Range
- **Distance:** 10 miles, 2,000 ft elevation gain
- **Departure:** 8:00 a.m.
- **Expected return:** 7:00 p.m.
- **Emergency contact:** Scoutmaster (555-0100), to be contacted if not back by 8:30 p.m.

At 2:00 p.m., you're only 3 miles in (behind schedule). One Scout has a blister that's slowing you down. You check your watch: *if we keep this pace, we won't be back until 9:00 p.m.* That's an hour past your plan. Decision: **turn around now.** You'll summit the lake next year, but this weekend you return by 7:30 p.m. and your emergency contact is never even called.

That's a trip plan working exactly right.
