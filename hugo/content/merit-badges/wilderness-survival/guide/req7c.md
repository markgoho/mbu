{{< drg/requirement number="7c" >}}
Describe from memory five ground-to-air signals and tell what they mean.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Ground-to-air signals are symbols large enough for an aircraft to see from altitude. They're a universal language recognized by search and rescue crews worldwide — no radio, no phone, no technical knowledge required. Just materials you can find on the ground and a clearing to lay them in.

{{< drg/image src="images/ground-to-air-signals-table.avif" alt="Six ground-to-air emergency signal symbols: V, X, N, Y, an arrow, and three vertical lines, each with its meaning labeled" >}}

## The Five Standard Ground-to-Air Signals

The following symbols are part of the international ground-to-air emergency code, used by search and rescue teams globally:

| Symbol | Meaning |
|--------|---------|
| **V** | Require assistance |
| **X** | Require medical assistance |
| **N** | No — negative |
| **Y** | Yes — affirmative |
| **→** (Arrow) | Proceeding in this direction |

A sixth signal worth knowing: a rectangle or three lines (|||) means "need food and water."

## How to Make Ground-to-Air Signals

The signals can be constructed from anything that creates visual contrast with the surrounding surface:

**In forest clearings:** Arrange dark rocks, logs, or branches on lighter ground. Large logs from deadfall work well — three or four logs in the right arrangement create a readable symbol.

**In snow:** Stamp the pattern into the snow with your feet (creates shadow), or fill the shape with dark rocks, branches, pine boughs, or any dark material.

**In sand or beach:** Dig a trench 1–2 feet deep (shadow creates contrast) or use rocks, kelp, or driftwood.

**With gear:** A bright orange tarp, a colored sleeping bag, an emergency blanket, or clothing spread in the right shape signals even faster than natural materials.

**Size:** Each line should be at least 10 feet long. The full symbol should be 30–40 feet across. Smaller symbols aren't visible from aircraft altitude.

## Preparing for Your Counselor

The requirement says "from memory" — meaning you need to be able to draw or describe all five without looking them up. Here's a memory strategy:

**V** — "V for victory, and right now I need help to be victorious." Require assistance.

**X** — "X marks the spot where I'm hurt." Require medical assistance.

**N/Y** — These are the same as "no" and "yes" in English — easiest to remember.

**Arrow** — Points where you're going. If you're leaving your position to attempt self-rescue, leave an arrow pointing your direction so search teams know where to look next.

{{< drg/tip >}}
If you build any ground-to-air signal, stay near it when you hear or see aircraft. The signal tells them where to look; your whistle and mirror signals confirm your precise location once they're in the area.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Using Signals Strategically

Ground-to-air signals are passive — they work while you sleep. Build them in your most visible clearing and then use your energy on the active priorities: shelter, fire, water.

**When to use N or Y:** Search aircraft sometimes fly low enough to drop notes or communicate via wing waggling. If a pilot flies in low and rocks the wings, they've seen you. An N or Y symbol lets you answer a yes/no question without voice contact — useful for communicating whether you're injured.

**Arrow as movement indicator:** If you decide to move (always think hard before doing this — staying put is almost always the right choice), lay an arrow pointing your direction of travel. This ensures that if searchers reach your original position, they know where to search next.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Aircraft Overhead — Maximize Your Signal" >}}
You hear a search aircraft in the distance. You have a signal fire ready, a mirror, and a whistle.

- **Step 1:** Ignite your signal fire immediately to create smoke.
- **Step 2:** Move to your most open clearing with your signal mirror.
- **Step 3:** Flash the mirror toward the aircraft in the direction you last heard the engine.
- **Step 4:** Blow three whistle blasts repeatedly.
- **Step 5:** Wave bright clothing overhead with both arms extended.
- **Step 6:** If the aircraft turns toward you or circles, you've been spotted. Continue signals until they confirm contact.
- **Step 7:** Stay in the clearing — aircraft need a landing zone or drop zone and will circle to identify one.
