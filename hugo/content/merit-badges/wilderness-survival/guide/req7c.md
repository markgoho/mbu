Describe from memory five ground-to-air signals and tell what they mean.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Ground-to-air signals are large symbols created on the ground — using rocks, logs, trampled vegetation, or other high-contrast materials — that can communicate your situation to search aircraft flying overhead. They were standardized by the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO) and are used by search and rescue operations worldwide.

{{< drg/image src="images/ground-to-air-signals-table.avif" alt="A grid showing five ground-to-air signal symbols — X, V, arrow, F, and LL — each with a label indicating its meaning" >}}

## The Five Core Ground-to-Air Signals

Your counselor wants you to describe these from memory — not read them off a card. Study them until you can draw them and state their meaning without prompting.

### Signal 1: X — Need Medical Assistance

An X symbol means someone in the group is injured or ill and requires medical attention.

**Construction:** Make the symbol at least 10 feet across — larger is better. Use rocks, logs, branches, or trample the grass into the shape.

**When to use:** When a member of your group has a significant injury (fracture, serious wound, severe illness) that requires medical evacuation rather than just a rescue escort.

### Signal 2: V — Need Assistance

A V symbol is the general distress signal — it means your group needs help but isn't necessarily injured.

**Construction:** Same materials, same size rule (10+ feet per arm).

**When to use:** When you're lost or stranded but everyone is physically okay.

{{< drg/tip >}}
If you only have time or materials to make one symbol before a plane passes, make V for general distress. It communicates the most important message — "We need help" — without requiring the aircraft crew to interpret a more specific signal under time pressure.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Signal 3: → (Arrow) — Traveling This Direction

An arrow indicates the direction the group is traveling — useful if you're mobile and trying to help rescuers intercept you.

**Construction:** Make it large enough to see from altitude. Point the arrow in your actual direction of travel. A helpful addition: the letter "Y" near the arrow means "Yes, we are moving." Leave additional arrows at intervals along your route as trail markers for the search team.

**When to use:** When you've decided to travel toward safety and want rescuers to know which way to follow.

### Signal 4: F — Need Food and Water

F indicates that the group's most urgent need is food and water — useful for indicating a non-ambulatory situation where the group can't move but isn't medically injured.

**Construction:** A capital F using sticks, rocks, or soil.

**When to use:** When the group is sheltered and stationary but running low on food and water, and can sustain for additional time without medical evacuation.

### Signal 5: LL — All Well / Proceed as Planned

Two parallel L's (or sometimes the symbol looks like a double-L) means "We are okay — proceed as planned." This is used when a group needs to communicate that a previously unusual situation has been resolved.

**Construction:** Two L shapes side-by-side.

**When to use:** If you were reported overdue, then found your way — signaling LL tells a searching aircraft that you've resolved your situation and don't need assistance.

## How to Make Effective Ground Symbols

The signal is useless if it can't be seen. Size and contrast are everything.

{{< drg/checklist title="Ground Signal Best Practices" subtitle="Make your signal impossible to miss from altitude" >}}
- **Make symbols at least 10 feet per stroke** — from altitude, smaller symbols look like random debris
- **Choose open ground:** Clear of tree cover, on a ridge, in a meadow, on a beach, or a frozen lake
- **Maximize contrast:** Dark materials on light ground (dark rocks on snow, burnt wood on sand), or light materials on dark ground (light rocks on dark soil)
- **Shadow helps:** Orient symbols so they cast shadows, which makes them more visible from above during angled sunlight
- **Add color:** If you have bright fabric (orange rain gear, space blanket), weight it down near the symbol for additional visual contrast
- **Arrow updates:** If you later change direction, add a new arrow. If you're rescued, remove or X out old signals if possible to avoid confusing future SAR operations
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Aircraft Overhead — No Materials Handy" >}}
A helicopter passes overhead before you've had time to build a ground signal. What do you do?

- **Mirror:** First option — aim a flash immediately
- **Whistle:** Three blasts immediately
- **Wave:** Both arms fully raised and lowered repeatedly (distress waving, not a friendly wave)
- **Signal fire:** If you have one going, add green vegetation immediately for visible smoke
- **Lay out gear:** Sprint to the most open area and spread your brightest-colored gear flat — your orange rain jacket takes 10 seconds to lay down

After the aircraft passes, immediately begin constructing ground signals so you're ready next time.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The ICAO ground-to-air signal system was developed specifically because voice radio communication with downed pilots was unreliable in early aviation. Search aircraft needed to be able to read the status of a group from the air without landing. The symbols chosen were simple enough to construct from any available materials and readable enough not to be confused with natural terrain features.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}
