When a search aircraft passes overhead, you only have seconds to communicate. Ground-to-air signals are standardized patterns that pilots recognize instantly. These signals can be made with rocks, logs, your body, or reflective material on the ground.

{{< drg/image src="images/ground-air-signals.avif" alt="Five ground-to-air signal patterns viewed from above: X pattern (distress), V pattern (assistance), triangle (safe to land), arrow (direction), and SOS pattern (three symbols in line), each pattern clearly distinct with rocks or logs" >}}

## The Five Essential Signals

### Signal 1: X Pattern (Distress)

**What it means:** "We need medical assistance" or "Distress – we need help"

**How to make it:**
- Lay two long branches or logs in an X shape at least 10 feet across
- Use large rocks arranged in an X pattern
- Arrange bright orange tarps or cloth in an X
- The bigger the X, the more visible from aircraft

**Why it works:** The X is instantly recognizable as the international distress symbol. Pilots know to report an X to rescue coordinators.

### Signal 2: V Pattern (Need Assistance)

**What it means:** "We need assistance" or "Help needed, but not medical emergency"

**How to make it:**
- Arrange rocks, logs, or bright material in a large V shape (at least 10 feet across)
- The point of the V can indicate a direction if needed

**Why it works:** Rescuers recognize V as a request for non-emergency assistance (fuel, water, supplies, guidance).

### Signal 3: Large Triangle (Safe to Land Here)

**What it means:** "It is safe to land here"

**How to make it:**
- Arrange rocks or logs in a triangle shape
- All sides at least 15 feet long
- Ensure the area inside is actually clear and safe for a helicopter or plane to land

**Why it works:** Pilots need to know if there's a clear landing zone. A triangle tells them the area is safe.

### Signal 4: Arrow Pointing Direction

**What it means:** "Direction of travel" or "We are moving in this direction"

**How to make it:**
- Create an arrow using rocks, logs, or bright material
- The arrow points in the direction you're moving or the direction rescuers should go
- At least 15 feet long

**Why it works:** If you've moved from your original location, an arrow helps rescuers understand your route and likely location.

### Signal 5: Large "SOS" (Extreme Distress)

**What it means:** "Life-threatening emergency – immediate rescue required"

**How to make it:**
- Arrange rocks or logs to spell "SOS"
- Each letter should be at least 10 feet across
- Or arrange three symbols in a line (three rocks, three crosses, three fires) to represent the SOS pattern

**Why it works:** SOS is the international maritime distress signal. It indicates life-threatening emergency requiring immediate response.

## Making Signals Visible

**Material choices:**
- **Rocks:** Visible in most environments, especially dark rocks in light areas or light rocks in dark areas
- **Logs:** Large and very visible, especially dead wood or green branches on light ground
- **Bright fabric or tarps:** Orange is the most visible color to aircraft
- **Reflection:** Arrange shiny material (aluminum foil, CDs, water) to catch light
- **Your body:** In open terrain, arranging your body or arms in these patterns is possible (though less precise)

**Location matters:**
- Place signals in open areas where they're visible from above
- Avoid dense forest (signals won't be seen through trees)
- Clear vegetation around signals to increase contrast
- Use high ground (hills, ridges) when possible

**Maintenance:**
- If you stay in one location, refresh signals daily so they're clear and visible
- If adding multiple signals, space them apart so they don't confuse the pattern

## Combining Signals

Don't rely on a single signal:
- Make a large X with rocks AND create a signal mirror flash AND light a fire with smoke
- The more signals you create, the more likely rescue teams spot you
- Different signals serve different purposes: ground markings are visible from high altitude, fires are visible through haze, mirror signals are bright and distinct

## What Happens When Aircraft Spot Your Signal

1. **The pilot reports your location** to the rescue coordination center
2. **Your coordinates are transmitted** to ground rescue teams
3. **A rescue team is dispatched** to your location
4. **You remain calm, maintain signals, and stay where you are** so rescue teams can find you

This is why **staying put** is so important. If you wander away from your signals, rescuers can't find you.

## Practicing These Signals

Before needing them, practice:

1. **In your backyard:** Use rocks or rope to make each signal at least 20 feet across
2. **From a distance:** Walk away and see if the pattern is recognizable
3. **From above:** If possible, have someone photograph the signals from a height (hill, building) to see how they look from above
4. **Memorizing meanings:** Quiz yourself on what each signal means

## Remembering the Five Signals

A helpful way to remember:
- **X = Need help** (distress)
- **V = Help needed** (assistance, but less urgent)
- **Triangle = OK to land** (safe zone)
- **Arrow = This direction** (direction of travel)
- **SOS = Emergency** (life or death)

## Important Note: Honesty in Signaling

**Never make false distress signals.** Using X, SOS, or emergency signals falsely wastes rescue resources and puts real emergencies at risk. Use these signals only if you genuinely need help.
