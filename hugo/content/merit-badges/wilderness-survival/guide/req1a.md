{{< drg/requirement number="1a" >}}
Show or explain first aid for, and prevention of, injuries and conditions that could occur in backcountry settings, including: (1) Dehydration, (2) Heatstroke, (3) Hypothermia, (4) Shock, (5) Blisters, (6) Eye injuries, (7) Ankle and knee sprains, (8) Bug bites of chiggers, ticks, mosquitoes, and biting gnats, (9) Bee stings, (10) Bites of spiders, (11) Sting of a scorpion, (12) Bite of a wild mammal, (13) Bite of a venomous snake.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This requirement covers the most common medical emergencies that happen in backcountry environments — far from ambulances, hospitals, or even a cell signal. Your counselor will want you to explain both **prevention** (how you avoid each condition) and **first aid** (what you do when it happens anyway). This page covers all 13 topics.

## Environmental Injuries

### 1. Dehydration

Your body is about 60% water. Lose too much and your blood thickens, your kidneys strain, and your brain stops working right — long before you feel truly thirsty. In the backcountry, you can lose a liter of sweat per hour during strenuous activity.

**Prevention:** Drink before you're thirsty. A general rule is 0.5 liters per hour of activity in moderate conditions — more in heat or at altitude. Don't wait until your mouth is dry.

**Signs:** Dark yellow urine, headache, dizziness, dry mouth, fatigue, muscle cramps.

**First aid:** Stop and rest in shade. Drink small amounts of water — about 4–8 oz every 15 minutes. Rehydration is slow; don't chug large amounts, which can cause nausea. If the person is unconscious, do not give fluids. Get help immediately.

### 2. Heatstroke

Heatstroke is a life-threatening emergency, not just a bad case of being hot. When your body's core temperature climbs above 104°F, it loses the ability to cool itself. This is different from heat exhaustion — heatstroke is when the system fails completely.

**Prevention:** Hike in the early morning and late afternoon during summer. Wear light, breathable clothing. Stay hydrated. Take regular breaks in the shade.

**Signs of heat exhaustion** (warning stage): Heavy sweating, cool/pale/moist skin, weakness, fast/weak pulse, nausea.

**Signs of heatstroke** (emergency): Hot/red/dry OR damp skin, confusion or altered mental state, fast/strong pulse, loss of consciousness.

**First aid for heatstroke:** Call for emergency help immediately. Move the person to shade. Cool them as rapidly as possible — wet their skin with cool water, fan them, apply ice packs to the neck, armpits, and groin if available. Do not give fluids to a confused or unconscious person.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Heatstroke can kill in minutes. Any time someone is confused or unresponsive in hot conditions, treat it as heatstroke and activate emergency services (personal locator beacon, whistle signals, or send a runner for help). Do not leave the victim alone.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### 3. Hypothermia

Hypothermia occurs when your core body temperature drops below 95°F. It kills more outdoor recreationists than any other environmental hazard. The tricky part: it can happen at 50°F, not just in blizzards.

**Prevention:** Wear moisture-wicking base layers and avoid cotton ("cotton kills"). Dress in layers. Stay dry — wet clothing loses 90% of its insulating value. Eat and drink regularly, as calories generate body heat.

**Signs:** Know the "umbles" — stumbles, mumbles, fumbles, and grumbles. Also watch for shivering (early), then paradoxically stopping shivering (severe), confusion, slow/slurred speech, loss of coordination.

**First aid:** Get the person out of the wind and wet. Remove wet clothing and replace with dry. Apply gentle warmth to the core — armpits, neck, and groin. Warm, sweet drinks if conscious and able to swallow. Do not rub or massage cold limbs vigorously; this can send cold blood back to the heart. Severe hypothermia requires evacuation.

{{< drg/tip >}}
A hypothermic person may insist they're fine — or even feel suddenly warm (a dangerous sign called "paradoxical undressing"). Trust the signs, not what they say.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### 4. Shock

Shock is when the body's circulatory system fails to deliver enough oxygen to vital organs. It can follow any serious injury — blood loss, severe allergic reaction, dehydration, heat injury, or spinal trauma.

**Prevention:** Prevent injuries through careful travel and proper gear. Treat bleeding aggressively and early.

**Signs:** Pale/cold/clammy skin, rapid/weak pulse, shallow breathing, confusion, nausea, extreme thirst.

**First aid:** Have the person lie down with their feet elevated about 12 inches (unless a head/spinal injury is suspected). Keep them warm with a space blanket or dry clothing. Do not give food or water. Calm and reassure them. Seek emergency help.

## Physical Injuries

### 5. Blisters

A blister the size of a quarter can end a 20-mile hike at mile three. Blisters form when friction separates layers of skin, letting fluid pool underneath.

**Prevention:** Break in boots before any major trip. Wear moisture-wicking liner socks plus hiking socks. Change socks when they get damp. Address "hot spots" (areas of rubbing) immediately before they become blisters.

**First aid for hot spots:** Clean, dry moleskin or foam padding with a hole cut out to surround (not cover) the area, reducing pressure.

**First aid for blisters:** In the field, leave intact blisters covered with moleskin donuts if you must keep moving. A large, painful blister that has to be drained should be done with a sterilized needle at the blister's edge — never puncture the center — then cover with sterile gauze. Watch for infection (redness spreading beyond the blister, warm skin, discharge).

### 6. Eye Injuries

Eyes are especially vulnerable in backcountry settings — UV light off snow or water can cause sunburned corneas (photokeratitis), and debris from wind, branches, or insects can scratch or embed in the eye.

**Prevention:** Wear wraparound UV-protective sunglasses. In windy or dusty conditions, goggles offer better protection.

**First aid for a foreign object:** Do NOT rub the eye. Flush gently with clean water — tilt the head with the affected eye down and let water flow from the inner corner outward. If the object is visible and loose, you can try using the corner of a clean cloth to gently lift it. If the object is embedded or the eye is severely irritated, cover it loosely and evacuate.

**First aid for photokeratitis (snow blindness):** Rest in a dark environment. Cool, damp cloths over closed eyes can relieve pain. Recovery usually takes 12–24 hours. Prevention is the cure — never travel in bright snow or water glare without eye protection.

### 7. Ankle and Knee Sprains

Sprains happen when ligaments stretch or tear. They're the most common trail injury, usually from twisting on uneven terrain.

**Prevention:** Wear ankle-supportive footwear on rocky terrain. Use trekking poles on steep descents. Watch your footing — most sprains happen when hikers are tired.

**First aid — Remember RICE:**
- **R**est: Stop putting weight on the joint
- **I**ce: Apply cold pack or cold water for 20 minutes at a time
- **C**ompression: Wrap with an elastic bandage to reduce swelling
- **E**levation: Keep the joint raised above heart level when resting

If the person cannot bear any weight at all, suspect a fracture, not just a sprain, and arrange for evacuation.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Can They Walk?" >}}
When assessing a sprain in the field, your key question is: can this person walk?

- **Minor sprain:** Some pain but can walk. Wrap with elastic bandage and use a trekking pole for support. Continue cautiously.
- **Moderate sprain:** Walking is painful but possible. Tape aggressively and reduce mileage. Monitor for worsening.
- **Severe or possible fracture:** Cannot bear weight. Set up camp or improvise a litter. Activate rescue if far from the trailhead.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

## Bites and Stings

{{< drg/image src="images/biting-insects-grid.avif" alt="Four illustrated close-ups of common biting insects — chigger, tick, mosquito, and biting gnat — each labeled" >}}

### 8. Bug Bites: Chiggers, Ticks, Mosquitoes, and Biting Gnats

**Chiggers:** Microscopic mite larvae found in grass and brush. They don't burrow into skin — they inject enzymes that dissolve skin cells, causing intense itching red welts.
- *Prevention:* Wear long sleeves and pants, apply permethrin to clothing.
- *First aid:* Wash affected areas with soap and water. Anti-itch creams and antihistamines reduce symptoms. Avoid scratching to prevent infection.

**Ticks:** Can transmit Lyme disease, Rocky Mountain spotted fever, and other illnesses. Embedded ticks should be removed promptly — Lyme disease transmission requires 24–48 hours of attachment.
- *Prevention:* Tuck pants into socks, use DEET or permethrin repellent, check for ticks after outdoor time.
- *First aid:* Grip the tick as close to the skin as possible with fine-tipped tweezers, pull straight out slowly and steadily. Do not twist or use heat/petroleum jelly. Save the tick in a sealed bag for ID. Wash the bite area with alcohol. Monitor for rash or flu symptoms over the next weeks.

**Mosquitoes:** Besides itchy bites, mosquitoes transmit West Nile virus, Zika, malaria (in some regions), and other diseases.
- *Prevention:* Use DEET, picaridin, or other EPA-registered repellents. Wear light-colored long sleeves in the evening when mosquitoes are most active.
- *First aid:* Hydrocortisone cream or calamine lotion for itching. Antihistamines if reaction is severe.

**Biting Gnats (No-See-Ums):** Extremely small — 1–3mm — but their bites punch far above their size. Common near water in evening hours.
- *Prevention:* Fine-mesh head nets, tightly woven clothing, avoiding dawn/dusk peak activity near water.
- *First aid:* Same as mosquito bites — anti-itch cream and antihistamines.

### 9. Bee Stings

Most bee stings cause localized pain and swelling — uncomfortable, but not dangerous. The serious risk is anaphylaxis (a severe allergic reaction) in people who are sensitive.

**Prevention:** Avoid floral-scented products in the backcountry. Walk away slowly and calmly from bee swarms rather than swatting.

**First aid for normal sting:** Remove the stinger by scraping it out sideways with a credit card or fingernail — do not pinch it, which squeezes more venom in. Wash the site, apply ice or cold water, use antihistamines or hydrocortisone cream if available.

**First aid for anaphylaxis:** Signs include hives, throat swelling, difficulty breathing, rapid pulse, and dizziness. If the person has an epinephrine auto-injector (EpiPen), use it immediately per instructions. Lay the person down with legs elevated unless breathing is labored. Call for emergency help. A second dose of epinephrine may be needed after 10–15 minutes if symptoms return.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Honey bees can only sting once — their barbed stingers get stuck. But a single bee releases a pheromone when it stings that signals other bees to attack the same target. Moving quickly away from the area is more important than stopping to remove the stinger immediately.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

### 10. Spider Bites

Most spider bites in North America cause only minor local irritation. Two species, however, are medically significant:

**Black Widow:** Found throughout the continental U.S. in dark, dry spaces (woodpiles, outhouses, under rocks). The bite itself may not be felt, but 30–60 minutes later causes severe muscle cramps, especially in the abdomen.
- *First aid:* Wash the bite, apply ice. Get to a hospital — antivenin exists and is very effective.

**Brown Recluse:** Found primarily in the south-central U.S. The bite can cause a slow-healing necrotic wound (tissue destruction). Symptoms may not appear for 3–8 hours.
- *First aid:* Clean the wound, apply ice, immobilize the bitten area, and seek medical attention. There is no antivenin for brown recluse; treatment is supportive.

**General spider bite first aid:** Clean the area, apply a cold pack, take antihistamines if available. Watch for systemic symptoms (fever, spreading redness, nausea, severe pain) and seek medical help if they develop.

### 11. Scorpion Stings

About 30 species of scorpions live in the United States, mostly in the desert Southwest. Only the bark scorpion (Centruroides sculpturatus) of Arizona and surrounding states is considered medically dangerous.

**Prevention:** Shake out boots and clothing before putting them on. Check sleeping bags and gear. Use a headlamp — scorpions glow under ultraviolet light.

**First aid:** Wash the sting site with soap and water. Apply a cold pack. Most stings cause local pain and swelling, similar to a bee sting. For suspected bark scorpion stings, especially in children, seek immediate medical attention — symptoms can include numbness, difficulty swallowing, and abnormal eye movements.

### 12. Bite of a Wild Mammal

Wild mammal bites — from raccoons, foxes, skunks, bats, or even squirrels — carry infection risk and the possibility of rabies. Rabies is nearly always fatal once symptoms appear, but the vaccine is highly effective if given promptly after exposure.

**Prevention:** Never approach, feed, or handle wild animals. A wild animal behaving strangely (active during daylight, showing no fear of humans) may be rabid.

**First aid:** Wash the wound aggressively with soap and running water for 5 full minutes — this is the most important step. Apply antibiotic ointment and a clean bandage. Get to medical care as quickly as possible. Post-exposure rabies prophylaxis (a series of shots) must begin promptly.

**Reporting:** Report the bite to local wildlife or health authorities. Capture or identification of the animal can help determine whether rabies treatment is necessary.

### 13. Bite of a Venomous Snake

In the United States, venomous snakes include rattlesnakes, copperheads, cottonmouths (water moccasins), and coral snakes. Most bites happen when people accidentally step on or attempt to handle a snake.

**Prevention:** Watch where you step and place your hands, especially around rocks, logs, and brush. Don't reach into places you can't see. Wear boots and long pants.

**Signs of envenomation:** Immediate burning pain, swelling and bruising spreading from the bite, nausea, dizziness, difficulty breathing or swallowing (coral snake bites may show delayed systemic symptoms).

**First aid — DO:**
- Keep the victim calm and still — movement spreads venom
- Immobilize the bitten limb at or below heart level
- Remove rings, watches, and tight clothing near the bite before swelling starts
- Get to a hospital as fast as possible — antivenin is the definitive treatment

**First aid — DO NOT:**
- Cut the bite or try to suck out venom (doesn't work, causes infection)
- Apply a tourniquet or ice (both cause additional tissue damage)
- Give alcohol
- Use electric shock (this does not help and can hurt)
