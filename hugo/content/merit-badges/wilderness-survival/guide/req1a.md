Show or explain first aid for, and prevention of, injuries and conditions that could occur in backcountry settings, including: dehydration, heatstroke, hypothermia, shock, blisters, eye injuries, ankle and knee sprains, bug bites (chiggers, ticks, mosquitoes, and biting gnats), bee stings, spider bites, scorpion stings, and bites of wild mammals and venomous snakes.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This requirement covers 13 serious conditions you might encounter in the wilderness. The good news: most of these are **preventable**. The goal here isn't to memorize every symptom, but to recognize the early warning signs and know what to do immediately. Let's walk through each one.

## Heat-Related Emergencies

**Dehydration** happens when your body loses more water than it takes in. In hot weather, you can lose a liter of sweat per hour without realizing it. Early signs are thirst, dark yellow urine, dry mouth, and dizziness. Left untreated, dehydration leads to heatstroke.

**Prevention:** Drink before you're thirsty. On a hot day, aim for 2–3 liters of water over 4–5 hours of activity. Drink even in cool weather—you can dehydrate without realizing it.

**Treatment:** Stop activity, sit in shade, and drink water slowly. If you can add electrolytes (salt tabs, sports drink), that helps your body retain the water. Recovery usually takes 30–60 minutes.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Carry a water bottle even on short hikes. If you forget it, know where streams are and bring a water purification method (we'll cover this in Req 8).
{{< /drg/tip >}}

**Heatstroke** is more serious. Your body's cooling system fails, and core temperature climbs dangerously. Signs include confusion, red and dry skin (not sweating), rapid heartbeat, and collapse. Heatstroke is a medical emergency.

**Prevention:** Acclimatize slowly to heat. Wear light-colored, breathable clothing. Take frequent shade breaks. Drink regularly.

**Treatment:** Cool the person immediately. Pour cool water over their head, neck, and arms. Get them to shade. Call for help. Do not give them water if they're confused (risk of aspiration).

## Cold-Related Emergencies

**Hypothermia** is when your core body temperature drops below 95°F. You lose heat faster than your body can generate it, usually in cold and wet conditions (wind makes it worse). Early signs are shivering, slurred speech, clumsiness, and confusion. This is why it's called "the umbles"—victims get the "fumbles," "mumbles," "stumbles," and finally the "grumbles" (irritability).

**Prevention:** Stay dry. Wear layers so you can adjust. Wind and water are your enemies—protect yourself from both. In cold weather, eat and drink regularly to fuel your body's heat production.

**Treatment:** Get the person to shelter and into dry clothing. Warm them slowly with body heat or blankets. Give them warm (not hot) drinks if they're conscious. Rapid rewarming can cause heart problems, so go slow.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Hypothermia can happen in temperatures above freezing—even at 50°F if you're wet and windy. This is why rain gear is part of your Scout Essentials.

## Shock

Shock isn't just "being startled." In medical terms, shock is when your circulation system fails to deliver enough oxygen to your organs. Causes include heavy bleeding, severe pain, severe allergic reactions, or intense fear. Signs are weakness, rapid pulse, pale and clammy skin, nausea, and confusion.

**Treatment:** Lay the person flat with legs elevated (unless they have a head or spine injury). Keep them warm and calm. Reassure them. Get medical help immediately. Do not give them food or water (they might vomit and choke).

## Musculoskeletal & Skin Injuries

**Blisters** form when friction causes the outer layer of skin to separate from deeper layers, and fluid pools inside. Prevention is easier than treatment: wear **proper footwear**, keep socks dry, and **stop early if you feel a hot spot** on your heel or toes. A hot spot is blistering beginning—apply tape or a blister pad immediately and the blister often doesn't form.

**Treatment:** If you get a blister, don't pop it. Cover it with a blister pad and adjust your footwear. If it does pop, clean it and apply antibiotic ointment and a sterile bandage.

{{< drg/tip >}}
On a long hike, carry blister prevention supplies: tape, moleskin, or specialized blister pads. They weigh almost nothing.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

**Eye injuries** can range from a scratched cornea (something in your eye) to a chemical burn. If something is lodged in your eye, resist the urge to rub. Flush it with water. For scratches, close the eye, apply a cool compress, and avoid light. For chemical burns, flush immediately with copious amounts of clean water for 15 minutes. All significant eye injuries need medical evaluation.

**Ankle and knee sprains** happen when ligaments tear. Signs are immediate pain, swelling, and difficulty walking. Immediately apply the **RICE protocol**: Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation. If you can't walk, don't try. Splint it and call for help. Most sprains heal with rest at home, but if you're out on a trail, getting out safely is the priority.

## Insect Bites & Stings

**Chiggers, ticks, mosquitoes, and gnats** all bite, but they differ in what they transmit and how much they itch. Chiggers are mite larvae that burrow into skin—the itch is intense. Ticks can carry Lyme disease and tick-borne encephalitis. Mosquitoes can carry disease. Gnats are mainly just annoying.

**Prevention:** Wear light-colored clothing, tuck pants into socks, use insect repellent (DEET or picaridin). Check yourself daily for ticks, and remove them immediately by grasping near the head with tweezers and pulling straight out (don't twist).

**Treatment:** For chiggers and gnats, resist scratching (infection risk). Calamine lotion or hydrocortisone cream helps. For tick bites, pull the tick out, wash the area, and watch for a bullseye rash (sign of Lyme disease—see a doctor).

{{< drg/external-link
    title="Preventing and Treating Tick Bites"
    url="https://www.aad.org/public/everyday-care/injured-skin/bites/remove-a-tick" >}}

**Bee stings** leave a stinger that you should remove by scraping (not squeezing, which injects more venom). Apply a cold compress and antihistamine. For bee allergies, an epinephrine auto-injector (EpiPen) is essential—carry it and know how to use it.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
If someone has a known bee allergy and gets stung, use an EpiPen immediately and call for help. Do not assume they'll be okay.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

**Spider bites** (including black widows and brown recluse) can cause pain, swelling, and in rare cases, serious systemic effects. Clean the bite, apply ice, and take pain reliever. Watch for spreading redness or systemic symptoms (muscle pain, fever) and seek medical advice. Most spider bites are harmless, but if you're uncertain of the spider species, it's better to err on the side of caution.

**Scorpion stings** vary by species. Most North American scorpions cause local pain only, treatable with ice and pain relievers. Arizona bark scorpions can cause severe neurological symptoms and require medical attention. If stung in scorpion country, monitor for neurological signs (tingling, muscle twitching) and seek help if present.

## Wild Mammal & Snake Bites

**Mammal bites** (from raccoons, foxes, bats, etc.) risk infection and rabies. If bitten, wash immediately with soap and water, apply antibiotic ointment, and get medical help. Rabies is nearly 100% fatal once symptoms appear, so post-exposure prophylaxis (vaccine series) is critical. Never approach a wild animal, especially one that seems sick or unusually bold.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
If a wild animal bites you, seek medical care immediately, even if the bite seems minor. Rabies is rare but nearly always fatal once symptoms appear.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

**Venomous snake bites** are also rare but serious. In North America, venomous snakes include rattlesnakes (most common), cottonmouths, and coral snakes. If bitten:
1. Stay calm and immobilize the bitten limb
2. Remove any rings or bracelets (swelling follows)
3. Get medical help immediately
4. Do NOT suck out venom or apply tourniquets (these cause more damage than they help)

**Prevention:** Wear boots in snake country, watch where you step, and give any snake plenty of space.

## The Mindset

Every single one of these conditions is **preventable** with the right gear, knowledge, and attention. Stay aware of weather and temperature changes. Keep your first-aid kit stocked. Dress for the conditions you might encounter, not just the conditions that exist right now. And most importantly: **know your limits**. If the trail is too steep, the weather is turning, or you're running low on water, turn back. Living to hike another day beats pushing through and becoming a rescue case.
