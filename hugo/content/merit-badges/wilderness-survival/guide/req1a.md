A twisted ankle on a day hike is inconvenient. The same injury three miles into the backcountry with no cell signal becomes a survival situation. This requirement covers thirteen injuries and conditions you might face when help is far away. For each one, you need to know two things: how to prevent it and how to treat it in the field.

## Heat-Related Conditions

### Dehydration

Your body loses water through sweat, breathing, and normal metabolism. In the backcountry — where you're hiking, climbing, and working harder than usual — that loss accelerates. Dehydration sneaks up on you because thirst is a *late* indicator. By the time you feel thirsty, you've already lost 1–2% of your body weight in water.

**Prevention:** Drink water regularly — at least half a liter per hour during moderate activity. Don't wait until you're thirsty. Monitor urine color: pale yellow means you're hydrated, dark yellow or amber means drink more immediately.

**First Aid:** Move the person to shade, have them sip water slowly (not gulp), and rest. If they show signs of confusion or stop sweating, the situation has progressed to heatstroke — a medical emergency.

### Heatstroke

Heatstroke happens when your body's cooling system fails and your core temperature rises above 104°F. It is a **life-threatening emergency**. The brain, kidneys, and heart can suffer permanent damage.

**Prevention:** Stay hydrated, take breaks in shade, wear light-colored and loose clothing, and avoid strenuous activity during the hottest part of the day (10 AM – 4 PM).

**First Aid:** Call for emergency help immediately. Move the person to shade, remove excess clothing, and cool them aggressively — pour water on them, fan them, apply cold packs to the neck, armpits, and groin. Heatstroke victims may stop sweating entirely, which is a critical warning sign.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Heatstroke can kill within minutes. If someone stops sweating, becomes confused, or loses consciousness on a hot day, treat it as a medical emergency. Cool them immediately and get help.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Cold-Related Conditions

### Hypothermia

Hypothermia occurs when your body loses heat faster than it can produce it, dropping your core temperature below 95°F. It doesn't have to be freezing — wet, windy conditions at 50°F can cause hypothermia faster than dry cold at 20°F. Watch for the "umbles": stumbling, mumbling, fumbling, and grumbling.

**Prevention:** Wear layers (moisture-wicking base, insulating middle, waterproof outer). Stay dry — change out of wet clothes immediately. Eat high-calorie foods to fuel your body's heat engine. Keep moving, but don't exhaust yourself.

**First Aid:** Get the person out of the wind and wet. Remove wet clothing and replace with dry layers. Warm them gradually — warm drinks (no caffeine or alcohol), body-to-body heat in a sleeping bag, and chemical heat packs in armpits and groin. Never rub frostbitten skin or use direct heat sources.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Shivering is actually a good sign — it means your body is still actively trying to warm itself. When shivering stops in a cold person, they've entered severe hypothermia and need emergency medical care immediately.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

### Shock

Shock is a medical condition where the body's organs don't get enough blood flow. In survival situations, it can result from injuries, blood loss, severe burns, dehydration, or even extreme fear. The body redirects blood to vital organs, leaving the skin pale, cool, and clammy.

**Prevention:** Treat injuries promptly, keep injured people warm and calm, and manage pain.

**First Aid:** Have the person lie down with legs elevated about 12 inches (unless a head, neck, or back injury is suspected). Keep them warm with blankets or extra clothing. Do not give food or water if you suspect internal injuries. Monitor breathing and consciousness.

## Soft Tissue Injuries

### Blisters

A blister the size of a dime can end your hike. Friction between your skin and your boot creates a fluid-filled pocket that hurts with every step. In the backcountry, an untreated blister can become infected and turn a minor problem into a serious one.

**Prevention:** Wear broken-in boots with moisture-wicking socks. Apply moleskin or athletic tape to known hot spots *before* they blister. Change into dry socks at rest stops.

**First Aid:** Clean the area with antiseptic. If the blister is small and intact, protect it with moleskin cut in a donut shape around the blister. If it's large and painful, sterilize a needle, drain the fluid from the edge, apply antibiotic ointment, and cover with a bandage. Never tear off the skin — it protects the tissue underneath.

### Eye Injuries

Branches, dust, insects, and UV radiation can all cause eye injuries in the backcountry. A scratched cornea is extremely painful and can impair your ability to navigate.

**Prevention:** Wear sunglasses or safety glasses when bushwhacking. Be aware of branches at eye level when following someone on a trail.

**First Aid:** For foreign objects, flush the eye with clean water. Don't rub it. For chemical exposure, flush continuously for at least 15 minutes. For a puncture wound, do not remove the object — stabilize it and get to medical care. Cover the injured eye and the uninjured eye (both eyes move together, so covering both reduces movement).

### Ankle and Knee Sprains

Uneven terrain, loose rocks, and tree roots make sprains one of the most common backcountry injuries. A sprained ankle can make self-evacuation impossible.

**Prevention:** Wear supportive footwear. Watch your footing, especially when tired. Use trekking poles on rough terrain — they reduce the force on your knees by up to 25%.

**First Aid:** Remember RICE: Rest, Ice (use cold stream water or a cold pack), Compression (wrap with an elastic bandage), Elevation. If the person cannot bear weight, you may need to splint the ankle and arrange for evacuation.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Carry an elastic bandage in your first aid kit — it works for sprains, compression bandages, and even as an improvised sling. One item, many uses.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Bites and Stings

### Bug Bites: Chiggers, Ticks, Mosquitoes, and Biting Gnats

Each of these pests delivers a different kind of misery, but prevention strategies overlap significantly.

**Chiggers** are tiny mites that burrow into skin folds and cause intense itching. They don't actually burrow *into* your skin — they inject enzymes that dissolve skin cells, then feed on the liquid. Tuck pants into socks and apply insect repellent with DEET to exposed skin.

**Ticks** are dangerous because they can transmit diseases like Lyme disease and Rocky Mountain spotted fever. Check your entire body after every outing. Use fine-tipped tweezers to remove ticks — grasp as close to the skin as possible and pull straight out with steady pressure. Don't twist or squeeze the body.

**Mosquitoes** spread diseases including West Nile virus and Zika. Wear long sleeves at dawn and dusk when they're most active. Apply DEET-based repellent.

**Biting gnats** (also called no-see-ums) are tiny enough to pass through standard window screens. They leave painful, itchy welts. Fine-mesh head nets and permethrin-treated clothing help.

### Bee Stings

A single bee sting is painful but rarely dangerous — unless the person is allergic. Allergic reactions (anaphylaxis) can be life-threatening and require immediate epinephrine.

**Prevention:** Avoid bright colors and sweet-smelling products in bee territory. Don't swat at bees — move away calmly.

**First Aid:** Scrape the stinger out sideways with a credit card or fingernail (don't squeeze with tweezers — that injects more venom). Clean the area, apply a cold pack, and take an antihistamine. If the person shows signs of anaphylaxis (swelling of the throat, difficulty breathing, rapid pulse), use an epinephrine auto-injector if available and call for emergency help.

### Spider Bites

Two spiders in North America deliver medically significant bites: the **black widow** and the **brown recluse**.

**Black widows** are shiny black with a red hourglass marking on their abdomen. Their venom affects the nervous system, causing muscle cramps, abdominal pain, and sweating.

**Brown recluses** are tan-brown with a violin-shaped marking on their back. Their venom destroys tissue around the bite, creating a slow-healing ulcer.

**Prevention:** Shake out boots, gloves, and clothing before putting them on. Check under logs and rocks before reaching underneath.

**First Aid:** Clean the bite, apply a cold pack, and seek medical attention. Try to identify or photograph the spider if you can do so safely. Keep the affected limb below heart level.

### Scorpion Stings

Scorpion stings are painful but rarely life-threatening in the US. The **bark scorpion** of the Southwest is the most venomous North American species.

**Prevention:** Shake out boots and sleeping bags. Use a UV flashlight at night — scorpions glow under ultraviolet light.

**First Aid:** Clean the area, apply a cold pack, and manage pain. Seek medical attention if symptoms include numbness, difficulty breathing, or muscle twitching.

### Wild Mammal Bites

Any wild mammal bite carries the risk of rabies — a virus that is almost always fatal once symptoms appear. Common culprits include raccoons, skunks, bats, foxes, and coyotes. A wild animal that approaches humans or acts unusually tame may be rabid.

**Prevention:** Never approach, feed, or corner wild animals. Store food properly to avoid attracting them to your campsite.

**First Aid:** Wash the wound thoroughly with soap and clean water for at least five minutes. Apply antibiotic ointment and a clean bandage. Seek medical attention immediately — rabies post-exposure treatment must begin promptly.

### Venomous Snake Bites

Four types of venomous snakes live in the United States: rattlesnakes, copperheads, cottonmouths (water moccasins), and coral snakes. Most bites happen when people step on or reach near a snake they didn't see.

**Prevention:** Watch where you step and where you put your hands. Wear boots and long pants in snake country. Never pick up a snake — even a dead one can reflexively bite.

**First Aid:** Keep the person calm and still. Remove rings and watches before swelling starts. Keep the bitten limb at or below heart level. **Do not** cut the wound, suck out the venom, apply a tourniquet, or use ice. Get to a hospital for antivenom as quickly as possible.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Forget everything you've seen in movies about treating snakebites. Cutting, sucking, and tourniquets all make the injury worse. The only effective treatment for a venomous snakebite is antivenom administered at a hospital.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Bitten on the Trail" >}}
Your hiking buddy steps off the trail and feels a sharp pain in their ankle. They look down and see a snake slithering away. Here's what to do:

- **Stay calm.** Panic increases heart rate, which spreads venom faster.
- **Move away** from the snake to a safe distance.
- **Note the snake's appearance** if you can — color, pattern, head shape. A photo from a safe distance helps doctors identify the species.
- **Remove jewelry** from the affected limb before swelling begins.
- **Keep the limb still** and at or below heart level.
- **Head toward the trailhead** at a calm, steady pace. Carry the person if possible.
- **Call 911** as soon as you have signal, or send someone ahead for help.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

That covers thirteen injuries and conditions you need to know for the backcountry. Knowing what to watch for — and what to do — keeps a bad day from becoming a dangerous one.
