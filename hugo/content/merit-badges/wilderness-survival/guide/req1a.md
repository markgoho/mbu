This requirement covers the thirteen most common medical threats you'll encounter in the backcountry. Knowing how to recognize and respond to each one is the difference between a manageable situation and a life-threatening one. The conditions are grouped below by severity and type.

## Temperature-Related Emergencies

### Dehydration

Your body is about 60% water. Lose just 2% of that through sweat and exertion, and your performance and judgment start to decline. Lose 10%, and you're in serious trouble.

**Prevention:** Drink before you feel thirsty. Thirst is a late-stage signal. Aim for half a liter of water per hour of moderate hiking, more in heat. Monitor your urine color — pale yellow is good; dark yellow or amber means drink up.

**Signs:** Dry mouth, headache, dizziness, fatigue, muscle cramps. Dark urine.

**First aid:** Move to shade. Have the person drink small amounts of water steadily (not gulping). Rest until symptoms clear. If they can't keep fluids down or become confused, treat as a serious emergency and get help.

### Heatstroke

Heatstroke is a medical emergency. It happens when your body's cooling system shuts down and core temperature climbs above 104°F. Without fast treatment, organs fail.

**Prevention:** Avoid hiking in peak midday heat. Wear light, breathable clothing. Take breaks in shade. Hydrate consistently.

**Signs:** Hot, dry or sweaty skin; confusion; loss of consciousness; rapid strong pulse; high body temperature. Heat exhaustion comes first (heavy sweating, weakness, cool pale clammy skin) — treat that aggressively before it becomes heatstroke.

**First aid:** Call for emergency help immediately. Move the person to shade. Cool them by any means available: dousing with cool water, fanning, applying wet cloths to neck, armpits, and groin. Do not give fluids to someone who is unconscious or confused.

### Hypothermia

Hypothermia occurs when body core temperature drops below 95°F. In the backcountry, it can happen in temperatures as mild as 50°F when wind, rain, and wet clothing combine.

**Prevention:** Dress in layers, stay dry, eat and drink regularly, and never ignore shivering (your body's first defense against cold).

**Signs:** The "umbles" — stumbles, mumbles, fumbles, grumbles. Shivering that stops suddenly (a bad sign), confusion, slurred speech, loss of coordination.

**First aid:** Get the person out of the cold and wet. Remove wet clothing. Insulate from the ground up. Warm the core first (torso, neck, armpits, groin) — not the hands and feet. Give warm sweet liquids if conscious. For severe cases, handle gently and get emergency help — rough movement can trigger cardiac arrest.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Your body loses heat five times faster in water than in air at the same temperature. A swimmer in 60°F water can become hypothermic in as little as one hour, even though 60°F feels comfortable on a summer day.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

### Shock

Shock isn't just emotional upset — it's a life-threatening condition where the body's vital organs stop receiving enough blood flow. In the backcountry, it can follow serious injury, blood loss, severe dehydration, or allergic reaction.

**Signs:** Pale, cool, clammy skin; rapid weak pulse; rapid shallow breathing; confusion; dizziness or fainting when standing; nausea.

**First aid:** Have the person lie down. Unless they have a head, neck, or spine injury — or breathing difficulty — raise their legs about 12 inches. Keep them warm with blankets or extra clothing. Don't give food or water. Treat the underlying cause if possible (stop bleeding, etc.). Get emergency help.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Shock can develop after any serious injury, even if the person initially seems okay. Anyone who has experienced major trauma, significant blood loss, or a severe allergic reaction should be monitored closely and seen by medical professionals as soon as possible.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Injuries from Terrain and Equipment

### Blisters

A blister the size of a quarter can turn a confident hiker into a limping one within half a mile. Hot spots — the burning warning signal before a blister forms — should be addressed immediately.

**Prevention:** Wear properly fitted boots broken in before long hikes. Use moisture-wicking socks and change them if they get wet. Apply moleskin or blister tape to problem areas before hiking.

**First aid:** If caught early (hot spot only), cover with moleskin cut into a donut shape around the area. If a blister has formed, don't pop it unless it's very large and painful — the skin covering it protects against infection. If you must drain it, use a sterile needle, pierce at the edge, drain, leave the skin in place, and cover with a bandage.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Pack a small blister kit in your daypack: moleskin, athletic tape, a sterile needle, and antiseptic. The few ounces it weighs are worth it when you're on mile 15 of a trail.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Eye Injuries

Backcountry eye injuries usually involve foreign objects (dust, debris, insects), UV damage (snow blindness), or scratches from branches.

**Prevention:** Wear sunglasses on snow or in bright sun. Be aware of branch heights on trail.

**First aid:** Don't rub. If a small particle is in the eye, try blinking rapidly or flushing with clean water. You can also gently pull the upper lid over the lower lid — the lower lashes may sweep debris out. If an object is embedded or stuck, cover both eyes (covering one causes the other to move in sympathy) and get medical help. Never try to remove an embedded object yourself.

For snow blindness (UV damage causing intense eye pain, usually appearing hours after exposure): apply cold compresses, rest in darkness, and cover eyes.

### Ankle and Knee Sprains

Ankle sprains are the most common backcountry injury. A single step onto uneven terrain and a sideways roll is all it takes.

**Prevention:** Wear boots or shoes with ankle support. Use trekking poles. Watch the terrain ahead.

**First aid:** Use the R.I.C.E. method:

- **Rest** — Stop putting weight on it immediately.
- **Ice** — Apply cold (a cold stream, snow in a bag, or a cold-soaked cloth) for 20 minutes at a time.
- **Compression** — Wrap with an elastic bandage, snug but not tight enough to cut off circulation.
- **Elevation** — Prop the limb above heart level when resting.

For backcountry evacuation decisions: if the person can bear weight and walk with support, slow evacuation may be possible. If they cannot bear weight, or there's significant deformity suggesting a fracture, get help.

## Bug Bites and Stings

{{< drg/image src="images/biting-insects-grid.avif" alt="Scientific illustrations of four biting insects: a chigger mite, a deer tick, a mosquito, and a biting gnat, each labeled" >}}

### Chiggers, Ticks, Mosquitoes, and Biting Gnats

**Chiggers** are microscopic mite larvae that cause intense itching by injecting digestive fluid into your skin. They don't burrow — they bite and fall off. Treatment: wash the area thoroughly with soap and water. Apply anti-itch cream. Scratching introduces infection.

**Ticks** require the most serious attention because they can transmit Lyme disease and other illnesses. After any time in tall grass or woods, do a full-body tick check. Remove attached ticks with fine-tipped tweezers: grasp as close to the skin surface as possible and pull straight out with steady pressure. Don't twist, burn, or use petroleum jelly. Clean with antiseptic. Watch the bite site for the characteristic bullseye rash (Lyme disease indicator) and see a doctor if it appears.

**Mosquitoes** cause itchy bites and can carry disease in some regions. Prevention: use EPA-registered repellent (DEET, picaridin, or oil of lemon eucalyptus). Treatment: ice, hydrocortisone cream, antihistamine for severe reactions.

**Biting gnats:** Treat like mosquito bites. Prevention is easier than treatment — long sleeves and repellent.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
A tick must typically be attached for 36–48 hours before it can transmit Lyme disease. This is why the daily tick check after time outdoors is so important — catching a tick within 24 hours dramatically reduces your risk.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

### Bee Stings

Most bee stings cause local pain and swelling that resolve within hours. The real danger is anaphylaxis (severe allergic reaction) in people who are allergic.

**First aid:** Remove the stinger by scraping sideways with a fingernail or card edge (don't squeeze it — that injects more venom). Wash the area. Apply ice. Take an antihistamine for swelling.

**Anaphylaxis signs:** Hives spreading from the sting site, swelling of lips or tongue, difficulty breathing, dizziness, or fainting. This is a medical emergency. If the person carries an epinephrine auto-injector (EpiPen), use it immediately. Call for emergency help.

### Spider Bites

Two spiders in North America deserve special attention: the **black widow** (black, glossy, with a red hourglass marking on the abdomen) and the **brown recluse** (brown, violin-shaped marking on the back).

**Black widow symptoms:** Painful bite followed by muscle cramps, abdominal pain, nausea, sweating. Not usually fatal in healthy adults but requires medical evaluation.

**Brown recluse symptoms:** The bite may be painless at first. Over hours, a necrotic (tissue-killing) lesion can develop. This requires medical treatment.

**First aid for any spider bite:** Keep calm, clean the bite, apply ice, elevate the limb. Try to note what the spider looked like. Get medical evaluation — especially for children, elderly, or anyone with concerning symptoms.

### Scorpion Sting

In North America, most scorpion stings cause local pain but aren't life-threatening. The exception is the bark scorpion (found in the southwestern U.S. and northern Mexico), whose sting can cause numbness, tingling spreading from the sting, difficulty swallowing, and rarely, more serious symptoms.

**First aid:** Clean the sting. Apply cold. Take pain reliever. Get medical evaluation promptly, especially in the Southwest, for children, or if symptoms spread beyond the sting site.

### Wild Mammal Bite

Any wild mammal bite — including bats, raccoons, foxes, and skunks — carries rabies risk. This is a medical emergency.

**First aid:** Wash the wound aggressively with soap and water for at least 5 minutes. Apply antiseptic. Get to a hospital or clinic immediately. If possible, report the encounter to local wildlife authorities. Rabies post-exposure prophylaxis (a series of shots) is very effective when given promptly — but the disease is almost always fatal once symptoms appear.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Animal Bite in the Backcountry" >}}
You're camping in bear country when a fellow Scout is bitten by a raccoon near camp.

- **Step 1:** Move everyone away from the raccoon — don't try to capture it.
- **Step 2:** Wash the wound immediately and thoroughly with soap and water.
- **Step 3:** Apply antiseptic from your first aid kit and bandage.
- **Step 4:** Activate your emergency communication device (PLB, satellite messenger, or cell service) and initiate evacuation — this is a priority medical situation.
- **Step 5:** Note the time, location, and description of the animal for health authorities.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

### Venomous Snake Bite

North America has about 20 venomous snake species, mostly rattlesnakes, copperheads, cottonmouths, and coral snakes. A large majority of venomous snake bites can be survived with prompt medical treatment.

**Prevention:** Watch where you step, especially on rocky terrain. Don't reach into crevices or log piles blindly. Give snakes space.

**First aid:** Keep calm. Immobilize the bitten limb below heart level. Remove rings and tight clothing before swelling begins. Get to emergency care as fast as possible — antivenom is the definitive treatment. Do NOT cut and suck the wound, apply a tourniquet, apply ice, or use a suction device. These methods don't work and can cause additional harm.
