{{< drg/requirement number="1a" >}}
Show or explain first aid for, and prevention of, injuries and conditions that could occur in backcountry settings, including:
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This requirement covers thirteen injuries and conditions you might face in the backcountry. For each one, you need to understand what causes it, how to recognize it, and how to treat it in the field — where a hospital might be hours or days away.

## Heat-Related Conditions

### Dehydration

Your body loses water constantly through sweat, breathing, and urination. In the backcountry, you are working harder than usual — hiking with a pack, building shelters, gathering firewood — and losing water faster. Dehydration starts before you feel thirsty.

**Warning signs:** Dark yellow urine, headache, dizziness, dry mouth, fatigue, and reduced urine output.

**Prevention:** Drink water at regular intervals, not just when you feel thirsty. A good rule is to drink about half a liter per hour during moderate activity. Bring more water than you think you need, and know where water sources are on your route.

**Treatment:** Move the person to shade and have them drink small sips of water — not gulps, which can cause vomiting. If they have electrolyte packets, add those to the water. Rest until symptoms improve.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Check your urine color throughout the day. Pale yellow means you are well hydrated. Dark yellow or amber means you need to drink more water immediately.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Heatstroke

Heatstroke is a life-threatening emergency. It happens when your body's cooling system fails and your core temperature climbs above 104°F. Unlike heat exhaustion (which is serious but manageable), heatstroke can kill.

**Warning signs:** Hot, red, dry skin (sweating may stop), confusion, loss of consciousness, rapid pulse, throbbing headache.

**Prevention:** Stay hydrated, take breaks in shade, avoid strenuous activity during the hottest part of the day (10 a.m. to 4 p.m.), and wear loose, light-colored clothing.

**Treatment:** This is a medical emergency — call for help immediately. While waiting, cool the person aggressively: move them to shade, remove excess clothing, pour cool water over their body, fan them, and place cold compresses on the neck, armpits, and groin. Do not give fluids if they are unconscious.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Heatstroke is the one heat condition that requires immediate evacuation. If someone stops sweating on a hot day and becomes confused, treat it as a life-threatening emergency. Cool first, transport second.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Cold-Related Conditions

### Hypothermia

Hypothermia occurs when your body loses heat faster than it can produce it, causing your core temperature to drop below 95°F. It does not require freezing temperatures — wet clothing, wind, and exhaustion can cause hypothermia at 50°F.

**Warning signs (the "umbles"):** Stumbles, mumbles, fumbles, grumbles. The person becomes clumsy, speech slurs, they cannot perform simple tasks with their hands, and their personality changes (often becoming irritable or apathetic).

**Prevention:** Stay dry. Wear layers that wick moisture. Change out of wet clothing immediately. Eat high-calorie foods regularly to fuel your body's heat production. Avoid exhaustion — a tired body generates less heat.

**Treatment:** Get the person out of wind and wet conditions. Remove wet clothing and replace with dry layers. Wrap them in a sleeping bag or emergency blanket. Give warm, sweet drinks if they are alert enough to swallow safely. Place warm water bottles (wrapped in cloth to prevent burns) near their core — armpits, neck, and groin. Handle gently; rough movement can trigger cardiac problems in severely hypothermic people.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
More hypothermia cases happen in temperatures between 30°F and 50°F than in sub-zero conditions. Why? Because people underestimate mild cold when they are wet and tired, and they do not take precautions until it is too late.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

### Shock

Shock is a medical condition where the body's organs are not getting enough blood flow. It can result from severe bleeding, allergic reactions, heatstroke, hypothermia, or psychological trauma. In a survival situation, shock can develop from injuries that seem manageable at first.

**Warning signs:** Pale, cool, clammy skin; rapid and weak pulse; rapid breathing; nausea or vomiting; weakness or faintness; enlarged pupils; confusion or anxiety.

**Prevention:** Treat injuries promptly and thoroughly. Keep injured people warm, calm, and hydrated.

**Treatment:** Have the person lie down. Elevate their legs about 12 inches (unless you suspect a spinal injury, head injury, or broken leg). Keep them warm with blankets or coats. Do not give food or drink. Monitor breathing and be ready to perform CPR. Get medical help as soon as possible.

## Foot & Joint Injuries

### Blisters

A blister seems minor — until it sidelines you three miles from camp. In the backcountry, a bad blister can turn a manageable hike into a painful ordeal or force an early evacuation.

**Prevention:** Wear broken-in boots that fit properly. Use moisture-wicking socks (never cotton). Apply moleskin or athletic tape to hot spots — areas of friction you notice before a blister forms. Keep feet dry by changing socks when they get sweaty.

**Treatment:** If a blister has not popped, protect it with a donut-shaped piece of moleskin (cut a hole in the center so the moleskin surrounds the blister without pressing on it). If it has popped, clean the area with antiseptic, apply antibiotic ointment, and cover with a bandage. Do not intentionally pop blisters in the backcountry — the risk of infection is too high when you cannot properly sterilize.

### Eye Injuries

Branches, dust, insects, and UV radiation can all injure your eyes in the wilderness. An eye injury far from medical care is especially concerning because infection can develop quickly.

**Prevention:** Wear sunglasses or safety glasses when bushwhacking. Be aware of branches at eye level, especially when following the person ahead of you on a trail.

**Treatment:** For debris in the eye, flush with clean water. Do not rub the eye. If an object is embedded, do not remove it — stabilize it with a cup or padding and evacuate. For UV damage (snow blindness), cover both eyes with a damp cloth and rest in a dark area.

### Ankle and Knee Sprains

Uneven terrain, loose rocks, and heavy packs make sprains one of the most common backcountry injuries. A sprained ankle on a remote trail can turn into a survival situation if you cannot walk out.

**Prevention:** Wear supportive footwear with good ankle support. Use trekking poles on rough terrain. Watch your footing, especially on wet rocks and loose gravel. Keep your pack weight reasonable.

**Treatment:** Use the **RICE** method: **R**est the injured joint, apply **I**ce (or cold stream water) wrapped in cloth, apply **C**ompression with an elastic bandage (snug but not tight enough to cut off circulation), and **E**levate the injured limb above heart level. An improvised splint using sticks and bandanas can stabilize a knee or ankle for hiking out.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Sprained Ankle on the Trail" >}}
You are four miles into a day hike when a member of your group steps on a loose rock and rolls their ankle. They cannot put weight on it.

- **Step one:** Help them sit down safely and remove the boot before swelling makes it impossible.
- **Step two:** Apply cold water from a nearby stream using a wet bandana. Wrap the ankle with an elastic bandage from your first aid kit.
- **Step three:** Create an improvised splint with two sturdy sticks and strips of cloth.
- **Step four:** Assess whether they can hobble out with help, or if you need to send for assistance. Two people can support a limping hiker using a "human crutch" — one on each side, arms over shoulders.
- **Step five:** If they cannot move, set up a sheltered rest spot and send the fastest hiker (with a buddy) back to the trailhead for help. Leave the injured person with water, food, and a whistle.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

## Bites and Stings

### Bug Bites: Chiggers, Ticks, Mosquitoes, and Biting Gnats

These tiny creatures are the most common wildlife hazard you will face in the backcountry. While individual bites are usually minor, they can transmit serious diseases like Lyme disease (ticks), West Nile virus (mosquitoes), and Rocky Mountain spotted fever (ticks).

**Prevention:** Wear long sleeves and pants tucked into socks in areas with heavy insect activity. Apply EPA-registered insect repellent containing DEET (20–30%) or picaridin. Treat clothing with permethrin. Do thorough tick checks after every outing, paying special attention to hairlines, armpits, waistlines, and behind the knees.

**Treatment for ticks:** Use fine-tipped tweezers to grasp the tick as close to the skin as possible. Pull straight upward with steady, even pressure. Do not twist, squeeze, or burn the tick. Clean the bite area with antiseptic. Save the tick in a sealed bag in case you develop symptoms later — a doctor can identify the species.

**Treatment for other bites:** Clean the area, apply anti-itch cream if available, and avoid scratching (which can cause infection).

### Bee Stings

Most bee stings cause local pain and swelling that resolve on their own. The danger comes from allergic reactions — a severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis) can be life-threatening.

**Prevention:** Avoid wearing perfume or brightly colored clothing in areas with heavy bee activity. Stay calm around bees — swatting at them increases the chance of being stung. Watch for ground nests on trails.

**Treatment:** Remove the stinger by scraping it sideways with a flat edge (credit card, knife blade). Do not squeeze with tweezers, which can inject more venom. Clean the area, apply cold, and take an antihistamine if available. If the person has a known allergy, help them use their epinephrine auto-injector (EpiPen) and call for emergency help immediately.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Anyone with a known bee allergy must carry an epinephrine auto-injector on every outing. If someone is stung and develops hives over their body, swelling of the face or throat, difficulty breathing, or a rapid pulse, use the EpiPen and call 911. Anaphylaxis can kill in minutes.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Spider Bites

Most spiders in North America are harmless, but two species demand respect: the **black widow** and the **brown recluse**. Black widows are shiny black with a red hourglass on their abdomen. Brown recluses are light brown with a dark violin shape on their back.

**Prevention:** Shake out boots, clothing, and sleeping bags before use. Wear gloves when handling firewood or reaching into dark spaces. Check under rocks and logs before sitting.

**Treatment:** Clean the bite area, apply a cold compress, and keep the bite below heart level. Seek medical attention — antivenin may be needed for severe reactions. Try to identify or photograph the spider if you can do so safely.

### Scorpion Stings

Scorpions are found in dry, warm regions — particularly the American Southwest. They hide under rocks, logs, and debris during the day and are active at night.

**Prevention:** Shake out boots and clothing in scorpion territory. Do not walk barefoot at night. Check your sleeping area before lying down.

**Treatment:** Most scorpion stings cause intense local pain but are not life-threatening (except for the bark scorpion, found in Arizona and neighboring states). Clean the area, apply cold, and take pain medication. For a bark scorpion sting — which can cause numbness, muscle twitching, and difficulty breathing — seek emergency medical care.

### Bite of a Wild Mammal

Any wild mammal bite carries the risk of rabies — a viral infection that is almost always fatal once symptoms appear. Common carriers include raccoons, skunks, foxes, bats, and coyotes. Any mammal that approaches you without fear, acts aggressively without provocation, or seems disoriented may be rabid.

**Prevention:** Never approach, feed, or attempt to touch wild animals. Store food properly to avoid attracting animals to camp (more on this in [Req 5c](/merit-badges/wilderness-survival/guide/req5c/)). Make noise on the trail to avoid surprising animals.

**Treatment:** Wash the wound thoroughly with soap and water for at least five minutes. Apply antiseptic and bandage. Seek medical attention as soon as possible — rabies post-exposure treatment must begin promptly. Try to remember the animal's species, size, behavior, and location.

### Venomous Snake Bite

Four types of venomous snakes live in the United States: rattlesnakes, copperheads, coral snakes, and cottonmouths (water moccasins). Most bites happen when people step on a snake they did not see or try to handle one.

**Prevention:** Watch where you step and where you place your hands, especially near rocks, logs, and tall grass. Wear boots and long pants in snake country. Never try to catch, kill, or handle a snake.

**Treatment:** Keep the person calm and still — movement spreads venom faster. Remove jewelry and tight clothing near the bite before swelling starts. Keep the bite at or below heart level. Get to medical care as quickly as possible. **Do not** cut the bite, try to suck out venom, apply a tourniquet, or pack the bite in ice — these old remedies cause more harm than good.
