This requirement covers 13 different injuries and conditions—a comprehensive field manual for backcountry hazards. The key to mastering this requirement is understanding both **prevention** and **first aid**. Prevention saves lives. First aid saves lives when prevention fails. Your counselor will ask you to demonstrate or explain several of these—so be ready to talk through the steps clearly, as if teaching another Scout.

## Heat-Related Injuries

### Dehydration

Dehydration happens when your body loses more water than it takes in. In the backcountry, it sneaks up fast: you're hiking, sweating, and not drinking enough. The danger is that early signs feel mild—thirst, mild fatigue—so Scouts often ignore them until the problem is serious.

**Prevention:**
- Drink *before* you feel thirsty. By the time thirst kicks in, you're already losing fluids.
- Drink 2–3 liters of water per day on moderate activity. More on hot days or at high elevation.
- Avoid caffeine and alcohol in the backcountry—both increase dehydration.
- Eat salty foods with water (nuts, jerky, trail mix)—salt helps your body retain water.

**First Aid:**
- Stop activity immediately.
- Sit in shade and cool off.
- Drink water steadily (small sips, not gulps). If the person has severe dehydration, water absorption is slower—be patient.
- Electrolyte drink (Gatorade, electrolyte powder) helps more than plain water, but plain water works.
- Recovery takes hours. Rest fully before resuming activity.

{{</* drg/tip */>}}
The easiest prevention trick: fill your water bottles before you think you need to. If your water bottles are empty, you're probably already dehydrated.
{{</* /drg/tip */>}}

### Heatstroke

Heatstroke is when your body's temperature-regulation system fails—your core temperature rises to dangerous levels (104°F+), and you can collapse, hallucinate, or go into shock. Unlike heat exhaustion (which is bad but recoverable), heatstroke is a **medical emergency**.

**Prevention:**
- Never rely on "toughness" in hot weather. Take breaks in shade every hour.
- Wear light-colored, loose clothing that lets sweat evaporate. Avoid dark colors and tight fits.
- Start activity early in the day, rest during peak heat (11am–3pm).
- Avoid heavy meals and alcohol before exertion in heat.

**First Aid:**
- Call 911 (or get help immediately).
- Cool the person aggressively: ice packs on neck, armpits, groin. Pour cold water over skin. Immerse in cold water if possible.
- Give cool (not cold) water to drink if they're conscious and can swallow.
- Do NOT let them resume activity—medical evaluation is essential.

{{</* drg/did-you-know */>}}
Heatstroke can cause permanent organ damage even if the person recovers. Fast cooling in the first 30 minutes is the difference between full recovery and lasting injury.
{{</* /drg/did-you-know */>}}

## Cold-Related Injuries

### Hypothermia

Hypothermia is when core body temperature drops below 95°F. It's dangerous because it impairs judgment—a hypothermic person might resist help or take dangerous actions. Hypothermia can occur even in mild weather if conditions are wet and windy.

**Prevention:**
- Dress in layers, starting with wool or synthetic materials (never cotton—cotton stays cold when wet).
- Keep dry. Change out of wet clothing immediately.
- Eat regularly to fuel heat production.
- Stay out of wind. Wind strips away your body's insulating air layer rapidly.
- Recognize early warning signs: uncontrollable shivering, clumsiness, slurred speech, confusion.

**First Aid:**
- Get the person to shelter and out of wind.
- Remove wet clothing. Replace with dry clothing and blankets.
- Warm them gradually—warm, not hot, beverages if they're conscious.
- Handle them gently (rough handling can trigger cardiac arrest).
- Keep them awake and calm.
- Seek medical help if severe.

{{</* drg/safety-first */>}}
Never assume someone is dead from hypothermia until they're warm and unresponsive. People have been revived after hours of cold exposure. Gentle, thorough rewarming is essential.
{{</* /drg/safety-first */>}}

### Frostbite

Frostbite is localized freezing of skin and tissue, usually on fingers, toes, nose, and ears. It progresses from redness (frostnip) to blistering and tissue death.

**Prevention:**
- Wear insulated gloves, socks, and a hat in cold weather.
- Keep extremities dry.
- Avoid touching cold metal or ice directly.
- Recognize wind-chill: a 20°F day with 20mph wind feels like -10°F to your skin.

**First Aid:**
- Rewarm gradually. Do NOT rub the area or use direct heat (fireplace, hot water).
- Immerse in warm (not hot) water for 20–30 minutes.
- Keep the area clean and bandaged.
- Avoid walking on frostbitten feet if possible.
- Seek medical help for anything beyond frostnip.

## Shock

Shock is a critical condition where blood pressure drops and the body goes into survival mode. It can result from blood loss, severe dehydration, severe injury, or even extreme fear. Shock can be fatal if untreated.

**Prevention:**
- Treat injuries early to prevent blood loss.
- Keep the injured person calm and reassured.
- Prevent severe dehydration (as above).

**First Aid:**
- Lay the person flat with legs elevated (unless spinal injury is suspected).
- Keep them warm with blankets.
- Calm them with reassurance. Your voice matters.
- Do NOT give food or drink (they may need surgery).
- Call 911 immediately.

## Blister and Foot Injuries

### Blisters

A blister is a fluid-filled bubble on the skin, usually on the feet. On a long hike, a small blister can end your trip. Prevention is far easier than treatment.

**Prevention:**
- Wear broken-in hiking boots, not new ones.
- Wear moisture-wicking hiking socks (wool or synthetic—never cotton).
- Keep feet dry. Change socks if they get damp.
- Use blister-prevention tape on hot spots (areas where friction starts) before a blister forms.
- Lubricate feet with foot powder or anti-chafe balm.

**First Aid:**
- If a blister hasn't popped: Don't pop it. Tape a bandage or blister pad over it.
- If it has popped: Clean with soap and water, apply antibiotic ointment, and cover with a sterile bandage.
- For a painful blister that needs to be drained: Sterilize a needle with rubbing alcohol, make a small hole at the edge (not the center), apply antibiotic ointment, and cover.
- Keep changing your hiking strategy to reduce friction—wear different socks, adjust your boot lacing, or stop more frequently.

{{</* drg/tip */>}}
The best blister treatment is prevention. On a multi-day hike, inspect your feet every evening and tape any hot spots *before* they blister.
{{</* /drg/tip */>}}

### Ankle and Knee Sprains

A sprain is a stretched or torn ligament. An ankle sprain is common on uneven terrain; knee sprains are less common but more serious.

**Prevention:**
- Wear good hiking boots with ankle support on rough terrain.
- Strengthen your ankles with balance exercises (stand on one leg for 30 seconds).
- Stretch calves and shins before hiking.
- Watch where you step—most sprains happen on wet or rocky ground.

**First Aid:**
- Stop activity immediately.
- Apply RICE: **R**est, **I**ce (cold water or ice pack for 15–20 minutes), **C**ompression (wrap firmly), **E**levation (raise above heart).
- If the ankle or knee is severely swollen, can't bear weight, or has severe pain, assume it's serious and seek medical help.
- For minor sprains, rest for a few days, then gradually resume activity.

## Eye Injuries

Eye injuries in the backcountry can range from minor (sand in the eye) to serious (scratched cornea). Always take eye injuries seriously—vision is irreplaceable.

**Prevention:**
- Wear sunglasses with UV protection.
- Avoid touching your eyes with dirty hands.
- Be careful with sticks and branches when moving through brush.

**First Aid:**
- Foreign object: Flush with clean water or saline. Don't rub. If it doesn't wash out, seek help.
- Scratched eye: Cover with a clean bandage, apply antibiotic ointment, seek medical help.
- Chemical exposure: Flush with water for 15+ minutes, then seek medical help.
- Never remove an object embedded in the eye—seek medical help immediately.

## Insect Bites and Stings

### Chiggers, Ticks, Mosquitoes, and Biting Gnats

These insects are annoying and can carry disease. Prevention is far easier than treatment.

**Prevention:**
- Wear long pants and long sleeves in brushy or grassy areas where these insects live.
- Apply insect repellent (DEET 20-30%) to exposed skin.
- Check your body for ticks every evening, especially in groin, armpits, and behind ears.
- Avoid areas with standing water (mosquito breeding grounds).

**First Aid:**
- Mosquito and gnat bites: Wash with soap and water, apply hydrocortisone cream or calamine lotion, resist scratching.
- Tick removal: Use tweezers to grasp the tick close to the skin, pull straight out (don't twist), disinfect the wound. Do NOT squeeze the tick or use heat.
- Chigger bites: Bathe in hot water, apply anti-itch cream. Chiggers don't burrow deep—standard treatment works.

{{</* drg/did-you-know */>}}
Ticks don't fall from trees—they climb onto you from grass and brush. The best prevention is tucking pants into socks in tick-prone areas.
{{</* /drg/did-you-know */>}}

### Bee Stings

Most bee stings are minor allergic reactions—pain, swelling, itching. Serious allergic reactions are rare but can be fatal.

**Prevention:**
- Avoid disturbing bees and nests.
- Don't wear bright colors or floral patterns (they look like flowers to bees).
- Avoid moving suddenly around bees.
- Know if you have a severe allergy—carry an EpiPen if you do.

**First Aid:**
- Remove the stinger: Scrape it off with a card or knife (don't pinch—pinching releases more venom).
- Wash with soap and water.
- Apply ice for 15 minutes.
- Take antihistamine (Benadryl) or apply hydrocortisone cream.
- Elevate the area if it swells significantly.
- If the person has difficulty breathing, severe swelling, or chest pain: **call 911 immediately**.

### Spider Bites

Most spider bites are harmless. Two exceptions exist in North America: the black widow (shiny black body with red hourglass marking) and brown recluse (brown with violin-shaped marking). Bites from these spiders can cause serious reactions.

**Prevention:**
- Inspect clothing and bedding before wearing or getting in.
- Shake out boots before putting them on.
- Avoid reaching into dark, confined spaces (woodpiles, rock crevices).

**First Aid:**
- Wash with soap and water.
- Apply ice.
- Black widow bite: Severe muscle pain and cramping. Seek medical help immediately.
- Brown recluse bite: Tissue damage around the bite. Seek medical help immediately.
- Other spiders: Minor itching. Apply anti-itch cream and leave it alone.

### Scorpion Stings

Scorpions are most common in the Southwest. Most scorpion stings cause only local pain and swelling. A few species have venom that affects the nervous system.

**Prevention:**
- Inspect shoes, clothing, and bedding before using.
- Avoid reaching into dark areas.
- Wear closed-toe shoes at night.

**First Aid:**
- Wash with soap and water.
- Apply ice.
- Local pain and swelling: Over-the-counter pain reliever and ice.
- Neurological symptoms (tingling, numbness, weakness, difficulty swallowing): Seek immediate medical help.

## Wild Mammal Bites

Any mammal bite carries risk of infection and rabies. All mammal bites deserve medical attention.

**Prevention:**
- Never feed or approach wild animals.
- Make noise while hiking to avoid surprising animals.
- Store food in bear-proof containers or hang it in a tree.

**First Aid:**
- Wash thoroughly with soap and water for at least 5 minutes.
- Flush with clean water.
- Apply antibiotic ointment and cover with a bandage.
- **Seek medical help immediately.** Rabies is fatal if untreated. The doctor will decide if rabies prophylaxis is needed.
- Save or get information about the animal if possible (species, behavior) to help with rabies assessment.

## Venomous Snake Bites

North American venomous snakes include rattlesnakes, copperheads, cottonmouths, and coral snakes. Snake bites are rare, especially if you wear boots and watch where you step.

**Prevention:**
- Wear boots that cover the ankle.
- Watch where you step and place your hands.
- Make noise while hiking—snakes avoid people.
- Never provoke or try to catch a snake.
- Be especially careful at dusk and dawn (when snakes are active).

**First Aid:**
- Remove any constrictive items (bracelets, rings) above the bite.
- Keep the bitten limb immobilized and below heart level (this slows venom spread).
- Get to a hospital immediately. Call 911 if in a remote area.
- Do NOT apply a tourniquet, do NOT cut the wound, do NOT try to suck out venom.
- Mark the spread of swelling with a pen and time on the skin—show the hospital staff.
- Antivenom is the only effective treatment. Time matters—get help fast.

## Before You Move On

By the end of this requirement, you should be able to explain (or demonstrate) first aid and prevention for at least several of these 13 conditions. Your counselor will ask you to show or explain how you'd handle different scenarios. Practice explaining—clear, confident communication is part of demonstrating mastery. You don't need to memorize every detail, but you should know the general approach: recognize the condition, remember the steps, and get help when needed.
