## A. Congratulations

You've covered the full spectrum of wilderness survival — from backcountry first aid to shelter building, fire starting to signaling, water treatment to the psychology of staying calm under pressure. You've assembled kits, learned the seven priorities, and spent a night in a shelter you built with your own hands. These aren't just merit badge skills. ## B. Advanced Navigation and Natural Wayfinding

A map and compass are essential — but what happens when you don't have them? Traditional navigation methods have guided travelers for thousands of years and remain reliable when technology fails.

### Navigating by the Sun

The sun rises in the east and sets in the west (roughly — the exact positions shift with season and latitude). At solar noon in the Northern Hemisphere, the sun is due south. You can use these facts to establish basic cardinal directions.

**Shadow stick method:** Push a straight stick into the ground and mark the tip of its shadow with a rock. Wait 15–20 minutes and mark the new shadow tip. A line between the two marks runs roughly east-west (the first mark is west, the second is east). A perpendicular line gives you north-south.

**Watch method:** Point the hour hand of an analog watch at the sun. Halfway between the hour hand and the 12 o'clock position is approximately south. (In the Southern Hemisphere, point the 12 at the sun and halve the angle to the hour hand for north.)

### Navigating by Stars

At night, Polaris (the North Star) indicates true north. Find it by locating the Big Dipper — the two stars forming the outer edge of the "cup" point directly at Polaris, roughly five times the distance between them.

In the Southern Hemisphere, use the Southern Cross to find south: extend the long axis of the cross 4.5 times its length to find the approximate south celestial pole.

### Natural Indicators

These are less reliable than celestial navigation but useful as supplementary clues:

- **Moss** grows on all sides of trees, but tends to be thicker on the north side (less direct sun) in the Northern Hemisphere
- **Spider webs** are often built on the south side of trees (warmer, more insect activity)
- **Snow** melts faster on south-facing slopes
- **Prevailing wind patterns** create distinctive tree shapes — leaning away from the dominant wind direction

Polynesian navigators crossed thousands of miles of open Pacific Ocean without instruments, using star paths, ocean swell patterns, cloud formations over distant islands, and the flight paths of migratory birds. Their navigation knowledge was passed down orally through generations and is considered one of humanity's greatest intellectual achievements.

## C. Wilderness Psychology: The Deep Survival Mindset

The mental game of survival goes deeper than avoiding panic. Researchers who study survivors — people who walked out of situations that killed others — have identified consistent psychological traits and strategies.

### The Survivor Personality

Laurence Gonzales, author of *Deep Survival*, identified key traits of people who survive against the odds:

- **Perceive and believe:** They accept their situation quickly rather than denying it. Denial wastes critical time. "I am lost" is more useful than "I can't be lost."
- **Stay calm and think clearly:** They manage fear through breathing, self-talk, and task focus.
- **Make decisions and act:** They commit to a plan rather than flip-flopping. Even an imperfect plan executed with commitment outperforms indecision.
- **Celebrate small victories:** They find satisfaction in small accomplishments — a fire that catches, a shelter that holds. Each victory builds momentum.
- **Count their blessings:** Survivors consistently report gratitude for what they have rather than despair over what they lack.
- **Surrender but don't give up:** They accept that they can't control everything, but they never stop working to improve their situation.

### Managing Fear Over Time

Short-term fear management (box breathing, STOP) works for the first few hours. In extended survival, you need strategies for managing fear, boredom, and hopelessness over days:

- **Set daily goals.** "Today I will improve my shelter insulation." Goals create forward momentum.
- **Keep a routine.** Wake up, tend fire, check signals, improve shelter, gather water. Structure prevents despair.
- **Talk to yourself out loud.** It sounds odd, but hearing your own rational voice counters the negative internal monologue. Narrate what you're doing and why.
- **Plan your first meal home.** Visualization of rescue reinforces belief that rescue is coming.

## D. Extreme Environment Survival Techniques

### Desert Survival Beyond the Basics

In [Req 5a](/merit-badges/wilderness-survival/guide/req5a/), you learned the fundamentals of hot and dry survival. Here are advanced techniques:

- **Solar stills:** Dig a hole in the ground, place a container at the bottom, cover the hole with a clear plastic sheet, and weight the center with a small rock so the sheet forms a cone pointing down. Moisture evaporates from the soil, condenses on the plastic, and drips into the container. Yield is low (1–2 cups per day), but it's better than nothing.
- **Flash flood awareness:** Desert terrain is carved by water that rarely flows — but when it does, dry washes (arroyos) can fill wall-to-wall in minutes from storms miles away. Never camp in a dry wash, even if the sky above you is clear.
- **Traveling at night:** In extreme heat, consider moving during the cool hours (8 PM to 8 AM) and sheltering during the day. Desert temperatures can swing 40–50°F between day and night.

### Mountain Survival

Altitude adds complications that lowland survival training doesn't cover:

- **Altitude sickness** affects most people above 8,000 feet. Symptoms include headache, nausea, fatigue, and poor judgment. The only reliable treatment is descending to lower elevation.
- **Lightning:** Above tree line, you are the tallest object on the landscape. During electrical storms, descend immediately. If caught, crouch on your feet (don't lie flat), away from ridges, isolated trees, and water.
- **Rockfall and avalanche** zones require constant awareness. Travel below cliff faces quickly, avoid snow-loaded slopes steeper than 30 degrees, and listen for warning sounds (cracking, rumbling).

## E. Real-World Experiences

These experiences let you practice and expand your wilderness survival skills in real settings. Each one builds competence and confidence beyond what you can learn from a book.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Wilderness Survival Weekend" >}}
**What:** A 48-hour survival exercise with your troop or a wilderness school, where you build shelter, start fires, purify water, and navigate with map and compass.

**Where:** Scout camps, state forests, or private land with permission.

**What you'll gain:** Extended practice under realistic conditions. You'll discover what skills come naturally and what needs more work.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Wilderness First Aid Course" >}}
**What:** A 16-hour certification course covering backcountry medical emergencies — everything from wound management to improvised splinting to patient assessment.

**Where:** American Red Cross, NOLS Wilderness Medicine, or SOLO Wilderness Medicine schools.

**What you'll gain:** A recognized certification that qualifies you for many outdoor leadership positions. Takes the first aid skills from Req 1a to a professional level.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Orienteering Competition" >}}
**What:** A timed navigation challenge where you find checkpoints in the backcountry using a map and compass. Courses range from beginner to expert.

**Where:** Local orienteering clubs hold regular events. Find a club through Orienteering USA (ousa.org).

**What you'll gain:** Sharp navigation skills under time pressure, fitness, and familiarity with terrain reading.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Winter Camping Trip" >}}
**What:** A cold-weather overnight that tests your layering system, fire-building skills, snow shelter construction, and cold-weather water management.

**Where:** High-adventure bases like Philmont or Northern Tier, or any winter-capable campsite in your council.

**What you'll gain:** Confidence in extreme conditions. Cold weather is the ultimate test of survival skills.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}
