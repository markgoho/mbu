## Option A — Research a Career

{{< drg/requirement number="10a" >}}
Explore careers related to this merit badge. Research one career to learn about the training and education needed, costs, job prospects, salary, job duties, and career advancement. With permission of your parent or guardian, your research methods may include an internet or library search, an interview with a professional in the field, or a visit to a location where people in this career work. Discuss with your counselor both your findings and what about this profession might make it an interesting career.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Wilderness survival skills open doors to a wide range of careers — many of which aren't obvious at first glance.

{{< drg/image src="images/wilderness-career-collage.avif" alt="A grid of four illustrated portraits of wilderness professionals: a park ranger, a wilderness medicine professional, a survival instructor, and a search and rescue volunteer" >}}

### Career Options to Research

**Search and Rescue (SAR) Technician/Coordinator**
SAR professionals work for county sheriffs, national parks, mountain rescue teams, and volunteer organizations. They combine wilderness skills with first aid, rope rescue, navigation, and team coordination. Most SAR volunteers start in volunteer roles — many county SAR teams actively recruit trained Scouts.

**Wilderness Emergency Medical Technician (WEMT)**
WEMTs provide emergency medical care in remote settings where standard EMS cannot respond. The certification combines EMT training with wilderness medicine protocols. WEMTs work for NOLS, Outward Bound, wilderness therapy programs, and backcountry expedition support.

**Park Ranger (Law Enforcement or Interpretive)**
National and state park rangers are often generalists with deep wilderness skills. Duties range from visitor education and trail maintenance to law enforcement and SAR coordination. The NPS, BLM, and state park systems employ thousands of rangers annually.

**Forest Service Fire Lookout / Wildland Firefighter**
Wildland firefighting requires extreme physical fitness, survival skills, and the ability to work in remote terrain in high-stress conditions. Hotshot crews, smokejumpers, and helitack crews all represent different levels of this career.

**Wilderness Guide / Expedition Leader**
Commercial wilderness guides lead multi-day backpacking, climbing, river rafting, and ski touring trips. Most certifications come from the American Mountain Guides Association (AMGA) or Wilderness Education Association (WEA).

**SERE Instructor (Military)**
Survival, Evasion, Resistance, and Escape (SERE) instructors train military personnel in survival and prisoner-of-war resistance skills. This is a military career that directly uses the skills in this badge.

**Wilderness Therapist**
Wilderness therapy programs use outdoor challenge and survival experiences to treat at-risk youth and adults dealing with mental health, addiction, or behavioral issues. This career combines outdoor leadership with counseling or psychology training.

### What Your Counselor Wants to Hear

When you discuss your research, be prepared to talk about:

{{< drg/checklist title="Career Research Checklist" subtitle="Address all six areas for Option A" >}}
- **Training and education needed:** Degrees, certifications, relevant experience
- **Costs:** College, training programs, certification fees
- **Job prospects:** Is this field growing, stable, or shrinking?
- **Salary:** Entry-level to experienced range
- **Job duties:** A specific day-in-the-life description, not just a title
- **Career advancement:** Where does this career lead over 5–10 years?
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

## Option B — Hobby or Volunteer Service

{{< drg/requirement number="10b" >}}
Explore how you could use knowledge and skills from the Wilderness Survival merit badge to pursue a hobby or to serve as a volunteer. Research any training needed, expenses, and organizations that promote or support it. Discuss with your counselor what short-term and long-term goals you might have if you pursue this.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Wilderness survival skills aren't just for emergencies. They translate into a range of hobbies and volunteer opportunities that let you continue developing and using them.

### Ideas to Explore

**Search and Rescue Volunteering**
Many county SAR teams welcome trained volunteers. Scout-age members sometimes participate in training and junior support roles. Adult SAR volunteers get regular wilderness skills training, rope rescue certification, and navigation practice as part of their volunteer commitment.

**Ultralight Backpacking**
Ultralight backpacking is a philosophy of reducing pack weight through skill substitution — you carry less because you know how to improvise more. The survival skills in this badge directly feed this hobby. MYOG (Make Your Own Gear) is a related community of people who sew their own shelters, tarps, and clothing.

**Bushcraft**
Bushcraft is the hobby of practicing traditional outdoor skills — fire-by-friction, natural shelter building, foraging, tracking, hide tanning, and primitive tool-making. Organizations like the Aboriginal Living Skills School and various bushcraft schools offer courses. This is the hobby most directly aligned with wilderness survival skills.

**Wilderness First Responder (WFR) Certification**
WFR (pronounced "Woofer") is a 70–80 hour wilderness medicine course that builds directly on the first aid knowledge in this badge. It's the standard certification for backcountry guides, expedition leaders, and serious wilderness travelers. NOLS Wilderness Medicine and Wilderness Medical Associates offer courses nationwide.

**Orienteering**
Competitive orienteering uses a topo map and compass to navigate a course of control points in natural terrain. It develops the navigation and observation skills that prevent you from needing to use survival skills in the first place. USA Orienteering maintains a directory of clubs and events nationwide.

### What Your Counselor Wants to Hear

Be ready to discuss:

{{< drg/checklist title="Hobby/Volunteer Research Checklist" subtitle="Address all areas for Option B" >}}
- **The specific hobby or volunteer role** you explored
- **Training needed:** Certifications, courses, skills prerequisites
- **Expenses:** Equipment, course fees, membership costs
- **Organizations:** Which groups or clubs support this activity
- **Short-term goals:** What you could realistically do in the next 6–12 months
- **Long-term goals:** Where you could take this in 3–5 years
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/tip >}}
The best discussions with your counselor go beyond facts. Be ready to say what *interested you* about the career or hobby — not just what you found in your research. Counselors are curious about your genuine reaction to what you learned.
{{< /drg/tip >}}
