This requirement asks you to look outward — at how the skills and knowledge from this badge connect to real careers and meaningful hobbies. Choose the path that interests you most.

- **Req 10a:** Research a career related to wilderness survival and outdoor emergency response.
- **Req 10b:** Explore how you could use Wilderness Survival skills as a hobby or volunteer pursuit.

You only need to complete **one** of the two options.

---

## Option A: Career Exploration

{{< drg/requirement number="10a" >}}
Explore careers related to this merit badge. Research one career to learn about the training and education needed, costs, job prospects, salary, job duties, and career advancement. With permission of your parent or guardian, your research methods may include an internet or library search, an interview with a professional in the field, or a visit to a location where people in this career work. Discuss with your counselor both your findings and what about this profession might make it an interesting career.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Wilderness survival skills underpin a surprisingly wide range of careers. Here are several worth researching:

{{< drg/image src="images/wilderness-career-collage.avif" alt="Four-panel collage of wilderness careers: a search and rescue volunteer on a mountain trail, a park ranger at a trailhead, an outdoor instructor demonstrating first aid, and an outdoor educator leading teens on a forest trail" >}}

### Search and Rescue (SAR) Specialist

SAR teams respond to missing person cases, wilderness accidents, and backcountry emergencies. Members include volunteer coordinators, technical rope rescue specialists, swift water rescuers, and tracking experts. Some SAR roles are career positions (county sheriff's office, National Park Service) while many are volunteer.

**Training path:** Emergency medical certifications (EMT, Wilderness First Responder), rope rescue training, navigation courses, and SAR-specific ICS (Incident Command System) training.

### Park Ranger / Conservation Officer

National Park Service, USDA Forest Service, and state park rangers manage public land, enforce regulations, educate visitors, and respond to emergencies. They need the full range of wilderness skills covered in this badge, plus specific resource management and law enforcement training.

**Training path:** Many rangers have degrees in natural resource management, forestry, or environmental science. Law enforcement rangers require federal law enforcement training.

### Wilderness Medicine Provider (WEMS / WFR / WEMT)

Wilderness medicine professionals apply emergency medicine principles in remote settings. Wilderness First Responder (WFR) is the standard certification for backcountry guides and outdoor educators. Wilderness Emergency Medical Technician (WEMT) adds to standard EMT training.

**Training path:** WFR is typically a 10-day course. WEMT requires prior EMT certification. These credentials are required by most outdoor guide outfitters and wilderness programs.

### Outdoor Education Instructor / Wilderness Program Leader

Organizations like NOLS (National Outdoor Leadership School), Outward Bound, and countless regional programs hire outdoor educators to lead backcountry expeditions, teach wilderness skills, and facilitate leadership development through outdoor challenges. This is one of the most direct career paths for someone who loves what this badge is about.

**Training path:** WFR certification, relevant technical skills (rock climbing, paddling, skiing depending on program), first aid, and often a degree in outdoor education, experiential education, or a related field.

### Fire Lookout / Wildland Firefighter

The U.S. Forest Service and Bureau of Land Management hire seasonal wildland firefighters, fire lookouts (who staff remote towers to spot fires), and fire crew supervisors. These roles require significant physical fitness and survival skills in remote environments.

**Training path:** Wildland firefighter certification (S-130/190 Firefighter Training), physical fitness standards, first aid.

## Option B: Hobby and Volunteer Exploration

{{< drg/requirement number="10b" >}}
Explore how you could use knowledge and skills from the Wilderness Survival merit badge to pursue a hobby or to serve as volunteer. Research any training needed, expenses, and organizations that promote or support it. Discuss with your counselor what short-term and long-term goals you might have if you pursue this.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Wilderness survival skills translate directly into some of the most meaningful outdoor hobbies and volunteer opportunities available. Here are several directions worth exploring:

### Volunteer SAR

Most county SAR teams actively recruit volunteers. You don't need to be a professional — many teams welcome motivated volunteers starting in their late teens. Training is provided, and the work directly applies every skill from this badge.

**Organizations to research:** Your county sheriff's office SAR team; Mountain Rescue Association (MRA); National Association for Search and Rescue (NASAR).

### Wilderness First Aid Volunteer

Community emergency preparedness programs and outdoor organizations often need volunteers with wilderness medical training. WFR-certified volunteers support backcountry events, trail crews, and remote Scout camps.

### Backpacking and Thru-Hiking

The skills in this badge — shelter, fire, water treatment, navigation, first aid — are the exact same skills needed for multi-day and long-distance backpacking. Trails like the Appalachian Trail, Pacific Crest Trail, and Continental Divide Trail are goals that some Scout-age hikers work toward for years.

**Starting point:** Plan a series of progressively longer overnight and weekend backpacking trips. The American Hiking Society and American Long Distance Hiking Association are good starting resources.

### Primitive Skills Practice

A dedicated hobbyist community exists around primitive skills — hand-tool woodworking, friction fire, natural cordage, hide tanning, and traditional navigation. Groups like the Society of Primitive Technology and local primitive skills schools host workshops and events.

### Amateur Radio Emergency Services (ARES)

Communications are critical in wilderness emergencies. Ham radio operators with ARES training provide emergency communications support during SAR operations and natural disasters. Getting a Technician-class amateur radio license is achievable in a few weeks of study and opens a direct connection between this badge's signaling skills and real emergency response.

{{< drg/tip >}}
When discussing this requirement with your counselor, bring specific research — not just "I looked at SAR" but "I found that our county SAR team requires WFR certification and accepts volunteers at 18. The WFR course costs about $700 and takes 10 days. A short-term goal would be to take a Wilderness First Aid course next summer as a stepping stone."
{{< /drg/tip >}}
