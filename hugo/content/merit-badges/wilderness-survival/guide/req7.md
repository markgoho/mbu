This requirement covers three related signaling skills. Your goal in any survival situation is to make yourself as visible and audible as possible to anyone searching for you — whether they are on foot, in a helicopter, or in a boat.

## Signaling Principles

Effective signals share three characteristics:

1. **Contrast with the environment.** Bright colors against dark forest, smoke against blue sky, reflected light against dull terrain. Anything that looks different from the natural surroundings attracts attention.
2. **Patterns that do not occur in nature.** Three fires in a triangle, straight lines, geometric shapes. Searchers are trained to spot unnatural patterns.
3. **Repetition in groups of three.** Three whistle blasts, three fires, three flashes — the universal distress signal across all outdoor contexts.

## Signaling Methods

### Audible Signals

**Whistle:** The most reliable audible signal. Three short blasts repeated at regular intervals is the universal distress signal. A whistle carries farther than a human voice, requires less energy, and works when your voice is gone from shouting. Always carry a pealess whistle (no moving parts that can freeze or clog with dirt).

**Yelling:** Less effective than a whistle but better than silence. Use a rhythmic pattern — three shouts, pause, three shouts — rather than continuous yelling, which exhausts your voice quickly.

### Visual Signals — Fire

Fire is visible day and night, making it one of the most versatile signaling methods:

- **Night:** Flames are visible from miles away in darkness. Build your fire in an open area on high ground if possible.
- **Day:** Smoke is more visible than flames during daylight. Create thick smoke by adding green branches, wet leaves, or damp moss to a hot fire. White smoke shows best against dark backgrounds; adding rubber, plastic, or oil creates dark smoke that shows against snow or light sky — but use these only in genuine emergencies due to toxicity.
- **Signal fire pattern:** Three fires arranged in a triangle (each about 100 feet apart) is the international distress signal recognized by aircraft.

### Visual Signals — Mirror

A signal mirror is one of the most effective daytime rescue tools ever invented. On a sunny day, a mirror flash can be seen from over 10 miles away by aircraft and over 7 miles by ground searchers.

**How to use a signal mirror:**

1. Hold the mirror close to your face, near one eye.
2. Extend your other hand toward the target (aircraft, rescuer, or distant point).
3. Tilt the mirror until the reflected sunlight falls on your outstretched hand — you will see a bright spot on your fingers.
4. Move the mirror slightly so the bright spot aligns with your target, using your fingers as a sight.
5. Flash the mirror rapidly (do not hold a steady beam) — the flickering catches attention better than a constant reflection.

If your mirror has an aiming hole, look through it and tilt the mirror until the bright spot visible through the hole falls on your target.

**Improvised mirrors:** A phone screen, the bottom of a polished pot, aluminum foil wrapped around a flat surface, or even a CD can substitute for a purpose-built signal mirror.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Practice your mirror technique before you need it. On a sunny day, try to flash sunlight onto a specific tree or rock 50–100 yards away. The aiming takes practice — and you do not want your first attempt to be when a helicopter is flying over.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Visual Signals — Ground Markers

When you are in an open area visible from the air, create ground signals that can be spotted by aircraft:

- Use materials that contrast with the ground — dark rocks on snow, light-colored clothing on dark soil
- Make signals as large as possible — at least 10 feet long and 3 feet wide for each letter or symbol
- Straight lines and geometric shapes stand out against natural terrain
- Stomp out signals in snow or arrange rocks, branches, or clothing on the ground

### Other Signaling Methods

- **Brightly colored clothing or gear** spread out in an open area
- **Emergency space blanket** (the reflective silver side catches sunlight and aircraft attention)
- **Flashlight at night** — flash in groups of three
- **Waving arms** in a specific pattern: both arms raised and lowered repeatedly signals distress. One arm raised and lowered means you are okay.

## Five Ground-to-Air Signals

These standardized symbols are recognized internationally by search aircraft pilots. You need to know five from memory:

| Symbol | Shape | Meaning |
|--------|-------|---------|
| **V** | Large V shape | Require assistance |
| **X** | Large X shape | Require medical help |
| **I** | Single straight line | Require supplies |
| **→** | Arrow pointing direction | Traveling this direction |
| **LL** | Two L shapes side by side | All is well |

Each symbol should be at least 10 feet tall and made with materials that contrast with the ground. In snow, stomp them out or fill them with dark branches. On dark ground, use light-colored rocks or clothing.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Ground-to-air signals were first standardized during World War II by the International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO). The same symbols used by downed pilots in the 1940s are still in use today by search and rescue teams worldwide.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Signaling Strategy

Do not rely on a single signaling method. Layer your signals:

- **Passive signals** (ground markers, bright gear spread out) work while you rest
- **Active signals** (mirror, whistle, fire) deploy when you see or hear searchers
- **Audible signals** (whistle) work through fog and dense forest where visual signals fail
- **Visual signals** (mirror, fire, markers) work at distances where sound cannot reach

{{< drg/be-prepared title="You Hear a Helicopter" >}}
You have been lost for six hours and hear the distant thrum of a helicopter. You have about 30 seconds before it passes over your area.

- **Step one:** Grab your signal mirror and move to the most open area you can reach in seconds.
- **Step two:** Begin flashing the mirror toward the helicopter, sweeping the beam back and forth across the aircraft's path.
- **Step three:** If you have a whistle, blow three blasts repeatedly.
- **Step four:** Wave brightly colored clothing or a space blanket overhead.
- **Step five:** If the helicopter does not see you, stay calm. It will likely make multiple passes. Build a smoke signal for the next pass — add green branches to your fire immediately.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}
