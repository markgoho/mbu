In a true wilderness emergency, you can't build a tent from scratch. You build with what's available: branches, snow, leaves, and natural features. This requirement is about understanding your options in two very different environments.

## Survival Shelters in a Forest

**Finding a Natural Shelter**

Before you build, look for natural features you can use:

- **Rock overhangs or caves:** Natural protection from rain and wind
- **Fallen trees:** A large dead tree with low branches creates a natural windbreak
- **Dense vegetation:** Thick evergreen trees block rain and wind; hollows between trees offer insulation
- **Embankments:** A raised area of earth provides wind protection on one side

If you find a good natural site, enhance it rather than starting from scratch.

**Building a Lean-To**

The lean-to is the simplest survival shelter and works well in forests:

1. **Find or build a ridgepole:** A sturdy branch 6–8 feet long, positioned 4–5 feet high between two trees (or propped on rocks)

2. **Add framework:** Lean branches at an angle against the ridgepole, creating an A-frame

3. **Add insulation and cover:** Layer evergreen branches, bark, large leaves, or moss over the frame to insulate and block wind and rain

4. **Build a floor:** Collect leaves, pine needles, or branches to create a thick, insulating layer between you and the ground

5. **Windbreak:** Position the open side away from the wind direction. Create a wind wall on the upwind side if possible

**Building a Debris Shelter**

A debris shelter is even simpler and provides excellent insulation:

1. **Find a ridgepole or create a frame:** A fallen log or branch about 3 feet high

2. **Create a peaked roof:** Lean branches on both sides to create a tent-like shape

3. **Cover thoroughly:** Pile leaves, pine needles, bark, moss, and soil over the entire structure until it's 2–3 feet thick. This insulation is critical.

4. **Leave an opening:** Keep one small entrance, just large enough to crawl through

5. **Plug the entrance:** Stuff leaves or branches in the opening to reduce air flow once you're inside

**Key Principle:** **Insulation from the ground is as important as insulation from above.** Lying directly on cold earth drains heat. Always build a thick floor of leaves or branches.

## Survival Shelters in Snow

**Snow Cave Shelter**

In deep snow, a snow cave provides excellent insulation (snow is full of air pockets that trap heat).

1. **Find appropriate snow:** Deep, compacted snow works best. Avoid unstable or avalanche-prone slopes. Look for a snowbank or drift against a natural feature like a tree or rock.

2. **Dig horizontally:** Create an entrance tunnel sloping slightly upward into the bank. This prevents cold air from sinking into your sleeping area.

3. **Create a sleeping chamber:** Dig a larger chamber inside, about 3–4 feet wide and tall enough to sit up

4. **Insulate the floor:** Pack snow carefully to create a smooth, level sleeping platform. Add pine boughs or your emergency blanket on top

5. **Smooth the ceiling:** Smooth the roof with your hands to prevent water droplets that form from dripping on you

6. **Entrance control:** Leave the entrance small to minimize heat loss. You can plug it partially once inside.

**Why it works:** Snow insulates because of its air pockets. A well-built snow cave can maintain an inside temperature near freezing (32°F) even when outside is far colder.

**Snow Shelter Alternatives**

If deep snow isn't available or digging is exhausting:

1. **Snow wall:** Stack snow blocks to create a wind wall. Sit behind it with your emergency shelter or sleeping bag.

2. **Burrow in:** If snow is deep, burrow into a snowbank and insulate yourself with pine boughs or emergency blankets.

3. **Use trees:** Dense evergreens hold snow on their branches, creating shelter underneath. The downside: snow can slide off and bury you if conditions warm.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
In avalanche terrain, choose shelter locations carefully. Avoid slopes that could slide. If unsure, choose a location on stable ground rather than under a slope.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Key Principles for All Shelters

**Location matters:**
- Avoid low-lying areas (cold air sinks; water collects)
- Avoid exposed ridges (wind is strongest)
- Look for flat ground or slight slopes for drainage
- Stay near water and fuel, but not in dense forest where you're invisible to searchers

**Insulation is survival:**
- Your body heat warms the shelter only if it's small and well-insulated
- Thick layers (leaves, branches, bark) do the work of synthetic insulation
- Ground insulation prevents your body heat from being drawn into the earth

**The goal isn't comfort:**
- You're not building a cozy cabin; you're building a heat-trapping cocoon
- It should be just large enough for you and maybe one other person
- Smaller = warmer

**Test your design at home:**
- Build a leaf pile shelter in your yard during cool weather
- Spend 30 minutes inside to understand how it works
- This builds confidence for the real thing

{{< drg/tip >}}
When gathering materials, work efficiently. Collect branches, leaves, and moss first, then assemble. This prevents you from being exhausted before your shelter is done.
{{< /drg/tip >}}
