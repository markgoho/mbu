{{< drg/requirement number="5d" >}}
Describe how to build or find survival shelters in a forest or in snow.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A survival shelter does not need to be pretty. It needs to do three things: block wind, shed water, and trap your body heat. The fanciest shelter in the world is useless if it takes so long to build that you exhaust yourself in the process. Speed and effectiveness beat aesthetics every time.

## Before You Build: Site Selection

Where you place your shelter matters as much as how you build it. A perfect shelter in the wrong spot can be flooded, crushed, or exposed to wind that makes it useless.

**Look for:**
- Natural windbreaks — rock faces, dense tree stands, hillsides
- Flat, slightly elevated ground (water flows downhill — do not camp in a low spot)
- Proximity to building materials (branches, leaves, bark)
- Proximity to water (but not too close — 200 feet minimum to avoid flooding and condensation)

**Avoid:**
- Hilltops and ridgelines (maximum wind exposure)
- Dry riverbeds and drainage channels (flash flood risk)
- Standing dead trees that could fall ("widow makers")
- Directly under large branches that could break under wind or snow load
- Avalanche paths in mountainous terrain

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Always look up before choosing a shelter site. Dead branches, loose rocks above, and leaning trees can all come down in a storm. A falling tree limb is called a "widow maker" for good reason.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Forest Shelters

### Debris Lean-To

The debris lean-to is one of the simplest and most effective forest shelters. It blocks wind and rain from one direction and can be built quickly with no tools.

**How to build:**
1. Find or create a ridgepole — a sturdy branch about 9–12 feet long. Prop one end on a stump, rock, or tree fork at about waist height. The other end rests on the ground.
2. Lean shorter branches against the ridgepole at a 45–60° angle, close together, to form a wall. These are your ribs.
3. Layer smaller branches, twigs, and leaves across the ribs, starting from the bottom and working up (like shingles on a roof). Each layer should overlap the one below.
4. Pile on leaves, pine needles, grass, or any debris you can find — at least 2–3 feet thick. The debris is your insulation and waterproofing. More is better.
5. Build a thick bed of debris on the ground inside — at least 6 inches deep. Ground insulation is critical.

The opening should face away from the prevailing wind. A fire built in front of the opening reflects heat into the shelter.

### Debris Hut

A debris hut is essentially a lean-to built low enough to form a cocoon around your body. It is warmer than a lean-to because the smaller air space traps heat more efficiently, but it is also more claustrophobic.

**How to build:**
1. Create a ridgepole just long enough for your body (your height plus 1 foot). Prop it up at one end about 3 feet high.
2. Frame both sides with ribs, creating a tent shape just wide enough for your body.
3. Layer debris thickly on all sides — 3 feet or more. Think of yourself as the filling inside a debris sleeping bag.
4. Stuff the interior with dry leaves for insulation. You will crawl in and pull leaves over yourself like a blanket.
5. Block the entrance with a plug of debris or your pack.

### Natural Shelters

Before building anything, look for natural shelters that need minimal modification:

- **Fallen trees** — a large fallen trunk with root ball attached creates an instant windbreak. Lean branches against the trunk to extend the shelter.
- **Rock overhangs** — natural ledges that shed rain. Add a debris wall to block wind.
- **Tree wells** — the space at the base of a large evergreen tree where snow has not accumulated. The branches above provide natural overhead cover.
- **Dense conifers** — the lowest branches of a spruce or fir tree can create a relatively dry, wind-sheltered space. Add debris for ground insulation.

{{< drg/tip >}}
A natural shelter that needs 30 minutes of improvement beats a from-scratch shelter that takes 3 hours to build. Always scout your area before committing to a build. You might find something that is already 80% done.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Snow Shelters

Snow is an excellent insulator — the temperature inside a well-built snow shelter stays near 32°F regardless of how cold it is outside. A snow shelter that is 32°F inside while it is -20°F outside is a 52-degree improvement that can save your life.

### Tree Well Shelter

The easiest snow shelter, requiring almost no construction. Large evergreen trees create a natural well around their trunk where snow cannot accumulate because the branches deflect it.

**How to use:**
1. Find a large spruce or fir with dense, drooping branches and a visible well around the trunk.
2. Dig down to the ground surface inside the well.
3. Line the bottom with branches for insulation.
4. Pull lower branches down to enclose the space, packing snow on top for additional wind protection.

### Snow Trench

Quick and effective when the snow is deep enough (at least 3–4 feet).

**How to build:**
1. Dig a trench just wider than your body and about 3 feet deep.
2. Cover the top with branches, a tarp, or snow blocks.
3. Insulate the floor with branches or a sleeping pad.
4. Create a small entrance that you can block with your pack.

### Quinzhee

A quinzhee is a hollowed-out mound of snow. Unlike an igloo (which uses carved blocks), a quinzhee is made from loose, piled snow that sinters (bonds together) as it settles.

**How to build:**
1. Pile snow into a dome at least 7 feet in diameter and 5 feet high. Mix snow from different depths for varying moisture content, which helps it bond.
2. Let the pile settle and sinter for at least 1–2 hours (longer is better).
3. Insert sticks 12–18 inches long evenly around the dome. These are thickness guides — when you dig from inside and hit a stick, stop. This prevents accidentally digging through the wall.
4. Hollow out the interior, leaving walls at least 12 inches thick.
5. Create a ventilation hole in the roof (critical — without ventilation, carbon dioxide can build up to dangerous levels).
6. Make the entrance lower than the sleeping platform so cold air sinks down and out.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Every snow shelter needs a ventilation hole. Snow shelters are well-insulated, which means they can trap carbon dioxide from your breathing. A ventilation hole in the roof — even just a fist-sized opening — prevents dangerous CO2 buildup. Keep a stick inside to clear the hole if it gets blocked by drifting snow.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The Inuit word "quinzhee" comes from the Athabaskan peoples of northern Canada. Unlike igloos (which require specific snow conditions and significant skill), quinzhees can be built from any type of snow, making them far more practical for most survival situations.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}
