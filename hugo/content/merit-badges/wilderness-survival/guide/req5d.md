{{< drg/requirement number="5d" >}}
Describe how to build or find survival shelters in a forest or in snow.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Shelter is Priority 3 in the seven priorities of survival, right after STOP and First Aid. In extreme conditions, you have about three hours before exposure starts shutting your body down. That means your shelter doesn't need to be comfortable — it needs to be *fast*. The best survival shelter is the one you can build in thirty minutes with what's around you.

## Forest Shelters

### Natural Shelters (Found, Not Built)

Before you start building, look around. Nature often provides ready-made shelter that takes zero construction time:

- **Rock overhangs and shallow caves** — instant rain and wind protection. Check for animal occupants, loose rocks above, and drainage (you don't want water pooling under your shelter during rain).
- **Fallen trees** — a large fallen tree with space underneath can serve as a ready-made roof beam. Lean branches against the trunk to create walls.
- **Dense evergreen canopy** — the area beneath a large spruce or fir tree often has dry ground and reduced wind. The lower branches form a natural roof.
- **Hollow trees** — large enough hollows can provide emergency shelter, but check for insects, snakes, and rot.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Spend five minutes scanning your surroundings before you start building. A natural rock overhang or fallen tree might save you an hour of construction — time and energy you can spend on fire, signaling, or water.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Debris Lean-To

The lean-to is the most common survival shelter and can be built almost anywhere with fallen branches and leaves.

**Construction:**

1. Find a ridge pole — a sturdy branch 9–12 feet long and 3–4 inches thick.
2. Prop one end in the crotch of a tree or between two rocks, about 3 feet off the ground. The other end rests on the ground.
3. Lean shorter branches against the ridge pole on both sides (or just the windward side if time is short), spacing them 6–8 inches apart.
4. Layer debris over the frame: leaves, pine needles, grass, bark, ferns — anything that sheds water and traps air. The debris layer should be at least 2–3 feet thick. If you can see daylight through it, add more.
5. Pile a thick layer of debris on the floor for insulation from the ground. This is as important as the roof — you'll lose more heat to the cold ground than to the air.

**Sizing:** Build it just big enough to fit your body. A smaller shelter retains body heat more effectively than a large one.

### A-Frame Shelter

An A-frame is like a lean-to, but with branches on both sides of the ridge pole, creating a tent-like structure.

1. Set up the ridge pole the same way as a lean-to.
2. Lean branches against both sides, creating a triangular cross-section.
3. Leave one end open (the higher end) as your entrance. Face the entrance away from the wind.
4. Cover with thick layers of debris.
5. Insulate the floor generously.

The A-frame is warmer than a lean-to because it encloses more of your body. The trade-off is it takes more materials and time.

### Tree Well Shelter

In a forest with deep snow, the area around the trunk of a large evergreen tree is often free of snow. Snow builds up around the outer branches but can't reach the trunk, creating a natural well.

1. Find a large conifer with low-hanging, snow-laden branches.
2. Carefully enter the tree well without knocking snow off the branches above.
3. Line the floor with evergreen boughs for insulation.
4. The overhanging branches provide a natural roof. You can reinforce them with additional branches or a tarp.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Tree wells can be dangerous for skiers — falling into a deep tree well headfirst is called "snow immersion suffocation" and causes several deaths every year. But for a prepared survivalist who enters carefully, the same tree well becomes a ready-made shelter with excellent insulation.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Snow Shelters

Snow is one of the best insulating materials in nature. A properly built snow shelter maintains an interior temperature near 32°F, regardless of how cold it is outside.

### Snow Trench

The fastest snow shelter to build. Good when you need protection quickly.

1. Dig a trench in the snow about 3 feet wide, 7 feet long, and 3–4 feet deep using a shovel, pot, snowshoe, or even your hands.
2. Cover the trench with branches, a tarp, or snow blocks cut from the surrounding area.
3. Line the floor with evergreen boughs or a sleeping pad for insulation.
4. Leave a small gap at one end for ventilation.

**Time to build:** 30–60 minutes.

### Quinzhee

A quinzhee is a hollowed-out mound of snow — simpler than an igloo and suitable for any type of snow.

1. Pile snow into a mound 6–7 feet high and 8–10 feet in diameter. Pack it lightly.
2. **Let it sinter** (settle and harden) for at least 2 hours. This is critical — fresh snow is too loose to hold a hollow.
3. Push sticks 12–18 inches long into the mound from the outside. These serve as thickness gauges — when you see a stick tip from inside, you've reached the right wall thickness.
4. Hollow out the interior through a small entrance, leaving walls at least 12 inches thick.
5. Poke a ventilation hole in the roof with a stick. This is essential — carbon dioxide from breathing can accumulate in an enclosed snow shelter.
6. Build a sleeping platform higher than the entrance. Warm air rises, so the platform stays warmer than the door opening.

**Time to build:** 3–4 hours (including sintering time).

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
**Ventilation is life-or-death in snow shelters.** Always maintain at least one ventilation hole in the roof. Carbon dioxide is heavier than oxygen and builds up in enclosed spaces. People have suffocated in snow shelters with no ventilation. Check your vent hole regularly — drifting snow can block it.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Snow Cave

A snow cave is dug into a hillside snowdrift and is the warmest snow shelter type.

1. Find a snowdrift at least 6 feet deep on a slope (not under an avalanche-prone area).
2. Dig a tunnel entrance slightly below where your sleeping platform will be.
3. Hollow out a sleeping chamber above the tunnel entrance. Warm air rises and stays in the chamber; cold air sinks and drains out the entrance.
4. Smooth the ceiling to prevent dripping. A dome shape is strongest.
5. Poke ventilation holes. Mark the shelter from outside so others don't walk on the roof.

## Site Selection

Where you build matters as much as what you build.

{{< drg/checklist title="Shelter Site Checklist" subtitle="Evaluate before you build" >}}
- Protected from wind (terrain feature, dense trees, rock wall)
- Not in a drainage path (avoid dry creek beds, low spots that collect water)
- Not under dead branches or standing dead trees ("widowmakers")
- Near a water source but not in a flood zone
- Visible for signaling (or near an open area for signaling)
- Available building materials nearby
- Flat or gently sloped ground
- Not in an avalanche path (for snow shelters)
{{< /drg/checklist >}}
