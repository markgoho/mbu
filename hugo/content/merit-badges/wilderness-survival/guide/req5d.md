{{</* drg/requirement number="5d" */>}}
Describe how to build or find survival shelters in a forest or in snow.
{{</* /drg/requirement */>}}

Shelter is priority #1 in wilderness survival. In cold conditions, an improvised shelter can prevent hypothermia. In hot conditions, shade is essential. In wet conditions, protection from rain is critical. This requirement asks you to describe shelter options in two specific environments: forest and snow.

## Forest Shelter Building

Forests are ideal for shelter-building because they offer materials (branches, leaves, bark) and the trees themselves provide structure.

### **Finding Natural Shelter (Fastest Option)**

Before building, always look for natural shelter:
- **Rock overhang or cave:** Provides ready-made walls and roof.
- **Fallen tree:** Creates a natural ridge to build against.
- **Dense conifer forest:** The thick branches of firs, spruces, and hemlocks naturally shed rain and block wind.
- **Hollow tree:** If large and safe, can provide emergency shelter (careful with animals inside).

If you find good natural shelter, improve it: block the opening with branches, add leaves for insulation, create a bed of leaves to insulate from the ground.

### **Building Forest Shelters**

**Lean-To Shelter (Universal, Fast)**

The lean-to is the standard emergency shelter. It can be built in an hour by one person.

{{</* drg/image src="images/survival-shelter-construction.avif" alt="Step-by-step illustration of lean-to shelter: anchor trees with ridge pole, branches leaned against pole, completed shelter with layered insulation and fire" */>}}

**Steps:**
1. **Find two trees or anchor points** 8–12 feet apart. If no trees, use rocks or large branches to create anchor points.
2. **Lay the ridge pole:** Lash a stout branch between the two trees at about head height (5–6 feet high).
3. **Lean branches against the ridge pole** at an angle. Overlap them densely so rain sheds off the sides instead of pooling.
4. **Create a thick roof:** Layer smaller branches, bark, and leaves on top. The thicker, the better—your roof needs to shed water.
5. **Block the sides:** Add branches and leaves to block wind. Leave the front open (toward a fire) unless wind is extreme.
6. **Insulate the floor:** Pile leaves, pine needles, or grass inside. This insulates you from cold ground and prevents moisture seepage.

**A-Frame Shelter (More Protection)**

An A-frame is a lean-to's more protective cousin. It takes longer but provides full coverage.

**Steps:**
1. **Lash two poles together at the top** (forming an A shape). You can set them on the ground (no trees needed) or suspend them between trees.
2. **Lean branches or a ridgepole across the top** of the A.
3. **Layer branches and leaves** on both sides, creating a tent-like structure.
4. **Leave an opening at one end** for entrance. You can close it with branches at night.
5. **Insulate the interior floor** with leaves and grass.

**Snow Shelter / Debris Hut (Extremely Effective)**

A debris hut is one of the warmest emergency shelters for its size.

**Steps:**
1. **Lay a ridge pole** between two trees, or prop it up on rocks/logs at waist height (3–4 feet).
2. **Lean stout branches against the ridge pole** to create a frame (similar to a lean-to).
3. **Cover the frame with bark, branches, and debris** to weatherproof it.
4. **Pile leaves, pine needles, grass, and snow inside** to create insulation. The debris acts as an insulating blanket.
5. **Create a small entrance** low on the front (easier to heat a small space).
6. **Crawl inside** and make a bed of additional leaves and grass (you want 1–2 feet of insulation under you).

The magic of a debris hut is that you're creating a cocoon of insulation. The trapped air inside warms quickly and stays warm.

## Snow Shelter Building

Snow is one of the best insulators available—it traps air and blocks wind. Snow shelters require snow that's deep enough to dig, but they're phenomenally effective in winter.

{{</* drg/image src="images/snow-cave-shelter.avif" alt="Cross-section of snow cave showing entrance tunnel, elevated sleeping platform, vent hole in ceiling, person sleeping inside" */>}}

### **Finding Natural Snow Shelter (Fastest Option)**

Before building, look for existing shelter:
- **Rock overhang with snow underneath:** Dig out a small cave.
- **Thick conifer tree** with heavy snow on branches: Dig underneath and let the branches form a roof.
- **Steep hillside:** Wind creates natural snow caves on leeward sides (avoid avalanche terrain).

### **Building Snow Shelters**

**Snow Cave (Deep Snow Required)**

A snow cave is the warmest shelter in winter conditions. It requires at least 4–6 feet of stable snow.

**Steps:**
1. **Find a stable snowbank** (on a hillside, not in an avalanche zone).
2. **Dig a horizontal entrance** about 3 feet above the ground level. Dig upward and inward—the entrance should be lower than the sleeping area to prevent warm air from escaping.
3. **Excavate a sleeping chamber** inside, larger than the entrance tunnel.
4. **Create a sleeping platform** higher than the entrance (heat rises and concentrates at the top of the cave).
5. **Vent the roof:** Poke a small hole in the ceiling to allow carbon dioxide to escape (you don't want to suffocate in a sealed cave).
6. **Insulate the sleeping platform:** Use branches, pine boughs, or a sleeping pad—you don't want to sleep directly on cold snow.

A properly built snow cave can keep the interior 30°F warmer than outside air. It's remarkably effective.

**Snow Trench (Less Snow Required)**

If snow is shallow, a snow trench is a fast alternative.

**Steps:**
1. **Dig a trench** in the snow, roughly body-length and body-width, deep as you can.
2. **Create a roof:** Lay branches or snow blocks over the top, leaving a small entrance.
3. **Insulate inside:** Pile branches, boughs, or leaves on the bottom.
4. **Cover the top:** Pack snow around the roof for insulation.

A snow trench is less sophisticated than a cave, but it works when conditions don't permit a full cave.

**Tree Well Shelter (Emergency Shelter)**

On steep mountain slopes, large trees create natural shelters called "tree wells"—the snow melts around the tree trunk in spring/fall or doesn't accumulate heavily due to the tree's insulation.

**Steps:**
1. **Find a large tree** with a deep well around its base.
2. **Clear away excess branches** so you can fit inside.
3. **Pile branches and insulating material** on the ground.
4. **Stay close to the tree trunk** for warmth.

Tree well shelters are not ideal (limited space, branches overhead), but they work in emergencies.

## Choosing Between Forest and Snow Shelter

| Situation | Best Shelter | Why |
|-----------|--------------|-----|
| Dry forest, no snow | Lean-to or A-frame | Fast to build, uses available branches |
| Wet forest or rain expected | Debris hut | Excellent insulation, weatherproof |
| Deep snow, below freezing | Snow cave | Warmth of 30+ degrees above outside |
| Shallow snow, below freezing | Snow trench or tree well | Works with minimal snow depth |
| Forested area with deep snow | Combination: tree well shelter with branches | Insulation from both tree and snow |

## Key Principles for Shelter Building

1. **Work quickly but carefully.** Speed matters when you're stranded, but a collapsed shelter is worse than no shelter.
2. **Insulation from ground up.** You lose as much heat to the ground as to the air. Always insulate your sleeping area.
3. **Seal gaps.** Wind penetration defeats a shelter. Layer materials densely—leaves, branches, bark, snow.
4. **Keep the entrance small.** A large entrance lets heat escape. Make it just big enough to crawl through.
5. **Vent for carbon dioxide.** In sealed shelters (snow caves, underground shelters), ensure air circulation or you risk carbon dioxide poisoning.
6. **Build near a fire if possible.** If you can safely have a fire outside the shelter, place the shelter nearby so the fire's heat warms it.

## Practice Before Emergencies

The time to learn shelter-building is *not* when you're stranded. Build a shelter on a camping trip, even if you don't sleep in it. Practice with your Scout troop. By the time you need an emergency shelter, muscle memory and technique should be automatic.
