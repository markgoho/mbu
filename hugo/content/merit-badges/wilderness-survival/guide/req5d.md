{{< drg/requirement number="5d" >}}
Describe how to build or find survival shelters in a forest or in snow.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A survival shelter doesn't have to be elaborate. It has one job: reduce the rate at which your body loses heat to the environment. The best shelter is the one you can build fastest with what's available — because you might have an hour of light left, or you might have ten minutes.

{{< drg/image src="images/debris-hut-shelter-forest.avif" alt="A well-built debris hut in a forest: a ridgepole propped on a stump with branch ribbing covered in a thick layer of dry leaves, person-sized opening at one end" >}}

## Before You Build: Choose Your Site

Shelter site selection is as important as construction. A poorly chosen site turns a good shelter into a bad situation.

**Avoid:** Low-lying areas that collect cold air and water; exposed ridges with wind; dry riverbeds or flood plains; dead trees (widowmakers) that could fall; areas with signs of wildlife activity.

**Look for:** Natural windbreaks (dense conifers, rock faces, hillside hollows); proximity to dry wood and debris (your building materials); high enough ground to stay dry; a south or east-facing slope in cold conditions (gets morning sun and stays warmer).

## Finding Natural Shelters in a Forest

Before building anything, look for what nature already provides:

**Rock overhangs and cliff faces** offer immediate overhead cover from rain and partial wind protection. Check for loose rock above and water runoff paths before settling in.

**Dense conifer stands** reduce wind significantly and provide overhead canopy. The ground beneath large conifers often stays relatively dry even in rain.

**Fallen logs** can become one wall of a quick lean-to. Find a large fallen log and lean branches against it to form a lean-to frame.

**Root mounds** of fallen trees create ready-made windbreaks and partial enclosures. The root ball can provide overhead cover while the depression beneath provides shelter.

## Building Shelters in a Forest

### The Debris Hut

The debris hut is the gold standard of natural emergency shelter. Built correctly, it maintains warmth with body heat alone — no fire required.

**Construction:**

1. Find a ridgepole 9–10 feet long and about as thick as your wrist. Prop one end on a stump, rock, or forked stick about 2.5 feet off the ground. The other end rests on the ground — this is your entrance.

2. Lean branches (ribbing) along both sides of the ridgepole, creating a triangular tunnel. The width should be just wide enough for your body — a tight fit retains heat far better than a roomy one.

3. Cover the ribbing with smaller sticks and brush to create a lattice frame.

4. Pile debris (dry leaves, grass, pine needles, ferns) over the entire outside until it's 2–3 feet thick. This is the insulation layer. More is better — professional survival instructors say most students don't add nearly enough.

5. Fill the inside with loose dry debris — you'll sleep inside this material like a sleeping bag.

6. Plug the entrance with a large armload of debris once you're inside.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
A debris hut requires a surprisingly large volume of material — experienced instructors estimate it takes about a cubic yard of dry leaves for every foot of your height. On a calm dry night, a well-built debris hut can maintain a temperature 40–50°F warmer than the outside air, with no fire.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

### The Lean-To

A lean-to is faster to build than a debris hut and works well in moderate conditions when combined with a fire. It's not as warm as an enclosed shelter, but it provides significant wind and rain protection.

**Construction:**

1. Find two trees or uprights about 8 feet apart. Lash or prop a horizontal ridgepole between them at about shoulder height.

2. Lean poles or branches from the ridgepole down to the ground at about a 45° angle on the side facing the wind or rain.

3. Weave smaller branches through the leaning poles to create a thatched surface.

4. Cover with debris, bark, large leaves, or conifer boughs until waterproof enough for conditions.

**Fire placement:** Build a fire in front of the lean-to opening, far enough that it doesn't set the shelter on fire. The lean-to reflects radiated heat from the fire back toward you.

## Building Shelters in Snow

Snow contains up to 90% trapped air, making it an excellent insulator. A properly built snow shelter can maintain 32°F inside regardless of outside temperature.

### The Snow Trench

The fastest snow shelter is a simple trench. Dig a body-length, body-width trench as deep as you can — ideally at least 18 inches deep. Cover the top with branches, your skis, poles, a tarp, or any flat material, then pile snow on top for insulation. Block the entrance. Your body heat will gradually warm this small space.

{{< drg/image src="images/snow-trench-cross-section.avif" alt="Cross-section diagram of a snow trench shelter showing a human figure inside the trench, branch roof, entrance, and ventilation hole" >}}

### The Quinzhee

A quinzhee is a hollowed-out pile of snow — more accessible than an igloo and buildable with any snow texture.

**Construction:**

1. Pile snow into a dome about 7 feet tall and 10 feet wide. Pack it to consolidate.

2. Let the pile sit for 2 hours to sinter (the snow crystals bind together), making it strong enough to support itself.

3. Push 12-inch sticks evenly through the entire surface as depth guides.

4. Tunnel in from one side, scooping out snow until you reach the sticks — this ensures uniform 12-inch wall thickness everywhere.

5. Create a sleeping platform raised above the entrance — cold air sinks, so the raised area stays warmer.

6. Poke a small ventilation hole in the roof with a ski pole or stick. This is not optional — without ventilation, carbon dioxide accumulates inside.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Any snow shelter can collapse. Never sleep alone in a snow shelter without a way to signal or dig out. Before sleeping, check that your door hole is small and can be reopened from the inside. Mark the outside with a ski pole or stick so rescuers can find you and so you can orient yourself if the entrance drifts over.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Tree Well Shelter

In deep snow forests, the base of large conifers often remains relatively snow-free due to the protective canopy above. These "tree wells" can be deepened and lined with boughs to create immediate shelter with minimal effort.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Whatever shelter you build, mark its location with something visible from the air or from a distance — a brightly colored piece of clothing, an X pattern made from dark branches on the snow, or any contrasting material. This helps rescuers find you, especially in white winter conditions.
