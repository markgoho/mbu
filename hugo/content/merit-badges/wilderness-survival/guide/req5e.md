{{< drg/requirement number="5e" >}}
Improvise a natural shelter. For the purpose of this demonstration, use techniques that have little negative impact on the environment. Spend a night in your shelter.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This is the requirement that separates reading about survival from experiencing it. Spending a night in a shelter you built yourself teaches you more about shelter construction, thermal regulation, and outdoor resilience than any book or video ever could. It also shows you exactly where your knowledge gaps are — in the most motivating way possible.

## Planning Your Overnight

### Choose Your Shelter Type

Based on what you learned in [Req 5d](/merit-badges/wilderness-survival/guide/req5d/), pick a shelter that matches the season, terrain, and available materials. For most Scouts, a debris lean-to or debris hut works best for a first overnight. Talk with your counselor about which type makes sense for your location and the time of year.

### Minimize Environmental Impact

The requirement specifically asks you to use techniques with little negative impact. This means:

- **Use materials that are already on the ground.** Fallen branches, dead leaves, and loose bark are fair game. Do not cut live trees or strip bark from living plants.
- **Choose a site that is already disturbed** — a cleared area, an existing campsite, or a spot with abundant ground debris.
- **Dismantle your shelter when you are done.** Scatter the debris and return the site to as close to its original condition as possible. Leave No Trace applies even in survival practice.
- **Avoid damaging root systems** by not digging near tree bases.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Collect materials from a wide area rather than stripping one spot bare. Taking a few branches from many fallen trees is less impactful than clearing every bit of debris from one area.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Building for the Night: Practical Tips

The shelters in [Req 5d](/merit-badges/wilderness-survival/guide/req5d/) covered the structural basics. Here are the lessons that experienced shelter-sleepers wish they had known their first night:

### Ground Insulation Is Everything

Most first-timers focus all their energy on the walls and roof, then lie down on bare ground and freeze. The ground pulls heat from your body through conduction — a far more efficient heat transfer than cold air.

Build your ground bed at least 6 inches thick with dry leaves, pine needles, grass, or small branches. If you can lie on it and not feel the ground through it, it is thick enough. If you sink through to dirt, add more. Then add more. You almost certainly need more than you think.

### Smaller Is Warmer

Your body heat is the primary heating system in a debris shelter. A shelter just big enough for you traps that heat efficiently. A shelter big enough for three people but occupied by one person is just a cold room. Build it snug — you should be able to touch both walls while lying down.

### Build Before Dark

Start building at least two hours before sunset. Shelter construction always takes longer than expected, and working in darkness is slow, frustrating, and potentially dangerous. Factor in time to gather materials, build, add ground insulation, and make adjustments while you can still see.

### Test Before Committing

Crawl inside your shelter before nightfall. Check for:
- Drafts — plug gaps with debris
- Water entry points — will rain run inside?
- Comfort — can you lie down fully extended?
- Ground insulation depth — is it enough?
- Headroom — can you sit up to adjust clothing?

## What to Expect

### It Will Be Uncomfortable

Be honest with yourself: your first night in a natural shelter will not be luxurious. You will probably be colder than you expected, hear unfamiliar sounds, and wake up multiple times. This is normal. The goal is not comfort — it is proving to yourself that you can survive a night without a tent and sleeping bag.

### Temperature Management

- Wear all your layers to bed. Remove outer shells only if they are wet.
- Stuff extra debris around and over your body inside the shelter. Think of it as a natural sleeping bag.
- If you have an emergency space blanket, wrap it around yourself with the reflective side inward.
- Eat a high-calorie snack before sleeping — your body burns fuel to generate heat overnight.

### Sounds and Animals

The forest at night is loud. Owls, coyotes, mice, and wind will create a soundscape that can be unsettling if you are not used to it. Remind yourself: none of these sounds are new — you just never noticed them from inside a tent. Your food is stored properly (per [Req 5c](/merit-badges/wilderness-survival/guide/req5c/)), and animals are far more afraid of you than you are of them.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Awake at 2 a.m. and Cold" >}}
You wake up shivering inside your shelter. The fire has died down and the temperature dropped.

- **Step one:** Do not panic. Shivering means your body is still generating heat. Get up and move around to warm up.
- **Step two:** Rebuild your fire if you have materials ready. (Always stage extra firewood before sleeping.)
- **Step three:** Stuff more debris inside your shelter and over your body for additional insulation.
- **Step four:** Eat something if you have food — calories become heat.
- **Step five:** Do some light exercises inside your shelter: leg lifts, arm circles, squeeze your muscles and release. Movement generates warmth.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

## After the Night

Congratulations — spending a night in an improvised shelter is one of the most challenging requirements in all of Scouting. Take time the next morning to reflect on what worked and what you would change:

- Was your ground insulation thick enough?
- Did your shelter block wind effectively?
- Where did water get in (if it rained)?
- What would you build differently next time?

These observations are exactly what your counselor wants to discuss. The learning comes from the experience, not from getting it perfect the first time.

### Clean Up

Dismantle your shelter completely. Scatter debris, return branches to where you found them (or spread them naturally), and leave the site looking undisturbed. Other visitors — and the ecosystem — should never know you were there.
