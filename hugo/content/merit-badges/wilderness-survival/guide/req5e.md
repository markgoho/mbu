{{</* drg/requirement number="5e" */>}}
Improvise a natural shelter. For the purpose of this demonstration, use techniques that have little negative impact on the environment. Spend a night in your shelter.
{{</* /drg/requirement */>}}

This is the hands-on requirement. You've learned *about* shelter building in Requirement 5d. Now you *actually build one and spend a night in it*. This requirement demonstrates that you can execute survival skills, not just talk about them.

## Planning Your Shelter Night

This is a practical, measurable task. Work with your merit badge counselor to plan the event.

### **Choosing the Location**

- **Scout camp or nearby property:** The easiest option. Use a designated area where you have permission to build.
- **Backcountry:** If your counselor approves, you can build in a genuine wilderness setting.
- **Seasonality:** Build your shelter in a season where the overnight conditions are manageable—not extreme cold or extreme heat.

### **Environmental Impact: Building Responsibly**

This requirement specifically requires "techniques that have little negative impact on the environment." This means:

**DO:**
- Use dead branches and fallen wood (already on the ground).
- Use bark from already-fallen trees.
- Pile leaves and pine needles (naturally shed).
- Use rocks and existing features (fallen logs, rock outcrops).

**DON'T:**
- Cut live trees or branches.
- Strip bark from living trees.
- Remove large quantities of moss or lichen.
- Dig into hillsides or remove soil.
- Damage vegetation around the shelter site.

Essentially: use what nature has already discarded. A survival shelter is temporary—leave no trace when you're done.

## What Your Shelter Should Accomplish

Your shelter needs to:
1. **Protect you from weather.** If it's cold, it should insulate. If it's wet, it should shed water. If it's hot, it should provide shade.
2. **Be large enough to sleep in.** You'll spend 6–8 hours inside—adequate space matters.
3. **Have insulation from the ground.** A bed of leaves, pine needles, or branches insulates you from cold ground.
4. **Block wind and rain.** Your roof and walls should shed water and block wind penetration.

## Types of Shelters for This Requirement

### **Lean-To Shelter (Most Popular)**

A lean-to is the standard choice for this requirement because it's:
- Fast to build (2–3 hours)
- Effective (protects from wind and rain)
- Minimally impactful (uses fallen branches)

**Construction Steps:**
1. Find two trees or anchor points 8–12 feet apart.
2. Secure a ridge pole (sturdy branch) between them at chest height.
3. Lean branches against the ridge pole at a 45-degree angle.
4. Layer smaller branches, bark, and leaves densely on top.
5. Leave the front open (toward where your fire will be).
6. Create a bed of insulation inside: pile leaves, pine needles, dry grass 6–12 inches deep.

**Sleeping comfort:** A lean-to with good insulation is surprisingly comfortable. The fire in front provides additional warmth.

### **Debris Hut**

A debris hut is more fully enclosed and provides better insulation in cold conditions.

**Construction Steps:**
1. Lay a ridge pole 3–4 feet off the ground.
2. Lean branches against it to create a frame.
3. Cover densely with bark, branches, and leaves.
4. Create a small entrance at one end.
5. Fill the interior with a deep layer of leaves (you'll burrow into this like a sleeping bag).

**Sleeping comfort:** Debris huts are warm but compact. You'll be tightly enclosed, which concentrates your body heat.

### **Natural Shelter Enhancement**

If you find natural shelter (rock overhang, dense tree grove, fallen tree), you can enhance it:

1. Clear out debris and sharp objects.
2. Block the opening with branches and leaves.
3. Create a bed of insulating material inside.
4. Improve the roof if water leaks in.

This is faster than building from scratch and minimizes environmental impact.

## What to Bring for Your Night

You're not testing survival with zero equipment. You're demonstrating shelter-building and spending a night in your creation. Bring:

- **Sleeping bag or blankets** (for safety and warmth—you're not risking hypothermia for the badge)
- **Pillow** (optional but nice)
- **Flashlight**
- **Water bottle**
- **Snacks**
- **Change of clothes**
- **Toiletries** (toothbrush, etc.)
- **First aid kit**

You're roughing it, but safely. The point is experiencing your shelter, not suffering.

## The Overnight Experience

### **Before Darkness Falls**

- Test your shelter: sit inside, lie down, move around. Is it comfortable? Does anything leak?
- Improve weak spots before dark. It's much harder to fix problems after sunset.
- Arrange your sleeping area: clear out rocks and sticks, add extra insulation.
- Have everything you'll need for the night inside the shelter (flashlight, water, etc.).
- If there's a safe place for a fire (permitted and safe), build it now. A fire provides warmth and psychological comfort.

### **During the Night**

- You won't sleep as well as in your bed—that's normal.
- Listen to the sounds of the wilderness. Get comfortable with them.
- If anything feels wrong (leak, cold, discomfort), adjust your shelter.
- Morning often reveals improvements you could have made. Note them mentally for next time.

### **In the Morning**

- Assess how your shelter performed. What worked? What would you do differently?
- Carefully dismantle your shelter. Leave the site exactly as you found it.
- Pack out everything you brought. Leave no trace.
- Debrief with your counselor: how was the experience? What did you learn?

## What Your Counselor Will Assess

Your counselor will be looking for:
- ✓ **Shelter quality:** Does it protect from weather? Is it well-built and sturdy?
- ✓ **Environmental responsibility:** Did you use fallen materials? Did you leave no trace?
- ✓ **Comfort and safety:** Is the shelter a safe place to sleep? Did you survive the night comfortably?
- ✓ **Initiative:** Did you build the shelter with minimal help, or did you problem-solve as issues arose?
- ✓ **Reflection:** Can you discuss what you learned? What would you do differently?

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

| Mistake | How to Avoid It |
|---------|-----------------|
| Shelter too small to lie down in | Test lying down before committing to the design |
| Roof leaks | Layer materials densely and test with water before night |
| Cold ground under sleeping area | Add 6–12 inches of insulation (leaves, pine needles, grass) |
| Entrance facing wind | Orient the opening away from prevailing winds |
| Building too close to camp | Put shelter 20+ feet from cooking area (fire safety) |
| Failing to leave no trace | Dismantle completely, scatter materials, remove all trash |

## After the Experience

Bring a journal or take notes:
- What type of shelter did you build?
- How long did it take?
- What materials did you use?
- How warm/comfortable was it?
- What would you improve next time?
- What surprised you?

This reflection shows your counselor that you're thinking critically about your performance, not just checking a box.

{{</* drg/be-prepared title="Your First Shelter Night" */>}}
1. **Scout the location early.** Find the best spot before you run out of daylight.
2. **Gather materials first.** Collect all branches, leaves, and insulation *before* you start building.
3. **Build the frame first.** Get the basic structure up, then worry about weatherproofing.
4. **Layer densely.** Thicker roof and thicker insulation = warmer, more comfortable night.
5. **Test thoroughly.** Sit and lie in your shelter. Adjust before dark.
6. **Set up sleeping area last.** Once the shelter is solid, create your sleeping comfort zone.
7. **Don't wait for perfection.** A good shelter built early gives you time to rest. A perfect shelter you build in darkness is worse than no shelter.
{{</* /drg/be-prepared */>}}

## Before You Move On

This requirement is about doing, not just describing. By the time you complete it, you'll have:
- Built a shelter from natural materials
- Spent a night in it
- Learned what works and what doesn't
- Demonstrated responsible environmental practice

That's genuine survival skill—not theory, but experience.
