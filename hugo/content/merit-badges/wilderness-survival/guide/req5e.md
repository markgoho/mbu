{{< drg/requirement number="5e" >}}
Improvise a natural shelter. For the purpose of this demonstration, use techniques that have little negative impact on the environment. Spend a night in your shelter.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This is where theory becomes practice. You'll build an actual shelter using only natural materials and spend a night in it. This is your proof that you can survive—not in an emergency, but by intention and skill.

## Planning Your Shelter Build

**Choose your site wisely:**

1. **Get permission.** Build on private land with permission, a Scout camp, or public land that allows dispersed camping. Check local regulations.

2. **Pick the right location:**
   - Flat or gently sloping ground (not a ridge or valley floor)
   - Near a water source but not on a floodplain
   - Access to dead wood for fuel (dead branches, fallen logs—not living trees)
   - Somewhat open so searchers or rescuers could find you (not deep in dense forest)

3. **Scout the area.** Walk around the site in daylight. Identify hazards (dead trees that might fall, slippery areas, water crossings).

**Choosing your shelter type:**

For most Scouts, a **lean-to or debris shelter** is the best choice because:
- It's buildable in 2–3 hours
- It uses materials found on-site
- It's environmentally low-impact (no trees cut, just dead wood and leaves used)
- It's effective for a single-night stay in mild to moderate conditions

**Estimating materials:**

A shelter for one person needs:
- One ridgepole (6–8 feet long, 2–3" diameter)
- 20–30 smaller branches for framework
- Several armfuls of leaves, pine needles, or other insulating material for the roof and floor
- Possibly a tarp or emergency shelter for waterproofing (reduces environmental impact and increases likelihood of staying warm)

## Building Step-by-Step

**Step 1: Prepare the ridgepole**
Find a sturdy dead branch 6–8 feet long. Test it by applying your weight—it shouldn't bend significantly. If needed, drag it to your intended shelter site.

**Step 2: Position the ridgepole**
Prop the ridgepole 4–5 feet high, supported by:
- Two trees (between two branches or a natural fork)
- Two rocks or logs placed at angles
- A combination of natural features

**Step 3: Build the frame**
Lean branches at an angle against the ridgepole on both sides to create an A-frame shape. Leave a small entrance opening (large enough to crawl through, but small enough to retain heat).

**Step 4: Add insulation material**
Layer leaves, pine needles, bark, moss, and other dead vegetation over the frame until it's 2–3 feet thick. This is your primary insulation—don't skimp. The thicker, the warmer.

**Step 5: Create the floor**
Inside, pile leaves and pine needles 1–2 feet deep to insulate you from the ground. This is as important as the roof insulation.

**Step 6: Weatherproof (optional but recommended)**
If you have a tarp, emergency blanket, or any waterproof material, drape it over the shelter. This provides weatherproofing and dramatically improves comfort.

**Step 7: Final touches**
- Pack extra leaves around the base and sides to block wind
- Arrange materials at the entrance to reduce drafts
- Ensure you can see the sky if possible (for signaling purposes in an emergency)

## The Night In Your Shelter

**What to bring:**

Your shelter might keep you warm, but don't test survival skills in an uncontrolled way. Bring:
- A sleeping bag (you're demonstrating you *can* survive, not that you *have* to suffer)
- A flashlight
- A change of clothes
- A first-aid kit
- Water and snacks
- A fully charged phone or communication device

**Setting up for the night:**

1. **Test the shelter during daylight.** Crawl inside. Sit up. Practice arranging your sleeping bag. Make adjustments.

2. **Gather firewood before dark.** Collect dead branches and establish a small fire area outside the shelter (safely away from the structure and under control).

3. **Light a fire if weather allows.** A small fire outside the shelter entrance warms the opening and boosts morale. Never use an open fire inside—carbon monoxide risk.

4. **Eat before bed.** Eat a meal and drink water. Going to bed hungry and thirsty reduces sleep quality.

5. **Settle in early.** Get into your shelter before full darkness. This gives you time to adjust.

**What to expect:**

- **Sleeping will be uncomfortable.** A natural shelter is not a bed. You'll shift positions frequently. That's normal.
- **It might be colder than you expect.** Insulation is good but not perfect. Your sleeping bag does most of the warming.
- **Sounds will be louder at night.** Wind in branches, small animals moving—all seem magnified. It's normal and not dangerous.
- **You might hear rain.** Even good insulation can show drips. Move slightly to avoid them.

**Morning:**

1. Wake and spend 30 minutes in and around your shelter in daylight, noting what worked well and what you'd improve.
2. Carefully disassemble the shelter, returning materials to the forest (scatter leaves, lean branches back where they fell).
3. Leave no trace: no garbage, no disturbed soil, no cut trees.

## Environmental Stewardship

The requirement specifically asks that you minimize environmental impact. This means:

- **No tools.** Use only hands and fallen materials
- **No trees cut.** All materials should be dead or naturally fallen
- **Leave no trace.** Disassemble completely and restore the area to its natural state
- **No permanent structures.** Your shelter is temporary

This is not just an environmental principle—it's also a legal requirement on public lands, and it's respectful to the land and others who will camp there.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="You Get Uncomfortable During the Night" >}}
You're cold, there's a drip above you, and you regret being here. What do you do?

- **Step 1:** Remember this is temporary. You have a sleeping bag; you'll survive.
- **Step 2:** Move the drip source (adjust branches overhead).
- **Step 3:** Add more leaves over your sleeping bag for insulation.
- **Step 4:** Get up and do light activity (walk around, gather wood) to warm up if really cold.
- **Step 5:** Remind yourself that demonstrating resilience is part of the badge.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

## Documenting Your Achievement

Your merit badge counselor will likely ask:
- "How did you build it?"
- "What materials did you use?"
- "How did you sleep?"
- "What would you do differently?"

Have photos if possible (before, during, after). Be honest about comfort level and what you learned. The counselor isn't looking for "I was perfectly comfortable"—they're looking for evidence that you actually did this and that you understand how shelters work.

## The Bigger Picture

This night proves something important: **you can survive with minimal gear.** You don't need the latest expensive equipment. You need knowledge, skill, and the ability to use what the environment offers. That confidence carries into every outdoor adventure from this point forward.
