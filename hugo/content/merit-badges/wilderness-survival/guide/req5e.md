{{< drg/requirement number="5e" >}}
Improvise a natural shelter. For the purpose of this demonstration, use techniques that have little negative impact on the environment. Spend a night in your shelter.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This is where theory becomes reality. You've read about shelter types in [Req 5d](/merit-badges/wilderness-survival/guide/req5d/). Now you build one, sleep in it overnight, and discover firsthand what works — and what you'd do differently next time.

## Planning Your Shelter Night

### Choose Your Shelter Type

For this requirement, most Scouts build a debris lean-to or A-frame, since these can be constructed from fallen materials with minimal environmental impact. Talk with your merit badge counselor before your shelter night to confirm:

- Which shelter type you'll build
- The location (camp property, a state park with permission, or private land)
- Weather conditions and backup plans
- Safety protocols (nearby adults, emergency communication, etc.)

### Low-Impact Techniques

The requirement specifically says to use techniques with "little negative impact on the environment." This means:

- **Use only fallen branches and deadwood.** Do not cut living trees or break live branches.
- **Gather leaves and debris from the forest floor.** Avoid stripping a single area bare — spread your gathering across a wide area.
- **Do not dig or disturb the soil** unless building a snow shelter in winter.
- **Disassemble your shelter after use** and scatter materials back to where you found them. Leave the site looking like nobody was there.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Scout your building site and available materials during daylight, ideally the afternoon before your shelter night. Knowing where the best ridge poles, debris piles, and windbreaks are saves time and energy during construction.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Building Tips from Experience

These tips come from Scouts who've done this before you — lessons usually learned the hard way:

### The Floor Matters More Than You Think

Most first-time shelter builders put all their effort into the roof and walls, then sleep on bare ground. Ground conduction steals body heat relentlessly. Build your floor insulation at least 6 inches thick — then add more. If you're cold during the night, it's almost always because your ground insulation is too thin, not because your walls aren't thick enough.

### Smaller Is Warmer

Your body heat is your shelter's furnace. A shelter just big enough to lie down in retains that heat. A shelter large enough to sit up in wastes it. Build for sleeping, not for standing.

### Debris Thickness Saves You

For a debris shelter, the wall and roof covering should be 2–3 feet thick. The test: reach your arm into the debris layer. If your arm goes through to the other side, add more. If you can see daylight through any part of the roof, add more. When rain comes, a thin debris layer becomes a shower head; a thick one becomes a waterproof roof.

### Wind Direction Changes

Check the wind when you start building, then build your entrance facing away from it. But know that wind can shift during the night, especially in mountain terrain. If possible, build your entrance facing east (morning sun will warm you) and your back wall facing the prevailing wind direction.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Your First Shelter Night" >}}
Here's a timeline that works well for most Scouts:

- **2:00 PM** — Arrive at site, scout for materials and location
- **2:30 PM** — Begin construction. Build frame first, then pile debris.
- **4:30 PM** — Shelter should be largely complete. Build floor insulation extra thick.
- **5:00 PM** — Gather firewood if a campfire is part of your plan. Start fire.
- **5:30 PM** — Eat dinner, prepare for nightfall
- **6:30 PM** — Final shelter improvements. Add more debris. Test by lying inside.
- **Dark** — Get in your shelter. Adjust as needed. Note what works and what doesn't.
- **Morning** — Evaluate how the night went. What would you change?
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

## What to Bring

This is a survival *exercise*, not an actual emergency. Be smart about safety:

{{< drg/checklist title="Shelter Night Packing List" subtitle="Safety first, even during practice" >}}
- Sleeping bag or blankets (backup warmth if your shelter fails)
- Ground pad or extra tarp (backup insulation)
- Headlamp with fresh batteries
- Water and snacks
- First aid kit
- Warm clothing layers
- Rain gear (just in case)
- Whistle (to signal adult leaders if needed)
- Watch or phone (for time awareness, keep in pocket for warmth)
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

Your counselor may allow or require additional items. The point of this exercise is to practice shelter-building skills, not to endure unnecessary suffering. You should be challenged — but safe.

## After Your Night

Reflect on these questions for your counselor discussion:

- What worked well about your shelter design?
- What would you change if you built it again?
- How did the temperature inside your shelter compare to outside?
- Where did you lose the most heat — roof, walls, or floor?
- How long did it take to build? Could you do it faster next time?
- What was the hardest part — physically or mentally?

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
If at any point during the night you feel genuinely unsafe — severe weather, medical issue, or uncontrollable shivering that won't stop — use your backup gear or signal your adult leaders. This is practice, not an actual survival situation. There is no merit badge worth a trip to the hospital.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

Spending a night in a shelter you built yourself is one of the most rewarding experiences in Scouting. You'll go to sleep wondering if it will work — and wake up knowing it did. That confidence stays with you long after the merit badge is earned.
