{{< drg/requirement number="5e" >}}
Improvise a natural shelter. For the purpose of this demonstration, use techniques that have little negative impact on the environment. Spend a night in your shelter.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This is the most physical requirement in the badge — and the most memorable. Reading about debris huts is one thing. Building one at dusk with tired arms and spending a night inside it is something you'll remember twenty years from now.

This page prepares you to build successfully, sleep comfortably, and leave no trace behind.

{{< drg/image src="images/scout-building-debris-hut.avif" alt="A teenager in work clothes piling armloads of dry leaves over a debris hut frame in a forest clearing, late afternoon light, ridgepole and branch structure visible" >}}

## What "Little Negative Impact" Means

The requirement specifically asks you to use techniques that don't damage the environment. This reflects the Leave No Trace principles that run throughout Scouting.

**Acceptable materials:**
- Deadfall — branches and limbs that have already fallen
- Loose leaves, pine needles, dry grass, bark that's naturally shed
- Downed logs and stumps (but not ones that are clearly wildlife habitat — decomposing logs shelter insects, salamanders, and small mammals)

**Avoid:**
- Cutting live branches or stripping bark from living trees
- Digging into soil or root systems
- Building on fragile vegetation like moss beds or young saplings

**Restoration:** After your overnight, dismantle your shelter and scatter the materials. Leave the site looking as close to its natural state as possible.

## Planning Your Build

### Before You Start

**Estimate the time needed.** A debris hut for one person takes an experienced builder 1–2 hours. A beginner takes 3–4 hours, especially when collecting debris. Start well before dark — rushing a shelter build in failing light leads to a cold night.

**Scout the site first.** Walk the area before committing. Find your ridgepole candidate, note where dry leaves are deepest, identify your windbreak, check for hazards overhead.

**Gather materials before building.** Pile them all in one place first — ridgepole, ribbing branches, lattice material, and the debris itself. Knowing you have enough before you start assembly prevents the frustrating experience of building half a shelter and running out of material.

### The Debris Hut in Practice

Refer to Req 5d for the step-by-step construction method. Here's what first-time builders consistently underestimate:

**Wall thickness.** Two feet of leaves is the minimum. Three feet is better. If you press on the walls and feel little resistance, add more. The insulation value of leaves drops steeply when compressed — pile far more than you think you need.

**Interior fill.** The inside of the shelter is not just where you sleep — it's your sleeping bag. Fill it completely with dry leaves before you get in, and plan to burrow into them. This is where most of the warmth comes from on a cold night.

**Size.** Smaller is warmer. The interior should be just big enough to fit your body plus a few inches. A shelter the size of a small room will never warm up; a shelter that fits you like a sleeping bag bag will.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Bring a stuff sack or garbage bag and fill it with dry leaves to use as a pillow. It keeps your head in place, adds cushioning, and ensures your head stays within the insulating zone rather than sticking out into cold air.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## What to Expect During the Night

### Temperature Management

A well-built debris hut will be noticeably warmer than the outside air. If you wake up too warm, push some debris away from your body. If you're cold, the shelter needs more insulation — but that's harder to fix at midnight, which is why building thick walls from the start matters.

### Noise and Settling

The shelter will make sounds as it settles and as leaves shift. This is normal. You may also hear animal sounds — most are small nocturnal animals like mice or squirrels that are curious about the disturbance in the forest floor. They pose no threat.

### Getting Out and Back In

When you need to get up during the night, your carefully filled entrance will likely scatter. Plan for this — know where your flashlight is and how to refill the entrance when you return.

## Leave No Trace Teardown

After waking, before leaving:

1. Remove your entrance plug and scatter it.
2. Remove and scatter all interior debris.
3. Pull the ribbing branches off the ridgepole.
4. Remove and scatter lattice material.
5. Remove the ridgepole and lay it where you found it or in a natural position.
6. Scatter all remaining debris across the general area — no large piles left.
7. Walk the site and check for anything unnatural: rope, gear, food wrappers.

A good teardown takes 20–30 minutes. The site should look like you were never there.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Your Shelter Isn't Warm Enough at Night" >}}
You built your debris hut, crawled in, and two hours later you're shivering.

- **Step 1:** Don't lie there — cold is progressive and shivering costs calories you need.
- **Step 2:** Get out and improve the shelter while you still have energy. Add debris to the thinnest walls first.
- **Step 3:** If you have a tarp, poncho, or emergency blanket, drape it over the outside of the shelter before re-adding debris — this creates a wind and moisture barrier.
- **Step 4:** Fill the interior more completely with leaves. You should be nearly buried.
- **Step 5:** If improving the shelter isn't possible, move to your warmest available alternative: get in your sleeping bag if you have one, or huddle with others in the group.
