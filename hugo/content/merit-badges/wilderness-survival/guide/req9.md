{{< drg/requirement number="9" >}}
Explain why it usually is not wise to eat wild plants or wildlife in a wilderness survival situation.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Food is the **last priority** in survival (Req 2, Priority #6). You can live for weeks without eating. Yet many people get lost and panic about food, eating unknown plants or attempting to hunt when they should be staying put and signaling for rescue.

The sad truth: **eating the wrong thing often makes a bad situation worse.**

## The Problem with Wild Plants

**Poisonous plants are everywhere.** Thousands of wild plants are toxic, and many look identical to edible plants. Even experienced foragers make mistakes.

**Examples of the danger:**
- **Hemlock vs. wild carrot:** Hemlock is highly poisonous, but its young shoots and roots look almost identical to wild carrot. Eating a small amount of hemlock can kill you.
- **Poison hemlock vs. parsnip:** Same problem—visually nearly identical, vastly different toxicity.
- **Foxglove vs. comfrey:** Foxglove is a deadly cardiac poison; comfrey looks similar and is relatively safe. But how would you know the difference in a panic situation?
- **Mushrooms:** Possibly the most dangerous. There are thousands of species, many with look-alikes. Consuming a single deadly mushroom (like the Destroying Angels) is fatal.

**Even if you survive eating the plant, you might waste energy you can't afford to lose:**
- Spent hours foraging for wild plants that provided minimal calories
- Burned more calories searching than the food provided
- Used time and mental energy that should have gone to building shelter, signaling, or staying calm

## The Problem with Hunting/Trapping Wildlife

**Energy expenditure outweighs gain:**
- Hunting or trapping requires significant energy: making weapons, setting traps, tracking, waiting, and processing the kill
- A small rabbit might provide 150–300 calories, but hunting and processing could burn 500+ calories
- You've made yourself weaker, not stronger

**Danger of injury:**
- Making hunting weapons (spears, traps) from natural materials is inefficient and risks cutting yourself
- Hunting larger animals is dangerous — they can injure or kill you
- In a survival situation, an injury becomes life-threatening

**Psychological burden:**
- Hunting and killing an animal takes emotional energy
- Fear of the animal, guilt after the kill, and focus on processing all distract from the actual task: staying safe and getting found

## Why Modern Wilderness Survival Prioritizes Rescue

The historical understanding of "wilderness survival" was about surviving for months or years in the wild, like a trapper or frontier settler. That's not a realistic modern scenario.

In reality:
1. **Your trip plan means someone knows where you should be** — within a few hours to a day, search teams will be looking for you
2. **Modern rescue is efficient** — hikers are usually found within 24–48 hours
3. **Food is not your bottleneck** — survival for a few days doesn't require food (water and shelter matter; food doesn't)

**The modern survival priority is: stay found, stay safe, wait for rescue.**

## When Food Might Matter

**In extended survival situations (more than 3 days):**
- Energy maintenance becomes important
- Carried emergency rations (in your survival kit) are your food source
- Wild food becomes an option, but only if you're absolutely certain it's safe

**Safe foraging indicators:**
- You have extensive previous knowledge of local plants
- You have a guidebook (yes, really — keep a plant identification guide in your pack)
- You recognize the plant with absolute certainty from multiple identifying features
- You've eaten this plant successfully before
- The plant is abundant (if you make a mistake, you won't starve from losing one source)

**Safer wildlife options (if absolutely necessary):**
- Insects: Some insects are edible (grasshoppers, crickets, ants)
- Worms: Protein source, though low calorie
- Fish: Generally safe if boiled or cooked thoroughly
- Small rodents: Risky because of disease (rabies, plague) — requires cooking to high temperature

But even these should be last resorts, only if you're confident in preparation and staying longer than 3 days.

## A Real-Life Example

**The 2009 Appalachian Trail incident:**

A hiker became lost and disoriented. Instead of staying at his last known location (where his trip plan indicated), he wandered and attempted to forage and trap. He spent days searching for food instead of signaling for rescue. When found, he was severely dehydrated and malnourished — despite the fact that rescue teams would have found him in 24–48 hours if he'd stayed put.

**The lesson:** Panic about food led to poor decisions that nearly killed him.

## What To Eat If You Must

**Your emergency food kit** (part of your survival kit) should include:
- High-calorie emergency rations (energy bars, trail mix)
- These are shelf-stable, lightweight, and safe
- A single energy bar provides 200–400 calories and requires zero preparation

If you've truly been missing for 3+ days and haven't eaten, an energy bar is far safer than any foraged or hunted animal.

## The Rule of Thumb

**If you're in doubt about whether a plant or animal is safe, don't eat it.**

The risk of poisoning (which could kill you, waste your energy treating illness, or require hospitalization you don't have access to) far outweighs the benefit of that meal.

## Preparing for Your Requirement

When you explain this to your merit badge counselor, discuss:

1. **Why food is lowest priority** — you can survive weeks without eating
2. **Specific examples of plant poisoning** — show that even experienced people make mistakes
3. **Energy math** — explain why hunting burns more calories than it provides
4. **Why modern rescue is different from historical survival** — trip plans mean you'll be found within days, not weeks
5. **Safe alternatives** — carried emergency rations eliminate the need for foraging
