Food. Explain why it usually is not wise to eat wild plants or wildlife in a wilderness survival situation.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Survival shows on television have a lot to answer for. Episode after episode, the host triumphantly holds up a grub worm, a handful of berries, or a snake they've just caught — as if foraging was the central skill of wilderness survival. It isn't. And in a real survival situation, foraging wild food often does more harm than good.

{{< drg/image src="images/wild-plant-danger-illustration.avif" alt="Split comparison illustration: edible blueberries with a green circle on the left, toxic pokeweed berries with a red crossed circle on the right" >}}

## Why Food Is the Lowest Survival Priority

As you learned in Req 2, food is Priority 7 — last on the list. A healthy person can survive two to three weeks without eating. In a typical search-and-rescue scenario, you'll be found within hours to a few days. You do not need to eat.

The survival calculus is clear: the risks of foraging — poisoning, wasted energy, time diverted from higher priorities — almost always outweigh the benefit of a few hundred calories.

## The Problem with Wild Plants

### Identification Is Hard — Even for Experts

The most dangerous thing about wild plants isn't that toxic ones look obviously different from edible ones. It's that they often look exactly the same. Deadly mistakes made by experienced outdoorspeople include:

**Water hemlock (*Cicuta* species):** One of the most violently toxic plants in North America. Roots look similar to wild parsnip or water parsley — plants that look edible. A small amount causes grand mal seizures and can kill in hours.

**Pokeweed (*Phytolacca americana*):** Dark berries that look superficially like elderberries or blueberries. Every part of this plant is toxic. Berries eaten raw have caused fatalities in children.

**Death camas (*Anticlea* species):** Bulbs look like wild onions. Unlike wild onions, death camas has no onion smell. The mistake is common, and the plant is highly toxic.

**Fool's watercress, fool's parsley:** Multiple species in the carrot family (*Apiaceae*) include edible plants alongside deadly ones that look nearly identical. Without expertise, you cannot reliably tell them apart.

### Energy Cost Versus Caloric Return

Even when plants are correctly identified as edible, the caloric return from backcountry foraging is often poor. Wild berries, for example, require significant time to collect enough for meaningful calories. The energy burned walking and collecting frequently exceeds the calories obtained. This is acceptable in a multi-week wilderness situation. In a two-day SAR scenario, it's a net negative.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The "universal edibility test" — often taught in survival courses — involves isolating plant parts and testing them on skin, lips, and tongue over several hours before eating a small amount. The full test takes eight hours per plant part. In a typical survival situation of 24–72 hours, this test consumes most of your remaining time before rescue.

## The Problem with Wild Animals and Insects

### Catching Them Takes More Than It Gives

A squirrel provides about 350 calories. Chasing and catching a squirrel burns hundreds of calories and hours of effort. Trapping is more efficient, but constructing effective traps takes time, knowledge, and materials — and there's no guarantee any trap will catch anything. In a short-duration survival situation, the math rarely works in your favor.

### Disease and Preparation Risks

Wild animals carry parasites and diseases. Eating undercooked wild meat risks *Trichinella* (roundworm from mammals), *Campylobacter* (from poultry), *Tularemia* (from rabbits), and *Salmonella* (from reptiles). Cooking thoroughly addresses most risks — but that requires confirmed fire and cooking vessels, adding more complexity to an already complex situation.

### Insects Are the Exception — With Caveats

Insects are nutritionally dense and calorie-efficient. Grasshoppers, crickets, and mealworm-type larvae are commonly consumed in survival situations. However:

- Avoid brightly colored insects (warning coloration often signals toxicity).
- Avoid insects that sting or bite.
- Remove legs, wings, and heads from larger insects.
- Cook them if possible.

Even here, in a short SAR scenario, the psychological cost (most people will not eat insects) may outweigh the caloric benefit.

## The Exceptions

The reasoning above applies to short-duration survival situations — the ones this badge prepares you for. In a genuine multi-week situation where rescue is genuinely uncertain, foraging becomes more important and the calculus changes. But that situation is rare, and it requires extensive prior knowledge of local edible plants, not hasty identification under stress.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Someone in Your Group Wants to Eat Wild Berries" >}}
You're on day two of a survival situation and a fellow Scout spots some berries.

- **Step 1:** Stop them before they eat. In the first two days of a survival situation, the nutritional benefit of berries is minimal — the risk is not.
- **Step 2:** Explain: we don't know what these are, and misidentification can cause vomiting and diarrhea, which worsens dehydration — the real threat right now.
- **Step 3:** Redirect energy toward high-priority tasks: improving the signal fire, treating water, and maintaining the shelter.
- **Step 4:** Use your emergency food from your survival kit (Req 4). That's what it's for.
