{{< drg/requirement number="9" >}}
Food. Explain why it usually is not wise to eat wild plants or wildlife in a wilderness survival situation.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This requirement surprises many Scouts. Television survival shows make foraging and hunting look like essential wilderness skills — the survivalist eating grubs, catching fish with a sharpened stick, and identifying edible berries. The reality is different. In most survival situations, attempting to eat wild plants or animals creates more risk than it eliminates.

## The Case Against Wild Foraging

### You Can Survive Weeks Without Food

Remember the Rule of Threes from [Req 2](/merit-badges/wilderness-survival/guide/req2/): you can survive approximately three weeks without food. Most wilderness survival situations last hours or days, not weeks. The average search and rescue response time in the U.S. is under 24 hours when a trip plan has been filed. Hunger is uncomfortable, but it is not going to kill you in a two-day survival scenario.

The calories you burn hunting, trapping, and foraging are often greater than the calories you gain — a net energy loss that accelerates your decline.

### Misidentification Can Kill

Many toxic plants look nearly identical to edible ones. The differences can be subtle — a slightly different leaf shape, a variation in stem color, or the presence of tiny hairs. Even experienced botanists have been poisoned by misidentified plants.

Some examples that illustrate the danger:

- **Water hemlock** looks similar to wild parsnip and Queen Anne's lace. It is one of the most violently toxic plants in North America — a single bite of the root can cause seizures and death within hours.
- **Death cap mushrooms** (*Amanita phalloides*) resemble several edible species. They account for more than 90% of fatal mushroom poisonings worldwide. Symptoms do not appear until 6–12 hours after eating, by which time the liver damage has already begun.
- **Holly berries, pokeweed berries, and nightshade** look appealing but cause severe gastrointestinal distress or worse.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
The universal rule in wilderness survival is this: **if you are not 100% certain a plant is safe to eat, do not eat it.** "Pretty sure" and "it looks like the picture in a book" are not good enough. A single misidentification can cause vomiting, diarrhea, organ failure, or death — any of which will make your survival situation dramatically worse.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Illness Compounds Your Problems

Even if you correctly identify an edible plant, eating it carries risks:

- **Allergic reactions** — you may be allergic to a plant you have never tried before. In the backcountry without medical care, anaphylaxis is potentially fatal.
- **Gastrointestinal distress** — unfamiliar foods can cause nausea, vomiting, and diarrhea even if they are technically safe to eat. Vomiting and diarrhea accelerate dehydration — the very condition you are trying to prevent.
- **Parasites** — raw wild meat, fish, and insects can carry parasites that cause severe illness. Cooking kills most parasites, but thorough cooking requires a sustained fire and a container, which you may not have.

The death cap mushroom (*Amanita phalloides*) is responsible for more fatal poisonings than all other mushroom species combined. It has a pleasant taste, which means victims do not realize anything is wrong until symptoms appear hours later — by then, liver damage may already be irreversible.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## What About Hunting and Trapping?

The same logic applies to wildlife. Hunting and trapping in a survival situation present several problems:

- **Energy expenditure** — setting snares, building traps, and processing game burns significant calories. Unless you are an experienced trapper, the energy cost usually exceeds the return.
- **Injury risk** — improvised hunting tools (sharpened sticks, thrown rocks) are ineffective and dangerous. Cutting yourself with an improvised knife far from medical care can be life-threatening.
- **Disease** — wild game can carry diseases including rabies, tularemia, and various parasites. Raw or undercooked meat is especially risky.
- **Legal issues** — even in a survival situation, hunting without a license and out of season creates legal complications. More practically, you are unlikely to succeed without proper equipment and experience.

## When Foraging Might Make Sense

There are limited exceptions where the benefit outweighs the risk:

- **Extended survival (beyond a week)** where rescue is not imminent and starvation becomes a real threat
- **Known, easily identified foods** — blackberries, blueberries, and raspberries are distinctive enough that misidentification is unlikely in their native range
- **Fishing** (where legal and practical) — fish are relatively easy to catch with improvised gear and provide excellent nutrition when cooked thoroughly
- **Insects** — many insects are edible and high in protein (grasshoppers, crickets, ants), though cultural squeamishness and the small caloric yield make this impractical for most people

## The Better Strategy

Instead of foraging, focus your energy on the things that actually keep you alive in a short-term survival situation:

1. **Maintain your shelter** — staying warm saves more calories than food provides
2. **Stay hydrated** — water is far more urgent than food
3. **Conserve energy** — rest when you can; do not waste calories on low-probability hunting
4. **Signal for rescue** — getting found is the real solution

If you packed trail food as part of your Scout Essentials (as discussed in [Req 1b](/merit-badges/wilderness-survival/guide/req1b/)), ration it carefully. Small portions of high-calorie food at regular intervals maintain energy and morale better than consuming everything at once.

{{< drg/tip >}}
The best "wild food" strategy is one you execute before the trip: pack more trail food than you think you need. An extra handful of nuts or an additional energy bar weighs almost nothing and can sustain you through an unplanned night in the woods.
{{< /drg/tip >}}
