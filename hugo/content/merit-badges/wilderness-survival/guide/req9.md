Food. Explain why it usually is not wise to eat wild plants or wildlife in a wilderness survival situation.
{{</* /drg/requirement */>}}

Many Scouts assume that in a survival situation, they'll find food by foraging for plants or hunting wildlife. The reality is far different. Wild foods are unreliable, dangerous, and consume more energy to obtain than they provide. This requirement teaches you why foraging is a survival myth—and what you should focus on instead.

## Why Wild Plants Are Dangerous

Identifying edible plants in the wilderness is much harder than it looks. Even experienced botanists can make mistakes, and one mistake can mean poisoning.

### **The Identification Problem**

Many poisonous plants look nearly identical to edible ones. Examples:

- **Water hemlock** (deadly poisonous) looks similar to wild carrot and parsnip
- **Poison hemlock** (toxic) resembles wild parsnip and fennel
- **Death cap mushrooms** are commonly mistaken for edible mushrooms
- **Foxglove** (digitalis—causes heart failure) looks like comfrey in early growth
- **Oleander** (all parts poisonous) grows in warm regions and is easily confused with other shrubs

{{</* drg/safety-first */>}}
In a survival situation, you have no access to field guides, poison control experts, or time to verify. A single plant you can't confidently identify should never enter your mouth. The risk is too high.
{{</* /drg/safety-first */>}}

### **Symptoms of Plant Poisoning**

If you eat the wrong plant, symptoms may appear within minutes or take hours:

- **Gastrointestinal:** Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhea
- **Neurological:** Confusion, seizures, hallucinations, loss of consciousness
- **Cardiac:** Rapid heartbeat, heart rhythm problems, heart failure
- **Respiratory:** Difficulty breathing

In a wilderness survival situation with no medical care nearby, serious poisoning means death.

### **The Energy Cost**

Even if you correctly identify an edible plant, gathering enough calories to sustain yourself is exhausting:

- **Acorns** require leaching and grinding (hours of labor) to remove tannic acid and become edible
- **Cattail roots** must be dug, washed, and processed
- **Pine nuts** require locating trees, identifying ripe cones, and cracking open seeds
- **Berries** provide quick calories but are seasonal and scattered

**The math:** If you spend 4 hours gathering plants to obtain 500 calories, but you burn 2,000 calories in those 4 hours, you've created a deficit. You're weaker, not stronger.

In short-term survival (the first 3–5 days), expending energy on food gathering is a losing strategy. In a rescue scenario, rescuers will find you faster if you stay put and signal rather than exhaust yourself foraging.

## Why Hunting and Fishing Are Impractical

Many Scouts imagine themselves hunting rabbits or fishing for trout. The reality of wilderness survival is different.

### **Hunting Challenges**

- **You likely have no weapons.** Scouts don't carry guns or bows on camping trips.
- **Traps and snares take time.** Building an effective trap requires materials, knowledge, and patience. A basic deadfall trap takes 1–2 hours to construct, and most fail.
- **Tracking and stalking are skills.** Approaching an animal without startling it requires camouflage, wind awareness, and practice. Most hunters spend hours per animal.
- **Processing takes skill and tools.** Gutting, butchering, and cooking game requires sharp knives and knowledge of food safety.
- **Game is seasonal and unpredictable.** You can't count on finding large animals when you need them.

**Time cost:** Even experienced hunters spend hours stalking one animal. In a survival situation, that time is better spent on shelter and signaling.

### **Fishing Challenges**

- **Fishing requires equipment.** You need hooks, line, or a net. Making a primitive hook takes time and materials.
- **Fish populations vary.** Not all streams have fish. Not all fish are edible.
- **Fishing takes patience.** You might cast a line for hours and catch nothing.

**Result:** Fishing is a supplementary food source, not a survival strategy.

## The Survival Priority System (Revisited)

Remember the **survival priorities** from Requirement 2:

1. **Shelter** — Prevents exposure
2. **Signal** — Gets rescue
3. **First Aid** — Prevents injury complications
4. **Water** — Prevents dehydration (you can survive 3–5 days without it)
5. **Fire** — Warmth, morale, water purification
6. **Food** — Prevents starvation (you can survive 3 weeks without it)
7. **Morale** — Keeps you thinking clearly

**Food is #6**, not #1. In a short-term survival scenario (typical lost-Scout scenario), you'll be rescued within 3–5 days. You can survive that long without food, but not without shelter and signaling.

{{</* drg/did-you-know */>}}
Survival instructors often say: "Starving scouts are rarely rescued. Hypothermic scouts are never rescued." The first priority is staying alive through exposure and cold—not finding food.
{{</* /drg/did-you-know */>}}

## The Real Food Strategy in Survival

If you must address food in a wilderness survival situation:

**Day 1–3:** Focus on shelter, signaling, and water. Food is not necessary.

**Day 4+:** If rescue is delayed:
- **Gather nuts and seeds** if they're abundant and easily identified (acorns, hickory nuts in fall)
- **Fish if you have time** (set a simple trap and check it while doing other tasks)
- **Hunt small game** only if you have equipment and skill
- **Never experiment** with unfamiliar plants

**Best sources of calories (low risk):**
- **Cattail** — rhizomes are starchy and relatively safe
- **Pine nuts** — nutritious, but time-consuming to crack
- **Hickory nuts** — high in fat and calories
- **Walnuts** — rich in protein
- **Berries** — safe if you're 100% certain of identification (blueberries, blackberries, raspberries in their season)

None of these are quick, and all require energy to gather and process.

## Why This Requirement Matters

This requirement teaches **realistic survival thinking**. A Scout who wastes hours trying to catch fish or hunting for game while ignoring shelter and signaling has misunderstood survival priorities.

Rescue is the goal. Food can wait.

## Before You Move On

Your counselor will ask: "Why shouldn't you eat wild plants in a survival situation?" Be prepared to explain:

- **Identification risk:** Plants can be poisoned, and you can't verify with field guides
- **Energy loss:** Gathering takes more calories than you'll obtain
- **Time cost:** Hours spent foraging are hours not spent signaling or sheltering
- **Low priority:** Food is #6 in survival priorities; you'll be rescued before starvation becomes critical

Explain that in a true survival scenario, you'd focus on the first five priorities and only consider food if rescue takes more than 3–5 days. Show that you understand the **real threat** is exposure, dehydration, and despair—not hunger.
