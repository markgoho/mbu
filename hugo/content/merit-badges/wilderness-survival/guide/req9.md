{{< drg/requirement number="9" >}}
Food. Explain why it usually is not wise to eat wild plants or wildlife in a wilderness survival situation.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Survival shows make foraging look easy — a few berries here, a grub there, maybe a fish on a pointed stick. Reality is far less romantic. In most wilderness survival situations, eating wild plants or animals is a net loss. The risks and energy costs outweigh the calories gained.

## The Energy Equation

Your body has reserves. A healthy person can survive three weeks or more without food. Most survival situations are resolved within 72 hours — long before starvation becomes a real threat. The calories you already have stored in your body fat and muscles are enough to sustain basic survival activities for days.

Now consider what foraging costs you:

- **Time** spent searching for food is time not spent building shelter, tending fire, or signaling
- **Energy** burned hiking, digging, and gathering often exceeds the calories you collect
- **Water** — digesting food requires water, which may already be scarce
- **Risk** — a wrong identification can poison you, creating a medical emergency with no hospital nearby

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The average adult has about 100,000 calories of stored energy in body fat — enough to survive for over a month without eating. Meanwhile, many edible wild plants provide fewer than 50 calories per serving. The math doesn't favor foraging in a short-term survival situation.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Why Wild Plants Are Dangerous

### Misidentification

This is the biggest risk. Many edible plants have toxic look-alikes that are difficult to distinguish without expert knowledge:

- **Wild carrot** (edible) looks nearly identical to **poison hemlock** (fatal). The difference is subtle — hemlock has purple blotches on its stem, but stressed or young plants may not show this clearly.
- **Edible elderberries** look similar to **water hemlock berries** (extremely toxic).
- **Wild onion** (edible) can be confused with **death camas** (fatal). Both grow in similar habitats and have similar leaves.

Even experienced botanists make identification mistakes in the field. Under the stress, fatigue, and poor light of a survival situation, your chances of correctly identifying a plant drop dramatically.

### Delayed Symptoms

Many plant toxins don't cause immediate symptoms. You might eat a handful of berries, feel fine for hours, and then experience severe vomiting, diarrhea, or organ failure — at which point you're worse off than if you'd eaten nothing.

### Allergic Reactions

Even plants that are generally safe can cause allergic reactions in some individuals. In the backcountry, an allergic reaction — especially one involving throat swelling — is a life-threatening emergency.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
There is no reliable "test" for whether a wild plant is safe to eat. The idea that you can rub a plant on your skin, wait, then taste a small amount and wait again (the "Universal Edibility Test") is unreliable and can expose you to toxins that take hours or days to show effects. The only safe approach is positive identification by an expert — and even experts get it wrong.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Why Wild Animals Are Risky

### Hunting Is Calorie-Expensive

Setting snares, building traps, and pursuing game burns significant calories. In survival studies, most untrained people who attempt to hunt or trap animals in the wild spend more energy than they gain — a net caloric loss.

### Disease and Parasites

Wild game can carry diseases (tularemia, hantavirus, plague) and parasites (tapeworms, roundworms, trichinosis) that require thorough cooking to eliminate. In a survival situation with limited fire control, undercooking is a real risk.

### Injury Risk

Attempting to catch or kill animals — especially larger ones — risks injury from bites, scratches, or falls. A laceration that gets infected in the backcountry can become a serious medical problem.

### Legal Considerations

In most areas, hunting without a license is illegal, even in a survival situation. While legal protection may apply in a genuine life-or-death emergency, this underscores how unusual it is to actually *need* wild food.

## When Food IS a Priority

Food becomes important in **extended survival situations** — those lasting more than a week with no prospect of imminent rescue. In these rare cases:

- **Insects** are the safest, most energy-efficient wild food source. Grubs, ants, and grasshoppers are calorie-dense and easy to find. Cook them to kill parasites. Avoid brightly colored insects (warning coloration often means toxic).
- **Fish** are relatively safe if you can catch them and cook them thoroughly. A hook and line from your survival kit is more efficient than improvised methods.
- **Known edible plants** — if you have absolute, positive identification from prior training (not a field guide skimmed in an emergency), certain plants are safe. But this requires extensive pre-existing knowledge.

{{< drg/tip >}}
The best way to handle food in a survival situation is to carry it with you in the first place. High-calorie trail food (nuts, energy bars, jerky) in your pack and survival kit eliminates the need to forage entirely. An extra 500 calories of trail food weighs a few ounces and covers you for days.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## The Bottom Line

| Factor | Foraging | Using Stored Body Energy |
|--------|----------|--------------------------|
| **Risk** | High (poisoning, injury, disease) | Zero |
| **Energy cost** | High (searching, catching, preparing) | Zero |
| **Water requirement** | Increases (digestion requires water) | Minimal |
| **Time cost** | Hours of effort | Zero — time used for shelter, fire, signaling |
| **Reliability** | Unpredictable | Guaranteed for 2–3 weeks |

In a short-term survival situation, your priorities are shelter, fire, water, and signaling — not food. Your body has the reserves to carry you through. Spend your energy getting rescued, not getting poisoned.
