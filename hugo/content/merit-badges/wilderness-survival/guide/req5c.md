Explain how to protect yourself from bears and raccoons.

Bears and raccoons are the two most common wildlife hazards at North American campsites — and they're almost always after the same thing: your food. Understanding their behavior lets you prevent most encounters rather than respond to them.

{{< drg/image src="images/bear-canister-and-hung-food-bag.avif" alt="A hard-sided bear canister sitting on a granite boulder in a forest camp setting" >}}

## Bears: Prevention First

The most important thing to know about bears is that the vast majority of bear encounters at campsites happen because of one thing: food or scented items were stored improperly.

**Bears are motivated, not malicious.** A black bear that gets into your food cache isn't being aggressive — it's foraging. The problem is that food-conditioned bears (those who have learned humans = food) become increasingly bold, creating genuine safety risks and usually ending in the bear being killed. Protecting your food protects both you and the bear.

### Food Storage

**Bear canisters** are hard-sided containers that bears cannot open or dent. They're required in many National Parks and wilderness areas. Store them at least 200 feet from your campsite, away from water sources, not near cliff edges.

**Hanging food** ("bear hang") is an alternative where a canister isn't required. Use the PCT method or ranger-recommended approach: hang food at least 12 feet off the ground and 6 feet from the tree trunk and any branch, using at least a 2-inch diameter branch.

**Keep a clean camp:** Cook and eat at least 200 feet from your sleeping area. Store all scented items (food, trash, toothpaste, sunscreen, lip balm, soap, and even chapstick) in your bear canister or hang, not in your tent. Pack out all trash.

{{< drg/checklist title="Clean Camp Checklist" subtitle="Do this before sleeping and when leaving camp" >}}
- Food stored: In bear canister or properly hung, 200+ feet from tent.
- Cookware stored: Packed into food storage area or hanging — smells linger.
- Trash stored: All trash goes with food — burns attract bears too, don't burn food scraps.
- Personal items stored: Toothpaste, sunscreen, deodorant, lip balm in canister.
- Tent interior: No food, wrappers, or scented items inside.
- Clothes: Don't sleep in the same clothes you cooked in if they're heavily scented.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

### If You Encounter a Bear

Most black bear encounters at campsites are defensive or curious rather than predatory. The general approach:

**Don't run.** Running triggers a pursuit instinct in any predator. Stand your ground.

**Make yourself large and make noise.** Raise your arms, speak in a firm, calm voice. If you're in a group, cluster together.

**Back away slowly.** Give the bear an escape route. Most bears want to leave — let them.

**For a charging bear:** Most bear charges are bluffs. If a bear charges, stand your ground, yell, and use bear spray if you have it. Bear spray has been shown in studies to be more effective than firearms in bear encounters.

**Grizzly vs. black bear:** If you're in grizzly country (Northern Rockies, parts of Canada), the response to an attacking grizzly is different — play dead in a face-down position with hands protecting the back of your neck. For a black bear attack (much rarer), fight back aggressively.

## Raccoons: Persistent and Clever

Raccoons don't carry the life-threatening risk of bears, but they're remarkably good at getting into food — and they're found in nearly every camping area in North America.

Raccoons are nocturnal, dexterous, and persistent. They can unzip bags, untie knots, open lightweight coolers, and will investigate anything that smells like food. A raccoon that successfully raided a campsite will return repeatedly.

### Protection Strategies

**Same principles as bears, smaller scale:** Store food in a bear canister or hanging. Even car camping: food in a hard-sided cooler in the trunk, not in soft bags in the passenger compartment or truck bed.

**Inspect your campsite:** Raccoons are often already in camp before you notice them, especially at established campsites where they've been rewarded before. Keep food and scented items secured from the moment you arrive.

**Don't feed them.** This seems obvious, but "cute" raccoons at picnic areas become habituated campers who lose their fear of humans. A habituated raccoon is a nuisance at best and a bite risk at worst.

### If Raccoons Visit at Night

Make noise — clap, shout, bang pots. Raccoons are less committed than bears; they'll usually retreat when confronted with loud noise. If one gets into your food, accept the loss, secure what remains, and don't grab for the food — raccoons can bite hard and, as noted in Req 1a, wild mammal bites carry rabies risk.
