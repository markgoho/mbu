Explain how to protect yourself from bears and raccoons.

Wildlife encounters are rare, but bears and raccoons are the most common concerns in North American backcountry. Fortunately, both animals are predictable: they're searching for food, not looking to hunt humans. Understanding how these animals think and behave is how you prevent dangerous encounters.

## Bears: Behavior and Prevention

There are three types of bears in North America: black bears (most common), grizzly bears (northern Rockies and Alaska), and polar bears (Arctic). This guide focuses on black bear safety, which covers most U.S. hiking situations. Grizzly bear country requires additional precautions beyond the scope of this badge.

### **Why Bears Matter**

Bears are not aggressive predators toward humans by nature. They're food-motivated. A bear looking for an easy meal will investigate a campsite. A surprised bear (especially a mother protecting cubs) may attack. **The goal is to avoid surprising bears and to avoid leaving food accessible.**

{{</* drg/image src="images/bear-food-storage-hanging.avif" alt="Diagram showing proper food storage hanging technique: food bag suspended 12 feet high from rope between two trees" */>}}

### **Bear Prevention: Before You Go**

1. **Research bear activity.** Check with the local Forest Service or park ranger station. Are bears active in the area you're hiking? Are there recent sightings?
2. **Hike in groups.** Bears avoid humans. A group of humans making noise is less likely to surprise a bear.
3. **Make noise while hiking.** Talk, use a whistle, clap occasionally. A surprised bear is a defensive bear. A bear that hears you coming will usually move away.

### **Bear Prevention: At Camp**

**Food Storage is Everything:**
- **Hang food 12 feet high and 10 feet away from trees.** Use a rope to suspend your food bag from a tree branch. A bear can't climb a thin rope or reach the bag.
- **Use bear canisters if required.** Some areas require bear canisters (hardened containers bears can't open). Follow local regulations.
- **Store *everything* that smells:** Food, trash, toiletries, cooking gear, garbage. A bear has an incredible sense of smell. Sunscreen, toothpaste, and soap attract bears.
- **Never cook or eat in your tent.** Cooking smells linger and invite bears. Eat at least 100 feet from where you sleep.
- **Clean up thoroughly.** Wash dishes immediately. Wipe down cooking surfaces. No food scraps should remain.

**Campsite Setup:**
- **Put tents upwind of your cooking area.** The scent of food travels downwind. If you're upwind, bears smelling food might approach from the opposite direction of your tents.
- **Choose an open campsite.** Avoid dense brush or areas where a bear could hide. You want maximum visibility so you see a bear approaching.

### **If You Encounter a Bear**

**DON'T:**
- Run. Bears can run 30mph. You can't outrun them.
- Climb a tree. Black bears are excellent climbers. This doesn't help.
- Play dead. This is grizzly bear advice, not black bear.
- Feed the bear. An fed bear is a dead bear (wildlife agencies kill habituated bears).

**DO:**
- **Stay calm.** Easier said than done, but panic makes things worse.
- **Make yourself look big.** Stand tall, raise your arms, open your jacket.
- **Make noise.** Yell, shout, clap. You want the bear to know you're human (not prey) and that you're here.
- **Back away slowly.** Don't turn and run. Move backward while keeping eyes on the bear. Let it leave.
- **If the bear charges or attacks:** Fight back. Use whatever tools you have—rocks, stick, knife, bear spray. A black bear attacking is unusual and terrifying, but you *can* defend yourself.

## Raccoons: Behavior and Prevention

Raccoons are far less dangerous than bears but more likely to invade your campsite. They're intelligent, clever, and relentless in pursuing food.

### **Why Raccoons Matter**

Raccoons don't attack humans, but they will ransack an unprotected camp. A raccoon can open coolers, tear open food bags, and create a dangerous mess. Additionally, raccoon feces can contain dangerous parasites. You want to prevent raccoon contact entirely.

### **Raccoon Prevention: At Camp**

**Food Storage:**
- **Hang food as you would for bears.** If you're already hanging food to bear-proof your camp, it's automatically raccoon-proof.
- **Use sealed containers.** Raccoons can tear into soft bags but struggle with hard plastic coolers or bear canisters.
- **Never leave food unattended at camp.** Even while cooking, don't set food down and walk away.

**Campsite Setup:**
- **Don't cook near where you sleep.** Cooking smells attract raccoons (same as bears).
- **Keep a clean camp.** Wipe down surfaces, clean dishes immediately, and don't leave garbage lying around.
- **Remove garbage completely.** Raccoons will chew through trash bags. Burn trash or pack it out.

### **If a Raccoon Approaches Your Camp**

**DON'T:**
- Let it get habituated to your camp. A raccoon that steals from you once will come back.
- Try to feed or pet it. Raccoons bite, and their bite can transmit rabies.
- Trap or harm it (it's illegal in most areas).

**DO:**
- **Make loud noises.** Yell, bang pots, clap. Raccoons are small and easily frightened. Noise drives them away.
- **Shine a light.** Raccoons are nocturnal. A flashlight in the eyes startles them.
- **Pick up food immediately.** If you're eating, finish quickly and store food.
- **Stay between the raccoon and your food.** Block its path to food stores.

## The Golden Rule: Food = Survival

Whether you're dealing with bears, raccoons, or other wildlife, the principle is the same: **Properly stored food prevents wildlife conflicts.** A locked cooler hanging from a tree isn't romantic, but it keeps you safe and keeps wildlife from becoming habituated to human food (which often results in the animal being killed by wildlife managers).

### **Universal Food Storage Checklist**

- [ ] Food in a bag or container
- [ ] Bag hanging 12 feet high, 10 feet from tree
- [ ] Cooking area at least 100 feet from tents
- [ ] All trash stored with food
- [ ] Toiletries and soap stored with food
- [ ] Dishes cleaned immediately
- [ ] No food scraps left in camp
- [ ] Camp in an open area with visibility

{{</* drg/tip */>}}
In established campgrounds, use bear boxes if provided. They're purpose-built and don't require climbing trees or rigging ropes. In backcountry areas, practice hanging food bags before you need to do it in the dark.
{{</* /drg/tip */>}}

## Before You Move On

Your counselor will ask: "How do you protect your food from bears? What would you do if a bear approached your camp?" Be ready with specific, practical answers. Explain the logic: bears are food-motivated, so proper food storage prevents encounters. If an encounter happens, your response is to appear large, make noise, and back away—all behaviors that de-escalate the situation.
