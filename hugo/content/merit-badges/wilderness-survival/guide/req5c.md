{{< drg/requirement number="5c" >}}
Explain how to protect yourself from bears and raccoons.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Bears and raccoons are the two animals most likely to invade your camp and cause problems. Both are intelligent, motivated by food, and far more capable of getting into your stuff than most Scouts expect. Bear encounters can be life-threatening; raccoon encounters, while less dangerous, can destroy food supplies, damage gear, and create unsanitary conditions.

## Bear Safety

Three species of bear live in North America: black bears (the most common and widespread), grizzly bears (found in the northern Rockies, Alaska, and parts of the Pacific Northwest), and polar bears (Arctic regions only). Most Scouts will encounter black bears; some may encounter grizzlies.

### Preventing Bear Encounters

Prevention is everything. Bears that never smell your food rarely visit your camp.

**Food storage:**
- **Bear canisters** — rigid, lockable containers approved for bear country. Required in many national parks and wilderness areas. Store all food, scented items (toothpaste, sunscreen, lip balm), and trash inside.
- **Bear hang (PCT method)** — hang your food bag from a tree branch at least 10 feet off the ground and 4 feet from the trunk. Use a stuff sack with 50 feet of cord. This keeps food out of reach but is less reliable than a canister against experienced bears.
- **Bear boxes** — metal storage lockers provided at established campsites in bear country. Use them when available.

**Camp practices:**
- Cook and eat at least 200 feet (70 paces) from your sleeping area. This creates separation between food smells and where you sleep.
- Change out of clothes you cooked in before going to bed. Store cooking clothes with your food.
- Never keep food, snacks, or scented items in your tent.
- Clean up all food scraps and pack out all trash. A single candy wrapper can attract a bear.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Never approach, feed, or take selfies with bears. A bear that associates humans with food becomes dangerous and is often euthanized by wildlife managers. Feeding bears kills bears.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### If You Encounter a Bear

**Black bears:**
- Make yourself look large. Stand tall, raise your arms, and speak in a firm, calm voice.
- Back away slowly. Do not run — running triggers a chase instinct.
- If a black bear attacks, fight back. Hit its nose and eyes with whatever you have — rocks, sticks, trekking poles.

**Grizzly bears:**
- Do not run. Back away slowly while speaking calmly.
- If a grizzly charges, it may be a bluff charge — stand your ground until the last moment.
- If a grizzly makes contact, play dead. Lie face down with your hands clasped behind your neck and your legs spread (so the bear cannot flip you over). Stay still until the bear leaves.
- If a grizzly attacks you in your tent at night, fight back — nighttime tent attacks are predatory, not defensive.

**Bear spray:**
- Bear spray is the most effective deterrent, proven more effective than firearms in stopping bear attacks. Carry it in a hip or chest holster where you can reach it in seconds — not buried in your pack.
- Aim slightly downward and spray in a wide arc when the bear is within 20–30 feet.
- Practice drawing and deploying your bear spray before you need it.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Bear spray has been shown to stop aggressive bear behavior in over 90% of documented encounters. Firearms, by contrast, are effective less than 75% of the time — partly because accurate shooting while a bear charges at 35 mph is extremely difficult.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Raccoon Safety

Raccoons may seem harmless compared to bears, but they are remarkably destructive camp visitors. They have nimble hands that can open zippers, latches, and even simple knots. They are persistent, work in groups, and are active at night when you are asleep.

### Preventing Raccoon Problems

- **Secure all food and trash** in hard-sided containers, bear canisters, or vehicle trunks. Raccoons can chew through soft-sided coolers and bags.
- **Never leave food unattended** during the day or night. Raccoons scout campsites during daylight and return after dark.
- **Store cooking gear** (pots, utensils, stoves) cleaned and away from your sleeping area. Food residue attracts raccoons.
- **Secure your campsite before bed.** Zip tent doors, latch coolers, hang or lock up anything with food residue.

### If Raccoons Invade Your Camp

- Make noise — bang pots, shout, shine a bright flashlight. Most raccoons will retreat from loud, sudden disturbances.
- Do not corner or grab a raccoon. They can bite and scratch, and raccoons are a primary carrier of rabies in many regions.
- If raccoons keep returning despite deterrence, store all food in your vehicle and report the issue to the campground host or ranger station.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Raccoons have incredible memories for food sources. If a raccoon gets food from your campsite once, it will return night after night. Prevention is far easier than dealing with a raccoon that already knows your camp has snacks.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## The Big Picture

Both bears and raccoons are wild animals doing what wild animals do — looking for easy calories. The common thread in preventing problems with both species is the same: **store food properly, keep a clean camp, and create distance between food smells and sleeping areas.** These are not just good practices for animal safety; they are fundamental backcountry skills covered by the Leave No Trace principles.
