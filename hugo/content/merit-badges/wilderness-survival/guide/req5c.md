{{< drg/requirement number="5c" >}}
Explain how to protect yourself from bears and raccoons.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Bears and raccoons are not aggressive by nature, but they're opportunistic. They're attracted to food, and they'll defend themselves if surprised. The good news: with the right practices, encounters are rare and preventable.

## Protecting Yourself from Bears

**Bear safety starts before your trip.** Research your destination: Are there bears there? What species? Black bears or grizzlies? Knowing this determines your precautions.

**Prevention: The Most Important Step**

1. **Make noise while hiking.** Bears want nothing to do with humans. Talk, clap, or wear a bear bell so they hear you coming and move away. Silent hikers surprise bears—surprises lead to defensive reactions.

2. **Never approach a bear, cub, or den.** A mother protecting cubs is extremely dangerous. Stay at least 100 yards away (about the length of a football field). If you see a cub, the mother is nearby.

3. **Secure ALL food.** This is the #1 rule. A bear that learns humans = food becomes a "problem bear" and is often killed. When camping:
   - Store food in a bear bag (hung 12+ feet high and 10+ feet from trees)
   - Use bear canisters (portable containers designed to resist bears)
   - Never leave food, trash, toiletries, or even dirty dishes in your tent

4. **Cook and eat away from your camping area.** At least 100 yards downwind. Bears smell food from miles away.

5. **Hang your trash.** Every piece of trash—including toilet paper with food residue—goes into a sealed bag hung like food.

**If You Encounter a Bear:**

1. **Don't run.** Running triggers a chase response. Stand your ground and back away slowly.

2. **Make yourself look large.** Raise your arms. Make noise. Appear as intimidating as possible.

3. **Speak calmly.** A calm voice might convince the bear you're not prey.

4. **Back away slowly.** Give the bear an escape route. It wants to leave as much as you want it to.

5. **If it charges, play dead (black bears) or fight back (grizzlies).** Different bear species require different responses:
   - **Black bears:** If attacked, play dead. Lie face-down, protect your head and neck.
   - **Grizzly bears:** If attacked, fight back with rocks, branches, or bear spray.
   - Know which bears live in your region.

6. **Bear spray as a last resort.** If available and trained to use it, bear spray can stop an attack. Most bear encounters don't need it.

## Protecting Yourself from Raccoons

Raccoons are smaller and less dangerous than bears, but they're clever, aggressive about food, and they'll rip into tents to get at food or trash.

**Prevention:**

1. **Secure all food like you would for bears.** Even small scraps attract raccoons. A raccoon will tear apart a tent to get at a granola bar.

2. **Don't leave trash anywhere near camp.** Raccoons are incredibly intelligent and can open coolers, trash cans, and even some types of bags. Hang trash just like food.

3. **Keep a clean camp.** No crumbs, no grease stains, no dirty dishes left out. Raccoons forage at night, so clean up completely after dinner.

4. **Secure your personal items.** Raccoons will steal sunscreen, toothpaste, lip balm—anything scented or shiny.

**If a Raccoon Approaches Your Camp:**

1. **Make loud noises.** Bang pots, shout, clap. Raccoons usually flee from noise.

2. **Never touch it.** Raccoons can bite and carry rabies. Keep your distance.

3. **Don't offer food.** Even "just a little bit" teaches raccoons that camps = easy food.

4. **If it's aggressive or acting strangely, leave the area.** A raccoon that doesn't flee from noise might be sick (rabid). Evacuate and contact a ranger.

## The Principle: Don't Habituate Wildlife

The core of wildlife safety is **don't teach animals that humans = food.** Once an animal loses its natural fear of humans, it becomes a problem and often must be killed. Every time you secure your food and trash, you're:
- Keeping yourself safe
- Keeping the animal safe (and wild)
- Protecting other campers who come after you

This is an ethical responsibility, not just a safety practice.

Bears have a sense of smell 7 times better than a bloodhound's. They can smell a single candy bar from 20 miles away. This is why scent management (hanging food, cooking away from camp) is so critical.
