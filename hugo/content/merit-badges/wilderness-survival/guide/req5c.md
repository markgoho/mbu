{{< drg/requirement number="5c" >}}
Explain how to protect yourself from bears and raccoons.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Bears and raccoons are the two most common wildlife threats at North American campsites. They're not out to get you — they're after your food. Understanding their behavior and removing the food incentive is the foundation of safe coexistence.

{{< drg/image src="images/bear-canister-and-hung-food-bag.avif" alt="A bear canister on a rock beside a food bag hanging from a rope between two trees, showing two food storage methods side by side" >}}

## Bears

Black bears and grizzly/brown bears are both present in North American wilderness areas, and they require somewhat different approaches.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
A bear's sense of smell is roughly 2,100 times more powerful than a human's — 7 times more sensitive than a bloodhound. They can detect food odors from over a mile away. This is why proper food storage is not optional: it's the entire game.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

### Food and Scent Management

The most important rule: **never have food or food-scented items in or near your tent.**

Bears that learn to associate humans with food become "food-conditioned" bears — and food-conditioned bears are eventually euthanized by wildlife managers. Protecting your food protects the bears too.

**Food storage methods:**

**Bear canister:** A hard-sided container that bears cannot grip or open. Required in many national parks and wilderness areas. The most reliable method.

**Bear hang (PCT method):** Hang your food bag 10 feet off the ground and 10 feet from any tree trunk. Use a counterbalance method with two bags of similar weight on either side of the rope, or use a carabiner and line.

**Bear boxes:** Many established campsites have metal bear-proof boxes installed. Use them. They're there for a reason.

**Treat these items the same as food:**
- Toothpaste, sunscreen, deodorant, lip balm
- Chapstick, trash, food wrappers
- Cookware that has touched food
- Clothing worn while cooking

Keep all of the above out of your tent and stored properly.

### Around Camp

- **Cook 100+ feet from your sleeping area** — downwind if possible
- **Change out of clothes you cooked in** before going to bed
- **Never sleep with food in your tent** — even a forgotten granola bar
- **Pack out all garbage** — don't bury it, burn it in the campfire, or leave it behind

### If You Encounter a Bear

**Black bear encounter:**
- Don't run (triggers pursuit instinct)
- Make yourself look large — stand tall, raise arms, open jacket
- Speak in a firm, calm voice: "Hey bear, I'm a human, I'm not food"
- Back away slowly while facing the bear
- If it follows: make noise, throw objects near (not at) it
- If attacked: fight back aggressively — black bears respond to resistance

**Grizzly/brown bear encounter:**
- Don't run
- Speak calmly, avoid direct eye contact
- Back away slowly
- If attacked unexpectedly: play dead — lie on your stomach with hands protecting neck, legs apart
- If a grizzly attacks in your tent or continues attacking after you play dead: fight back

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Carry bear spray in grizzly country and know how to use it. It is more effective than firearms in most bear encounter scenarios. Practice drawing and deploying your canister at home before you need it in the field. Bear spray has a 30-foot range — wait until the bear is close before using it.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Raccoons

Raccoons are far less dangerous than bears, but they are clever, persistent, and alarmingly good at defeating "raccoon-proof" food storage that doesn't actually exist. A raccoon that gets into your food at 2am will destroy your trip.

### Raccoon Behavior

Raccoons are nocturnal, extremely dexterous (their paws can manipulate zippers, clasps, and latches), and very bold around human camps. Unlike bears, raccoons don't generally pose a physical threat to adults — but they can scratch and bite if cornered.

### Food Protection

- **Hang your food** the same way you would for bears (raccoons are excellent climbers, but can't follow a properly hung bag)
- **Hard-sided containers** keep raccoons out reliably
- **Soft coolers and regular backpacks do not** — raccoons can bite through fabric and unzip most zippers
- **Never leave food unattended at camp** — raccoons operate while you're asleep or briefly away
- **Never feed raccoons** — even once. A raccoon that associates humans with food becomes a persistent camp pest

### If Raccoons Approach

- Shout and make noise to drive them off
- Never corner a raccoon — they bite when threatened
- Store food before dark if possible — raccoons are far bolder after dark
- If a raccoon behaves abnormally (active in daylight, approaching boldly, appearing disoriented), maintain distance — unusual behavior can indicate rabies

{{< drg/tip >}}
Hanging your food bag works better against raccoons than against experienced bears. But in raccoon-heavy areas (near established campgrounds and picnic areas), even a perfect bear hang might be defeated by a clever raccoon that's been practicing on sloppy campers for years. A bear canister is truly critter-proof.
{{< /drg/tip >}}
