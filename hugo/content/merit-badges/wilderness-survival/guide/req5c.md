{{< drg/requirement number="5c" >}}
Explain how to protect yourself from bears and raccoons.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A black bear can smell food from five miles away. A raccoon can open a cooler latch with its hands. Both animals are intelligent, persistent, and highly motivated by one thing: your food. Protecting yourself from bears and raccoons starts long before you encounter one — it starts with how you store, cook, and dispose of food in the backcountry.

## Bear Safety

Three species of bears live in North America: black bears, brown bears (including grizzlies), and polar bears. For most Scouts, black bears and brown bears are the ones you'll encounter.

### Prevention: Keep Bears Away

The best bear encounter is the one that never happens. Bears avoid humans when they know we're coming.

- **Make noise while hiking.** Talk, clap, or call out on blind corners and near streams (where water noise covers your footsteps). Bear bells are less effective than your voice.
- **Hike in groups.** Groups of four or more are almost never attacked by bears.
- **Stay on marked trails.** You're less likely to surprise a bear in areas with regular human traffic.
- **Never approach a bear.** Not even for a photo. Bears that seem calm can charge without warning.

### Food Storage

Food-conditioned bears — bears that have learned to associate humans with food — are the most dangerous bears. Proper food storage protects both you and the bear.

- **Use bear canisters** where required (many national parks mandate them). These hard-sided containers are bear-proof when closed correctly.
- **Hang a bear bag** where canisters aren't required. Hang food at least 10 feet off the ground and 4 feet from the trunk of any tree, using the PCT method or a carabiner-counterbalance hang.
- **Cook and eat at least 200 feet from your sleeping area.** The smell of food on the ground attracts bears to that spot — make sure that spot isn't your tent.
- **Store all scented items** with your food — toothpaste, sunscreen, lip balm, insect repellent. If it has a smell, it goes in the bear canister or bear bag.

{{< drg/checklist title="Bear-Safe Camp Setup" subtitle="Follow every time you camp in bear country" >}}
- Cook and eat at least 200 feet downwind from your tent
- Store food in a bear canister or properly hung bear bag
- Include all scented items (toothpaste, sunscreen, snacks) in bear storage
- Change clothes before sleeping if your cooking clothes smell like food
- Clean up all food scraps — pack out every crumb
- Never keep food, drinks, or scented items inside your tent
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

### Bear Encounters

If you do encounter a bear, your response depends on the species and the bear's behavior.

**Black bears** are generally timid. If a black bear approaches:
- Stand tall and make yourself look big
- Speak in a calm, firm voice
- Back away slowly — do not run
- If it charges, it's likely a bluff charge. Stand your ground.
- If a black bear attacks, **fight back** — hit it in the nose and eyes with whatever you have

**Brown/grizzly bears** are larger and more aggressive when surprised or defending cubs. If a grizzly approaches:
- Speak in a calm voice and back away slowly
- Do not make direct eye contact
- If it charges and makes contact, **play dead** — lie face-down with your hands protecting the back of your neck and your legs spread apart (so the bear can't flip you over)
- Only fight back if the attack continues after you've played dead

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
In designated grizzly country, carry bear spray and know how to use it. Bear spray is more effective than firearms at stopping a charging bear. Practice deploying the safety and aiming before you need it — you'll have about two seconds to react during a real charge.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Raccoon Safety

Raccoons may look cute, but they're aggressive when cornered, surprisingly strong, and common carriers of rabies. They're also nocturnal raiders who have adapted brilliantly to raiding human campsites.

### Prevention

- **Secure all food in hard-sided containers** — coolers with latches, bear canisters, or your vehicle. Raccoons have dexterous hands that can unzip backpacks, open Velcro, and pry open loose lids.
- **Don't leave food or trash unattended** — not even for a few minutes. Raccoons watch campsites from the tree line and move in the moment you walk away.
- **Clean up thoroughly after meals.** Grease on a grill or crumbs on a table is an invitation.
- **Hang food bags** if hard-sided storage isn't available. The same bear-bag technique works for raccoons — though raccoons are better climbers than most people expect.
- **Keep your tent closed.** Raccoons will explore open tents looking for food.

### Raccoon Encounters

- **Do not feed raccoons.** A raccoon that associates humans with food becomes aggressive and dangerous.
- **If a raccoon approaches, make noise** — clap, shout, bang pots together. Most raccoons will flee from loud, sudden sounds.
- **Never corner a raccoon.** Give it an escape route.
- **If a raccoon acts strangely** — aggressive behavior in daylight, stumbling, drooling, or approaching without fear — it may be rabid. Keep your distance and report it to a ranger.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Raccoons wash their food before eating it — that's where their scientific name (*Procyon lotor*, meaning "washing pre-dog") comes from. They do this because wetting their paws increases their sense of touch, helping them inspect food more carefully. In the wild, this behavior evolved near water sources.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## The Common Thread

Both bears and raccoons are attracted to food, trash, and scented items. The single most effective way to protect yourself from both is **impeccable food storage and campsite hygiene.** A clean camp is a safe camp.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Think of it from the animal's perspective: if your campsite smells like a restaurant, animals will treat it like one. If it smells like nothing, they'll keep walking. The effort you put into food storage directly determines your risk level.
{{< /drg/tip >}}
