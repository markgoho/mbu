{{< drg/requirement number="8" >}}
Demonstrate three ways to treat water found in the outdoors to prepare it for drinking.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Water from streams, lakes, and ponds can contain harmful bacteria, parasites, and chemicals that cause severe illness (giardia, cryptosporidium, cholera, dysentery). You cannot see, taste, or smell these pathogens. Treating water before drinking is **non-negotiable** in wilderness survival.

You'll demonstrate three different treatment methods, proving you understand how each works and when to use it.

{{< drg/image src="images/water-treatment-methods.avif" alt="Three water treatment methods shown as sequential panels: boiling water over fire with steam, purification tablets dissolving in cup, water being filtered through portable device" >}}

## Method 1: Boiling

Boiling is the most reliable method because high heat kills most pathogens.

**How it works:**
- Pathogens have proteins that denature (break apart) at high temperatures
- Boiling (212°F at sea level) kills bacteria, viruses, and most parasites
- Altitude reduces boiling point (at 5,000 feet, boiling point is 202°F), requiring longer boiling

**Steps:**

1. **Collect water** from a clean source (moving water is better than stagnant; avoid water with visible debris, silt, or algae)

2. **Strain the water** through cloth to remove large particles (optional but helpful)

3. **Heat the water to a rolling boil** (vigorous bubbling throughout, not just steaming)

4. **Boil for 1 minute** at sea level (3 minutes at high altitude above 6,500 feet)

5. **Cool the water** before drinking (let it sit or pour between containers to cool faster)

**Pros:**
- Kills virtually all pathogens
- No special equipment required beyond fire and a container
- Works at any altitude
- Works for all types of contamination (bacteria, viruses, parasites)

**Cons:**
- Requires fire and fuel
- Slow (boiling takes 10+ minutes)
- Water cools quickly, limiting quantity that can be treated at once
- Can't treat large quantities efficiently

**When to use:** When you have fire and fuel available, and time isn't critical. Best for small quantities (1–2 liters).

## Method 2: Water Purification Tablets

Purification tablets (iodine or chlorine dioxide) chemically kill pathogens.

**How they work:**
- Iodine (or chlorine dioxide) damages the cell membranes of bacteria, viruses, and some parasites
- The tablet dissolves in water, releasing the active chemical
- Takes 20–30 minutes for disinfection to complete

**Steps:**

1. **Collect water** from a reasonably clean source (strain out debris if possible)

2. **Add one tablet** to one liter of water according to tablet instructions

3. **Stir or shake** to distribute the chemical

4. **Wait 20–30 minutes** (or the time specified on the tablet)

5. **Drink** — the water is now treated

**Important:** Some tablets require dissolving first in a small amount of water, then adding the solution to your container.

**Pros:**
- Lightweight and compact (takes minimal pack space)
- Fast (20–30 minutes)
- No fuel or fire required
- Long shelf life
- Works in cold water

**Cons:**
- Doesn't remove sediment or discoloration
- Some people dislike the iodine taste (tablets often include flavor packets)
- Doesn't kill all parasites (specifically, Cryptosporidium oocysts are resistant)
- Less effective in very cold water

**Common brands:**
- Aquamira (chlorine dioxide)
- Potable Aqua (iodine)
- SteriPEN (UV light — a variation)

**Brands I recommend:** Chlorine dioxide tablets (Aquamira) are more effective than iodine for a broader range of pathogens.

## Method 3: Portable Water Filter

A portable water filter uses a fine membrane to physically remove pathogens.

**How they work:**
- Water passes through a ceramic, hollow-fiber, or plastic membrane with microscopic pores
- Bacteria and parasites are too large to pass through (you're physically blocking them)
- Viruses are smaller than most bacterial cells, so some filters don't eliminate viruses (important distinction)

**Types of portable filters:**

**Gravity filters** (e.g., Platypus GravityWorks, Sawyer Squeeze):
- Fill an upper bag with untreated water
- Water drips through a filter into a lower container
- Slow but requires no physical effort
- Treats large quantities

**Pump filters** (e.g., Katadyn Hiker Pro):
- Manually pump water from a source through a filter cartridge into a clean container
- Faster than gravity filters
- Requires physical effort
- Good for small quantities

**Straw filters** (e.g., LifeStraw):
- Drink directly from the source through a filter straw
- Fastest for drinking directly
- Limited to personal use (can't treat large quantities)
- Only works for small particles and bacteria; viruses can pass through some models

**Steps (using a pump filter):**

1. **Collect water** from source

2. **Set up the filter** according to manufacturer instructions

3. **Prime the filter** (push water through to seat the cartridge)

4. **Pump water** from the untreated container through the filter into a clean container

5. **Drink** — the water is now treated

**Pros:**
- Removes sediment, color, and odor (making water more palatable)
- Fast for pump and straw models
- Reusable (filter cartridges can treat thousands of liters)
- No chemicals or taste change (unless filter includes activated charcoal)
- Effective for most bacterial and parasitic pathogens

**Cons:**
- More expensive initially
- Requires maintenance (cartridges clog and need cleaning)
- Some filters don't eliminate viruses
- Mechanical failure risk
- Requires clean backup plan if filter breaks

**When to use:** On extended trips where water availability is high and you need to treat large quantities.

## Comparing the Three Methods

| Method | Speed | Effectiveness | Cost | Ease | Pack Weight |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Boiling | Slow (10+ min) | Excellent | Low | Easy | None (need fire) |
| Tablets | Medium (20–30 min) | Very good | Low | Easy | Minimal |
| Filters | Fast (immediate) | Very good | Medium | Moderate | Moderate |

## Choosing Your Method

**For survival situations:** Boiling if you have fire; tablets if you don't

**For extended camping trips:** Filters (best for volume and reusability)

**For emergency backup:** Carry both tablets and a filter for redundancy

## Demonstration to Your Counselor

Show or describe all three methods:

1. **Boiling:** Demonstrate heating water over a fire or stove until it boils. Explain the time required and how it kills pathogens.

2. **Purification tablets:** Show a tablet, explain how it works, demonstrate adding it to water (allow the 20–30 minute wait if possible).

3. **Portable filter:** Show a filter (or a photo if you don't own one yet), explain how it works, and describe how to use it.

For each method, explain:
- How it removes/kills pathogens
- Advantages and disadvantages
- When you'd use it in a real survival situation
- Any special considerations

## Special Case: Melting Snow for Water

In winter, you might need to melt snow for drinking water.

**Steps:**
1. **Never eat snow directly** — it lowers your core temperature (your body burns calories to melt it)
2. **Melt snow using fire or stove** — same process as boiling water
3. **Once melted, treat the water** using one of the three methods (though melting is already a heating process, adding a tablet or brief additional boil is safer)

## Critical Reminder

**Always treat water collected in the wilderness.** Even clear-looking mountain water can harbor giardia. One of the most common causes of wilderness-related illness is untreated water. Don't take shortcuts.
