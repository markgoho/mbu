{{< drg/requirement number="8" >}}
Demonstrate three ways to treat water found in the outdoors to prepare it for drinking.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

You will actually demonstrate three water treatment methods to your counselor — not just describe them. Clear, flowing mountain streams look clean, but every untreated natural water source should be considered potentially contaminated.

{{< drg/image src="images/water-purification-methods.avif" alt="Three scenes showing water purification methods: boiling water in a camp pot, using a water filter pump, and chemical purification tablets" >}}

## Why Water Must Be Treated

Natural water in backcountry settings may contain:

- **Giardia lamblia:** A protozoan parasite that causes severe diarrhea and cramping. Even pristine mountain streams can harbor Giardia — it can survive weeks in cold water and comes from infected animal feces upstream.
- **Cryptosporidium:** Similar to Giardia, but smaller and more resistant to chemical treatment (iodine tablets don't kill it).
- **Bacteria:** E. coli, Salmonella, Campylobacter — common downstream from grazing land or human activity.
- **Viruses:** Hepatitis A, norovirus — more common in heavily traveled areas and developing countries.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Clear water is not necessarily safe water. Giardia cysts are invisible to the naked eye. Water from a pristine-looking alpine stream is just as likely to contain pathogens as a murky lowland creek. Always treat backcountry water before drinking.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Method 1: Boiling

Boiling is the most reliable water treatment method — it kills all biological pathogens (bacteria, viruses, protozoa) and requires only fire and a container.

**How to do it:**
1. Collect water in a metal container (aluminum or stainless steel cup, pot, or can)
2. Bring water to a rolling boil (large, vigorous bubbles throughout — not just at the bottom)
3. At sea level to 6,500 feet: boil for 1 minute
4. Above 6,500 feet: boil for 3 minutes (lower atmospheric pressure means water boils at a lower temperature, requiring longer treatment)
5. Allow to cool before drinking (or place container in cold water/snow to speed cooling)

**Advantages:** 100% effective against all pathogens. Works without special gear.

**Disadvantages:** Requires fire, fuel, and time. Doesn't remove chemical contaminants or heavy metals. Hot water needs cooling before drinking.

## Method 2: Filtration (Pump or Squeeze Filter)

Modern backcountry water filters physically remove bacteria and protozoa by forcing water through a membrane with pores small enough to block them.

**Common filter types:**
- **Pump filter** (e.g., MSR MiniWorks): Pump water from source through a filter element into your bottle. Reliable, field-cleanable, handles larger volumes.
- **Squeeze filter** (e.g., Sawyer Squeeze): Squeeze water from a flexible bag through a filter element. Ultra-lightweight, no pumping required.
- **Straw filter** (e.g., LifeStraw): Drink directly from the source through the filter. Good for emergency use.

**How to use a pump filter:**
1. Place intake hose in the water source, output hose in your clean container
2. Pump at steady rate until container is full
3. Periodically backflush (clean) the filter per manufacturer instructions
4. Store filter dry to prevent freezing damage (frozen water in the membrane can crack it)

**Advantages:** Removes Giardia and Cryptosporidium reliably. No waiting time, no chemicals, filtered water tastes good.

**Disadvantages:** Does NOT remove viruses (needs UV treatment or chemical treatment added). Filter elements can clog in silty water. Can freeze and crack at low temperatures.

{{< drg/tip >}}
If your water source is silty or muddy, pre-filter through a bandana or coffee filter into a container and let it settle before running it through your pump filter. This dramatically extends filter life.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Method 3: Chemical Treatment

Chemical treatment uses either iodine tablets or chlorine dioxide tablets to kill pathogens through oxidation.

**Iodine tablets (e.g., Potable Aqua):**
1. Add one tablet per liter of water (two tablets if water is cloudy)
2. Wait 30 minutes before drinking (50 minutes in cold water below 40°F)
3. Works against bacteria, Giardia, and most viruses
4. Does **not** kill Cryptosporidium — a significant limitation

**Chlorine dioxide tablets (e.g., Aquatabs, Katadyn Micropur):**
1. Add one tablet per liter of water
2. Wait 30 minutes for bacteria and Giardia, **4 hours** for Cryptosporidium
3. Works against bacteria, Giardia, Cryptosporidium, and most viruses
4. Better taste than iodine, no long-term health concerns with occasional use

**Advantages:** Lightweight (tablets weigh almost nothing), no mechanical parts to break, long shelf life.

**Disadvantages:** Waiting time required (especially for Cryptosporidium with chlorine dioxide). Iodine has an unpleasant taste and shouldn't be used long-term by people with thyroid conditions. Doesn't remove heavy metals or chemical contaminants.

## Bonus Method: UV Treatment

UV purifiers (like the SteriPen) expose water to ultraviolet light, which scrambles pathogen DNA and renders them unable to reproduce.

**How to use:**
1. Fill your water bottle with clear water (UV works poorly in turbid/silty water)
2. Insert the UV light and stir for the required time (typically 60–90 seconds per liter)
3. Drink immediately — UV treatment leaves no residual protection

**Advantages:** Kills viruses, bacteria, Giardia, and Cryptosporidium. Fast, no taste impact.

**Disadvantages:** Requires batteries. Doesn't work in turbid water. No residual protection (retreated water can be recontaminated).

## Choosing the Right Method

| Situation | Best Method |
|---|---|
| Have fire and time | Boiling |
| Day hiking, carry nothing extra | Squeeze filter (light, fast) |
| Long expedition, larger volumes | Pump filter |
| Emergency kit, backup | Chemical tablets |
| Fastest possible treatment | UV purifier (SteriPen) |
| Snow/ice as water source | Melt + boil |

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Melting snow for water requires far more snow than you'd expect — about 8–10 cups of loose snow melts into 1 cup of water, depending on snow density. Fresh powdery snow is mostly air. If you're relying on snow as a water source, plan to spend significant fuel time melting.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}
