Water is priority #4 in wilderness survival. You can survive 3 weeks without food but only 3–5 days without water. In the wilderness, water is usually available—streams, lakes, rain—but it's often contaminated with bacteria, parasites, and chemicals. You need to know three methods to purify water safely.

## Why Water Treatment Matters

Untreated water from streams and lakes can contain:
- **Bacteria:** E. coli, Salmonella (cause diarrhea, fever)
- **Parasites:** Giardia, Cryptosporidium (cause severe intestinal disease)
- **Viruses:** Rare in North America but present worldwide
- **Chemicals:** Pesticides, heavy metals (less common but possible)

{{</* drg/image src="images/water-treatment-methods.avif" alt="Three water treatment methods shown side-by-side: boiling over fire, purification tablet in water, filter with sand and gravel layers" */>}}

Drinking contaminated water won't kill you immediately, but it will cause illness that weakens you, makes travel difficult, and is dangerous in a survival situation.

## Method 1: Boiling

Boiling is the most reliable water treatment—it kills all pathogens (bacteria, parasites, viruses) through heat.

### **How It Works**

Heat to 212°F (100°C) kills most pathogens within seconds. At higher altitudes where water boils at lower temperatures, the heated water is still safe—the extended time at elevated temperature is sufficient.

### **Process**

1. **Gather water** from a relatively clean source (moving water is better than stagnant; upstream is better than downstream).
2. **Filter out visible debris** through cloth or a makeshift filter (sand, gravel, leaves).
3. **Heat water to a rolling boil** (you'll see large bubbles breaking the surface).
4. **Boil for 1 minute** (at sea level). At high elevations (above 6,500 feet), boil for 3 minutes.
5. **Cool before drinking** (water is too hot to drink immediately).

### **Advantages**
- Kills all pathogens (bacteria, parasites, viruses)
- Doesn't require special equipment (just a heat source and container)
- Doesn't affect the taste of water

### **Disadvantages**
- Requires fire and fuel (not practical if you have no fire starter)
- Takes 10–15 minutes (gathering wood, heating, cooling)
- Uses a lot of energy

### **Practical Tips**
- Use a metal cup or makeshift container (flat stone heated and used to boil water in a cloth or container)
- Collect wood before you start boiling
- Boil enough water for drinking and cooking

## Method 2: Water Purification Tablets (Chemical Treatment)

Purification tablets use chemicals (iodine or chlorine dioxide) to kill pathogens.

### **How It Works**

The chemical oxidizes (breaks down) bacterial cell walls and destroys pathogens. Most tablets take 30 minutes to an hour to work.

### **Process**

1. **Fill a container with water** from a stream, lake, or rain collection.
2. **Add one tablet** per liter (follow package instructions—dosage varies).
3. **Stir and let sit** for 30–60 minutes (iodine) or 15–30 minutes (chlorine dioxide, which is faster).
4. **Drink.** The water is safe but may have a slight chemical taste.

### **Advantages**
- Lightweight (a small bottle of tablets weighs ounces)
- Works quickly (15–60 minutes)
- No fire required
- Long shelf life
- Inexpensive ($5–15 per bottle)

### **Disadvantages**
- Doesn't remove visible debris (filter first)
- Chemical taste (some people find iodine unpleasant)
- Not completely effective against Cryptosporidium (a tough parasite)
- Iodine isn't recommended for pregnant women or people with thyroid issues (long-term use)

### **Practical Tips**
- Carry a bottle of tablets in your survival kit
- Filter water through cloth first to remove sediment (tablets work better on clear water)
- You can improve taste by adding lemon juice or drink mix
- Mark the container with the treatment time so you don't drink before it's ready

{{</* drg/did-you-know */>}}
Chlorine dioxide tablets are becoming more popular than iodine because they work faster and taste better. They cost a bit more but are worth it if you have the budget.
{{</* /drg/did-you-know */>}}

## Method 3: Filtration

Filtration removes pathogens and sediment using a physical filter (cloth, sand, gravel). It's less reliable than boiling or chemicals alone, but it's a useful first step.

### **Types of Filters**

**Commercial water filter:**
- Portable filters (LifeStraw, Sawyer) remove bacteria and some parasites
- Cost: $20–50
- Weight: Ounces
- Effective for most wilderness pathogens

**Improvised sand/gravel filter:**
- Layer sand, gravel, and cloth to create a simple filter
- Effective at removing sediment and some bacteria
- Not fully reliable against all parasites (should be combined with boiling or chemical treatment)

### **Commercial Filter Process**

1. **Fill the filter** with water from the source.
2. **Squeeze or draw water through** the filter medium (depending on filter type).
3. **Drink directly** or collect filtered water in a container.

**Note:** Check the filter's specifications—some remove bacteria only, others also remove parasites. Read the packaging.

### **Improvised Filter Process**

1. **Layer materials in a container:**
   - Top: cloth or coffee filter
   - Second layer: Sand (2–3 inches)
   - Third layer: Gravel or small stones (2–3 inches)
   - Bottom: cloth to contain the gravel
2. **Pour water through slowly.**
3. **Collect the filtered water.**
4. **Combine with boiling or tablets** for complete treatment (improvised filters aren't 100% reliable).

### **Advantages of Filtration**
- Removes visible sediment and debris
- No chemicals or fire needed (for some filters)
- Improves water taste by removing bad flavors
- Reusable (for commercial filters)

### **Disadvantages**
- Doesn't eliminate all pathogens (especially viruses)
- Improvised filters are less effective than commercial
- Some commercial filters are pricey
- Requires a clean container to pour into

## Combining Methods for Maximum Safety

In a real wilderness situation, combining methods is safest:

**Most reliable approach:**
1. **Filter** water through cloth to remove sediment
2. **Boil** or use purification tablets
3. **Cool or let sit** until safe to drink

**If you only have one method:**
- **Boiling** is most reliable (kills everything)
- **Commercial filter** is convenient but less complete
- **Tablets** are lightweight and work when used correctly
- **Improvised filtration** alone is only a first step

{{</* drg/tip */>}}
Include water purification tablets in your survival kit *and* know how to boil water. Two methods ensure you're never stuck without clean water.
{{</* /drg/tip */>}}

## Practical Demonstration for Your Counselor

Your counselor will likely ask you to demonstrate water treatment. Here's what to prepare:

### **What You'll Need**
- A source of "dirty" water (stream, lake, or deliberately contaminated water)
- Three containers or methods:
  1. Boiling apparatus (fire, pot, or makeshift container)
  2. Purification tablets
  3. Filter (commercial or improvised)

### **What Your Counselor Will Observe**
- Can you properly use each method?
- Do you understand why each works?
- Can you combine methods for safety?
- Do you know the timeline (tablets: 30 mins, boiling: 1 min, filtration: immediate)?

### **Practical Example**

**Scenario:** You've found a stream. Show how you'd treat the water:

1. **Filtering:** Pour water through cloth to remove leaves and sediment
2. **Boiling:** Heat the filtered water to a rolling boil for 1 minute
3. **Cooling:** Let it cool in a clean container while preparing other camp tasks
4. **Alternative:** If you had no fire, describe using purification tablets instead

## Important Notes

- **Boiling works at any altitude** (even where water boils at lower temperatures than 212°F, the extended heat is sufficient).
- **Tablets have shelf lives.** Check expiration dates and replace expired tablets.
- **Melted snow and ice** require boiling/treatment just like stream water. Snow melting doesn't purify it.
- **Rainwater** is generally safe but can be contaminated by debris from collection surfaces.
- **Stagnant water** is riskier than moving water (fewer parasites in flowing streams).

## Before You Move On

You'll demonstrate or explain three ways to treat water. Your counselor will test your understanding:
- "You're stranded without a fire. How do you get safe drinking water?" (Tablets or filter)
- "How long do you boil water?" (1 minute at sea level, 3 minutes above 6,500 feet)
- "Your filter is slow. What's a faster method?" (Tablets or boiling)

Show that you can access clean water in a wilderness situation—because staying hydrated is how you survive long-term.
