{{< drg/requirement number="8" >}}
Water. Demonstrate three ways to treat water found in the outdoors to prepare it for drinking.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A crystal-clear mountain stream looks pure enough to drink straight. It probably isn't. Even the cleanest-looking water can harbor invisible threats: *Giardia* parasites, *Cryptosporidium*, bacteria like *E. coli* and *Salmonella*, and viruses that cause severe gastrointestinal illness. In a survival situation, vomiting and diarrhea from contaminated water accelerate dehydration — turning a manageable problem into a life-threatening one.

You need to demonstrate three treatment methods. Here are the most practical options for backcountry use.

## Method 1: Boiling

Boiling is the oldest, most reliable method of water purification. It kills bacteria, viruses, parasites, and their cysts — everything that can make you sick.

**How to do it:**

1. Collect water from the clearest source available. Moving water (streams) is generally cleaner than still water (ponds).
2. If the water is cloudy, pre-filter it through a bandana, coffee filter, or even a t-shirt to remove sediment.
3. Bring the water to a **rolling boil** — large, vigorous bubbles, not just tiny bubbles on the bottom.
4. Boil for **one minute at sea level**, or **three minutes above 6,500 feet** (water boils at lower temperatures at altitude, so it needs more time).
5. Let it cool before drinking.

**Pros:** Kills everything. Requires no special supplies beyond a container and heat source.

**Cons:** Requires fire (which takes time and energy), a heat-safe container, and fuel. Doesn't remove chemical contaminants or sediment.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
You can boil water in a plastic bottle if you have no metal container. Fill the bottle completely (no air gap), seal it, and set it near — not in — the coals of a fire. The water inside absorbs heat before the plastic can melt. It won't reach a full rolling boil, but it will get hot enough to kill pathogens. Some survivalists also boil water in birch bark containers, which work because the water keeps the bark below its ignition temperature.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Method 2: Chemical Treatment

Chemical treatment uses tablets or drops to kill microorganisms. The two most common chemicals are **iodine** and **chlorine dioxide**.

### Chlorine Dioxide Tablets (e.g., Aquamira, Potable Aqua ClO₂)

1. Drop the tablet into your water container (follow dosage on the package — typically 1 tablet per liter).
2. Wait the recommended time — usually **30 minutes**, or **4 hours** for *Cryptosporidium*.
3. The water is now safe to drink.

### Iodine Tablets

1. Add tablets per package instructions (usually 1–2 per liter).
2. Wait **30 minutes**.
3. Iodine leaves a slight taste. Adding a vitamin C tablet (or powdered drink mix) after the treatment period neutralizes the taste.

**Pros:** Lightweight, compact, no fire needed. Easy to use.

**Cons:** Requires waiting time. Iodine is not recommended for pregnant individuals or people with thyroid conditions. Neither chemical removes sediment — pre-filter cloudy water. Iodine is less effective against *Cryptosporidium*.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Pack chemical treatment tablets in your survival kit alongside your matches and ferro rod. They weigh almost nothing, take up no space, and provide a backup water treatment method when building a fire isn't practical.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Method 3: Filtration

Water filters physically remove contaminants by forcing water through a medium with microscopic pores. Modern backpacking filters use ceramic, hollow-fiber, or activated carbon elements.

**Pump filters:**

1. Place the intake hose in your water source.
2. Pump the handle to force water through the filter element.
3. Filtered water flows out the outlet hose into your clean container.
4. Most filters remove bacteria and parasites but NOT viruses (pore size is typically 0.2 microns — viruses are smaller).

**Squeeze filters (e.g., Sawyer Squeeze):**

1. Fill the included pouch with water.
2. Screw the filter onto the pouch.
3. Squeeze the pouch — filtered water flows through the filter.
4. Fast, lightweight, and effective.

**Gravity filters:**

1. Fill a dirty-water bag and hang it above a clean-water container.
2. Water flows through the filter by gravity — no pumping required.
3. Good for groups since they process large volumes hands-free.

**Pros:** Fast (no waiting time), removes sediment and most pathogens, improves taste.

**Cons:** Requires carrying the filter. Most filters don't remove viruses. Filters can clog in very turbid water and need backflushing. Doesn't work if the filter is frozen.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Most portable filters remove bacteria and protozoa but **not viruses**. In North America, waterborne viruses are rare in backcountry water, making filtration generally sufficient. However, in developing countries or near sources of human waste, use a purifier (which handles viruses) or combine filtration with chemical treatment.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Improvised Filtration

If you don't have a commercial filter, you can build a basic sediment filter from natural materials. This removes visible particles but does **not** kill microorganisms — you must still boil or chemically treat the water afterward.

**Simple gravity filter:**

1. Cut the bottom off a plastic bottle (or use a sock, bandana, or hollow log).
2. Layer materials inside from bottom to top: small pebbles, coarse sand, fine sand, crushed charcoal (from your campfire — not briquettes), more fine sand.
3. Pour water through the top and collect it from the bottom.
4. The water will be clearer but not safe — boil it after filtering.

Charcoal is the key filtering material. It adsorbs chemicals, reduces bad taste, and catches fine sediment. Use charcoal from hardwood fires, crushed into small granules.

## Finding Water Sources

Before you can treat water, you need to find it:

- **Streams and rivers** — best source; moving water is generally cleaner than still water
- **Lakes and ponds** — collect from deeper areas away from shore; shallow edges have more contamination
- **Rain** — collect directly in clean containers; rainwater is essentially pure
- **Snow and ice** — melt before drinking (eating snow lowers your core temperature); melt snow in a container near your fire
- **Dew** — drag a bandana or t-shirt across dewy grass at dawn, then wring it into a container
- **Plant transpiration** — tie a clear plastic bag around a leafy branch in direct sunlight; water condenses inside the bag over several hours
