{{< drg/requirement number="8" >}}
Water. Demonstrate three ways to treat water found in the outdoors to prepare it for drinking.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Clear, sparkling stream water looks drinkable. It is not. Even the most pristine-looking mountain stream can harbor invisible threats: bacteria like *E. coli* and *Salmonella*, protozoa like *Giardia* and *Cryptosporidium*, and viruses that cause severe gastrointestinal illness. In a survival situation, drinking contaminated water can cause vomiting and diarrhea — which accelerates dehydration and can turn a manageable situation into a medical emergency.

Every drop of water from a natural source must be treated before drinking.

## Finding Water Sources

Before you can treat water, you need to find it. Here are the most common natural sources:

- **Streams and rivers** — flowing water is generally safer than standing water, but still requires treatment
- **Lakes and ponds** — avoid water that is stagnant, discolored, or has a strong odor
- **Rain** — one of the safest natural sources; collect it in any clean container or on a tarp
- **Snow and ice** — must be melted first (eating snow lowers your core temperature); melt in a container rather than in your mouth
- **Dew** — wipe a cloth across dewy grass in the early morning and wring it into a container; slow but effective
- **Seeps and springs** — water emerging from the ground is often cleaner than surface water but should still be treated

{{< drg/tip >}}
Before treating, remove visible particles from your water by filtering it through a bandana, T-shirt, or coffee filter into a container. This pre-filtering step makes every treatment method work better and extends the life of commercial filters.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Three Water Treatment Methods

### 1. Boiling

Boiling is the gold standard of water treatment. It kills bacteria, viruses, and protozoa — everything that could make you sick. If you can build a fire (and after [Req 6](/merit-badges/wilderness-survival/guide/req6/), you can), boiling is your most reliable method.

**How to do it:**
1. Bring water to a **rolling boil** — big, churning bubbles, not just tiny bubbles forming on the bottom.
2. Maintain the rolling boil for **one minute** at elevations below 6,500 feet. At higher elevations, boil for **three minutes** (water boils at a lower temperature at altitude, so it needs more time).
3. Let the water cool before drinking.

**What you need:** A metal container (pot, canteen cup, tin can) or, in an emergency, a rock depression that can hold water near a fire. You can even boil water in a plastic bottle — the water inside prevents the plastic from melting, though this should only be done in a genuine emergency.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
You can boil water in a paper cup over a fire. As long as the water level stays above the flame contact point, the water absorbs heat fast enough to prevent the paper from reaching its ignition temperature (451°F). The water maxes out at 212°F — well below the paper's flash point.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

### 2. Chemical Treatment

Chemical treatment uses tablets or drops that kill pathogens through chemical reaction. The two most common chemicals are chlorine dioxide and iodine.

**Chlorine dioxide tablets (e.g., Aquamira, Katadyn Micropur):**
1. Drop the specified number of tablets into your water container (follow the package instructions — usually 1 tablet per liter).
2. Wait the required time — typically **30 minutes** for bacteria and viruses, and **4 hours** for *Cryptosporidium* (the hardest pathogen to kill chemically).
3. The water may have a slight chemical taste. Adding a vitamin C drink mix after treatment neutralizes the taste.

**Iodine tablets or drops:**
1. Follow package directions for dosage.
2. Wait at least **30 minutes** before drinking.
3. Iodine gives water a noticeable taste and yellowish color.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
People with thyroid conditions, pregnant women, and those allergic to iodine should not use iodine treatment. Chlorine dioxide is a safer alternative for these individuals. Always read the labels and understand the limitations of your treatment method.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### 3. Filtration

Portable water filters physically remove pathogens by forcing water through microscopic pores. Most backcountry filters have pore sizes of 0.2 microns or smaller, which blocks bacteria and protozoa.

**Pump filters:**
1. Place the intake hose in the water source (with the pre-filter screen submerged).
2. Pump the handle to force water through the filter element into your clean container.
3. Follow the manufacturer's maintenance schedule — clogged filters work slowly or not at all.

**Squeeze filters (e.g., Sawyer Squeeze):**
1. Fill the provided dirty-water bag.
2. Screw the filter onto the bag.
3. Squeeze the bag to force water through the filter and into your clean container.

**Straw filters (e.g., LifeStraw):**
1. Place one end in the water source.
2. Suck water through the filter directly.
3. Good for individual use but cannot fill containers for group use.

**Important limitation:** Most portable filters do **not** remove viruses. Viruses are smaller than bacteria and pass through standard filter pores. In North America, waterborne viruses are rare in the backcountry, so filtration alone is usually sufficient. In developing countries or areas with human sewage contamination, combine filtration with chemical treatment.

## UV Treatment (Bonus Method)

UV light treatment (using devices like SteriPEN) scrambles the DNA of pathogens, rendering them unable to reproduce. It is fast (90 seconds per liter), effective against bacteria, viruses, and protozoa, and leaves no chemical taste. However, it requires batteries, does not work well in cloudy water, and is useless if the device breaks. For these reasons, carry it as a convenience but always have a backup method.

## Comparing Methods

| Method | Kills Bacteria | Kills Protozoa | Kills Viruses | Speed | Limitations |
|--------|:---:|:---:|:---:|-------|-------------|
| Boiling | Yes | Yes | Yes | 1–3 min + cooling | Requires fire and container |
| Chemical | Yes | Partial* | Yes | 30 min – 4 hrs | Taste; some don't kill *Crypto* |
| Filter | Yes | Yes | No** | Immediate | Requires working filter |

*Chlorine dioxide kills *Cryptosporidium* after 4 hours; iodine does not reliably kill it.*
**Most portable filters; some have viral removal capability.*
