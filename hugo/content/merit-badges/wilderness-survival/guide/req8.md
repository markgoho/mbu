Water. Demonstrate three ways to treat water found in the outdoors to prepare it for drinking.

A clear mountain stream can look perfectly pure and contain *Giardia lamblia*, *Cryptosporidium*, or *Leptospira* bacteria — all capable of causing serious illness within days. In a survival situation, drinking untreated water out of desperation trades an immediate problem (thirst) for a worse problem (waterborne illness that causes dehydration through vomiting and diarrhea).

These three methods make virtually any freshwater source safe to drink.

{{< drg/image src="images/water-purification-methods.avif" alt="Three water purification methods side by side: a portable filter pump, iodine purification tablets beside a water bottle, and a pot of boiling water on a camp stove" >}}

## Finding Water First

Before treating water, you need to find it. In the backcountry:

**Follow terrain downhill.** Water collects in valleys and drainages. Ridges are dry; valleys have streams.

**Listen for running water.** A stream audible at 200 feet is easy to miss visually in dense forest.

**Look for vegetation.** Dense, lush green vegetation — willows, cattails, cottonwoods — indicates water nearby or just below the surface.

**Collect dew.** Morning dew can be gathered by dragging an absorbent cloth across grass and squeezing it out. Tedious, but usable in dry environments with no surface water.

**Melt snow.** Snow is safe once melted, but requires significant fuel to melt enough water (snow has a lot of air). Never eat snow directly — it lowers your core temperature.

## Method 1: Boiling

Boiling is the most reliable water treatment method. Heat kills all biological threats: bacteria, viruses, parasites, and protozoa.

**How to do it:** Bring water to a rolling boil (large, active bubbles — not just tiny bubbles at the bottom). At most backcountry elevations, one minute at a rolling boil is sufficient. At elevations above 6,500 feet, boil for three minutes (lower atmospheric pressure means lower boiling point, which requires slightly longer treatment time).

**Let it cool.** Boiled water is potable once cooled. Boil in the evening, cool overnight, and it's ready for morning.

**Limitations:** Requires fire or a stove, fuel, and a heat-safe container. In a survival situation, this is often your first method if you already have fire for warmth.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Boiling does not remove chemical contamination — pesticides, heavy metals, or fuel runoff. In wilderness settings far from agricultural or industrial activity, this is rarely a concern. But avoid collecting water from immediately downstream of obvious pollution sources (road runoff, mining tailings) even if you plan to boil it.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Method 2: Filtration

A portable water filter physically strains biological contaminants through a membrane with microscopic pores.

**Pump filters** (Katadyn Hiker, MSR MiniWorks): Push water through a ceramic or hollow-fiber membrane by pumping. Effective against bacteria and protozoa. Most do not filter viruses (add chemical treatment if virus risk exists — common in international travel but rare in U.S. wilderness).

**Gravity filters** (Platypus GravityWorks, Sawyer Gravity): Fill a reservoir and hang it — gravity pulls water through the filter into a clean reservoir below. Good for camp use, not fast for emergency.

**Squeeze filters** (Sawyer Squeeze, Sawyer Mini): Squeeze water through a filter into a bottle. Extremely lightweight and simple. One of the most popular backpacking filtration options.

**Hollow-fiber straws** (LifeStraw): Drink directly through the filter. Highly compact — fits in a first aid kit.

**Limitations:** Filters can freeze (hollow fibers burst in hard freezes), clog in silty water, and require mechanical parts that can break. Carry purification tablets as a backup.

## Method 3: Chemical Treatment

Chemical purification uses iodine or chlorine to kill biological threats. It's the lightest-weight and simplest backup method.

**Iodine tablets (Potable Aqua, Coghlan's):** Drop 1–2 tablets per liter of water. Wait 30 minutes (60 minutes for cold or cloudy water). The water is safe to drink. The taste is often described as medicinal but tolerable.

**Chlorine dioxide tablets (Aquatabs, MSR Aquatabs):** Effective against bacteria, viruses, and *Cryptosporidium* (which iodine doesn't reliably kill). Takes 4 hours for Cryptosporidium treatment — plan ahead.

**Bleach (emergency backup):** Eight drops of unscented household bleach (6% sodium hypochlorite) per gallon of clear water, 16 drops for cloudy water. Wait 30 minutes. This is a last resort — use proper tablets when possible.

**Limitations:** Doesn't remove sediment or chemical contamination. Iodine shouldn't be used long-term and should be avoided by people with thyroid conditions or iodine sensitivity. Pre-filter silty water through a bandana before treating.

## Pre-Treatment: Settling and Pre-Filtering

Silty or cloudy water clogs filters and reduces chemical treatment effectiveness. When possible, pre-treat:

1. Fill a container and let it sit for 30 minutes — silt settles to the bottom.
2. Carefully pour the clear upper layer into another container for treatment.
3. Or strain through a bandana, coffee filter, or tightly woven fabric folded several times.

Clear water is safer and treats more effectively than turbid water.
