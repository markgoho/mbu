Fire brings warmth. Signaling brings rescue. The two skills complement each other — your fire from Req 6 is also one of your most powerful signals. But fire is just one option in a toolkit that includes mirrors, sound, ground patterns, and improvised devices.

{{< drg/image src="images/signal-fire-smoke-column.avif" alt="A tall column of white-gray smoke rising above a forest canopy against a blue sky, showing a signal fire visible from a distance" >}}

## The Core Signaling Principle

Every signaling method works by creating a contrast — something that looks different from the natural environment. Aircraft search teams are looking for straight lines (unusual in nature), bright reflections, distinctive color contrasts, or regular patterns (like three evenly spaced fires, the international distress signal).

Your signals need to be:
- **Visible from distance:** Think big. A signal visible from 50 feet helps nobody.
- **Attention-getting:** Movement, flashes, smoke columns, and audible sounds trigger response.
- **Repeatable:** Continuous or regular signals are more likely to be noticed than a single burst.

## Signaling Fire

A fire — especially a smoky one — is among the most visible signals available. Smoke can be seen for miles and is unmistakable from the air.

**Day signal (smoke):** Add green leaves, wet grass, or rubber (from a tire, boot sole, or bag) to a fire to produce thick white or dark smoke. White smoke shows against dark forest; dark smoke shows against sky and cloud.

**Night signal (flame):** A bright flame is visible for miles in dark conditions. Keep fuel dry to maximize flame height. A mirror-positioned mylar emergency blanket behind the fire can amplify the light.

**Three fires in a triangle or line:** The international distress signal is three fires arranged in a triangle or straight line, evenly spaced. The pattern is what signals distress — a random single fire might be a campfire.

**Signal fire placement:** Build your fire in the most open, elevated, visible location you can safely reach. Forest clearings, open hilltops, lakeshores, and meadows are ideal. Avoid lighting signal fires in dry grass or near forest if there's significant fire risk — common sense applies.

## Audible Signals

**Three blasts on a whistle:** The universal distress signal. Three short blasts, pause, repeat. Continue until you hear a response or rescue arrives. A whistle carries about a mile through forest — far better than shouting, which carries a few hundred feet and exhausts you.

**Three calls or shouts:** "Help!" three times, pause, repeat. Use this only if you don't have a whistle and there's reason to believe rescuers are nearby.

**Banging:** Metal on metal (pot and rock, rock and knife spine) carries sound farther than voice. Three evenly spaced bangs.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Always use audible signals in sets of three. One sounds accidental. Two sounds like an echo. Three is recognized as a distress signal by anyone who has basic wilderness training.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Ground-to-Air Visual Signals

These signals are made visible to search aircraft from above. Use contrasting materials — dark rocks or logs on snow, bright material on dark ground, or patterns stamped into snow or sand. See Req 7c for the specific symbols and their meanings.

**Size matters:** Ground-to-air signals need to be large to be visible from aircraft altitude. Each element should be at least 10 feet long, with the full symbol 3–4 times that size. Tiny symbols go unnoticed.

**Placement:** Open terrain — clearings, lake edges, ridges, beaches — where aircraft have clear sight lines.

## Water-Based Signals

If you're stranded on or near water:

**Whistle and wave:** A whistle is your primary audible signal. Wave bright clothing overhead with both arms (not a casual wave) — exaggerated motion contrasts with the environment.

**Dye marker:** Some survival kits include a sea dye marker that creates a vivid colored patch on water, visible from the air for hours.

**Mirror flash:** A signal mirror works over open water just as effectively as over land — and the lack of obstructions means the flash reaches farther.

**Strobe light:** Many personal locator beacons (PLBs) and emergency kits include strobes for nighttime water signaling.
