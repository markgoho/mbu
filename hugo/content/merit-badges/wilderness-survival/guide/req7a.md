Building a signal fire is useless if no one is looking. But once rescuers are in your area — whether on foot, in a helicopter, or in a boat — your job is to make yourself as visible and audible as possible. Different signals work for different rescue approaches.

{{< drg/image src="images/signal-fire-smoke-column.avif" alt="A signal fire producing a dark smoke column visible against a clear blue sky in an open meadow" >}}

## The Universal Distress Signal

Before getting into specific techniques, know the universal rule: **three signals = distress.**

- Three whistle blasts
- Three gunshots (with pause between each group)
- Three fires in a triangle pattern
- Three flashes with a mirror
- Three smoke signals

Groups of three have been the distress signal by international convention for over a century. Any rescuer — on land, air, or water — recognizes it.

## Signals for Ground Search Teams

Ground teams move on foot through terrain. They're close, but visibility is often limited by trees and brush.

### Audio Signals (Most Effective for Ground Teams)

**Whistle:** A standard plastic whistle produces about 100 decibels — twice as loud as shouting, achievable indefinitely without tiring, and audible from up to a mile in open terrain. Three blasts, pause, repeat.

**Voice:** Shouting "HELP" works at close range, but exhausts you and carries less than a whistle. Reserve shouting for when you hear or see rescuers nearby.

### Visual Signals for Ground Teams

**Fire and smoke:** A fire with dark smoke (add green vegetation, rubber, or plastic if available) is visible above the treeline. Three fires in a triangle, 50–100 feet apart, are the classic distress signal.

**Ground-to-air symbols:** Large symbols created on the ground using rocks, logs, or trampled vegetation (covered in detail in Req 7c). Ground teams looking at ridgelines and open areas will spot these.

**Brightly colored gear:** Lay bright tarps, rain gear, or sleeping bags in open areas visible from elevated vantage points. Orange and yellow are most visible.

## Signals for Airborne Search Teams

Aircraft — helicopters, light planes, drones — cover enormous ground quickly but move fast. Your signal must be:

1. **Large** — visible from altitude and at speed
2. **Contrasting** — standing out from natural terrain colors
3. **Positioned in open areas** — trees obstruct aerial visibility

### Mirror Flashes (Most Effective for Aircraft)

A signal mirror's flash can be seen from 10 miles away in clear conditions — farther than any smoke signal. Helicopter pilots scanning for survivors watch for unusual reflections.

How to use one is covered in detail in Req 7b.

### Signal Fire

A fire visible from above requires open sky overhead. Burn green vegetation for white smoke in forested areas (contrasts with dark trees), or black rubber/plastic for dark smoke in desert or snow (contrasts with light backgrounds).

### Whistle

Jet helicopters are loud — your whistle won't carry above engine noise. But turboprop search aircraft are slower and quieter. Signal both visually and audibly.

### Personal Locator Beacon (PLB) or EPIRB

If you have one, activate it. A PLB sends your GPS coordinates to satellite-based rescue coordination via the COSPAS-SARSAT system. Rescuers know your exact location within minutes. This is by far the most effective signaling device for aerial teams.

{{< drg/tip >}}
If you hear or see a search aircraft, create your signals immediately — don't wait for it to get closer. Aircraft move fast and may not turn around for a late signal. Have your mirror in your hand, your whistle around your neck, and a pile of green vegetation ready to throw on a fire at all times.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Signals for Water Search Teams

Water rescues typically involve boats, personal watercraft, or rescue swimmers. Open water provides line of sight in all directions — your signals can be seen from far away.

### Visual Signals on Water

**Bright colors:** In the water, your survival suit, PFD, or bright clothing is your primary signal. Face-up position maximizes your visibility.

**Mirror flash:** On or near water, a mirror is extremely effective — water reflects light readily, and clear maritime air carries reflections farther than haze-filled land environments.

**Dye marker:** Some survival kits include sea dye markers (typically bright green or orange). When deployed, they create a large, strikingly colored patch on the water visible from aircraft.

**Waving arms:** The international waving distress signal — both arms fully raised and lowered repeatedly — is the recognized water distress signal for surface rescuers. Do NOT wave with just one arm (that looks like a friendly wave).

### Audio Signals on Water

**Whistle:** Carries well across open water.

**Air horn (if available):** Commercial marine distress signals. Three blasts.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Aircraft Spotted — What Do You Do?" >}}
You're in a forest clearing and see a search helicopter moving parallel to you about a mile away.

- **Grab your signal mirror immediately** — aim toward the aircraft, not at the sun
- **Throw green vegetation on your fire** if you have one going
- **Start your whistle** (three blasts repeated)
- **Lay brightly colored gear in the open** — spread it wide, not in a pile
- **Keep signaling until the aircraft acknowledges** (rocks wings or dips nose) or passes out of view
- **Don't assume they saw you.** Signal every time an aircraft is in range.
