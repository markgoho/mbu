{{< drg/requirement number="5a" >}}
Describe the steps you would take to survive in the following exposure conditions:
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This requirement covers five distinct environments, each with its own threats and strategies. The key insight is that the same survival priorities apply everywhere — STOP, first aid, shelter, fire, water, signaling, food — but the way you execute each priority changes dramatically based on conditions.

## 1. Cold and Snowy

Cold and snow are among the most dangerous conditions because heat loss is relentless. Your body burns calories fighting the cold every second, and wet clothing or wind accelerates the process. The clock starts ticking the moment you stop moving.

**Immediate actions:**

- **Find or create shelter from wind.** Wind chill can make a 30°F day feel like 0°F. Get behind a natural windbreak — a rock face, dense evergreens, or a snowbank. If none exist, build a snow wall on the windward side.
- **Insulate yourself from the ground.** The frozen ground pulls heat from your body faster than cold air does. Pile branches, leaves, pine needles, or your pack under you. Never sit or lie directly on snow or frozen earth.
- **Build a fire if possible.** Create a platform of green logs so your fire does not melt down into the snow and extinguish itself. Gather three times more firewood than you think you need — cold nights are long.
- **Melt snow for water.** Do not eat snow directly — it lowers your core temperature and takes energy to melt internally. Melt it in a container near your fire.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Sweating in cold weather is dangerous. When you stop moving, wet clothing from sweat rapidly chills your body. Adjust layers to prevent overheating during physical work, and change into dry clothing as soon as possible.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## 2. Wet

Constant rain or being soaked after a water crossing presents a specific threat: wet clothing loses up to 90% of its insulating value. You do not need sub-freezing temperatures to develop hypothermia when you are soaked.

**Immediate actions:**

- **Get out of the rain.** Any overhead cover helps — dense tree canopy, rock overhangs, a tarp or poncho strung between trees. Even a large trash bag with holes for your head and arms reduces heat loss.
- **Wring out and change clothing.** If you have dry clothing, change. If not, wring out what you are wearing and put it back on — wrung-out clothing insulates better than sopping wet fabric.
- **Start a fire.** This is harder in wet conditions but not impossible. Look for dry tinder inside dead standing trees (snap off branches — if they are dry inside, the wood will work). Birch bark ignites even when damp. Use your fire-starting kit from [Req 4](/merit-badges/wilderness-survival/guide/req4/).
- **Create a drying line near your fire.** Hang wet clothing close enough to dry but not so close that it scorches or melts.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Dead standing wood — trees that have died but are still upright — is your best fuel source in wet conditions. The bark sheds rain while the interior stays relatively dry. Snap branches off the trunk rather than picking wood up from the wet ground.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## 3. Hot and Dry

Desert and arid environments reverse the usual priorities. Water becomes your most urgent need, and getting out of the sun takes the place of getting out of the cold.

**Immediate actions:**

- **Get into shade immediately.** Direct sun exposure in a desert can raise body temperature dangerously fast. If no natural shade exists, create it — drape a tarp, emergency blanket (reflective side out), or extra clothing over a frame of sticks.
- **Minimize physical activity during the day.** Move only during dawn, dusk, or night. Rest in shade during peak heat (10 a.m. to 4 p.m.).
- **Conserve water — but do not ration it.** Drink when you are thirsty. The water in your bottle keeps you alive; the water you save "for later" does nothing if you collapse from heatstroke today.
- **Protect yourself from the ground.** Desert ground surfaces can exceed 150°F. Sit on your pack or insulating material, not directly on sand or rock.
- **Watch for signs of dehydration and heatstroke** (reviewed in [Req 1a](/merit-badges/wilderness-survival/guide/req1a/)).

## 4. Windy

Wind alone can be survivable, but wind combined with cold or wet conditions becomes extremely dangerous through wind chill. A 40°F day with a 30 mph wind has an effective temperature of 28°F — below freezing.

**Immediate actions:**

- **Find a windbreak.** Get behind terrain features that block the wind: rock walls, dense brush, fallen logs, hillsides. The leeward (downwind) side of any large object is calmer.
- **Build your shelter low to the ground.** Wind speed increases with height. A shelter that is only 3 feet tall catches less wind than one that is 6 feet tall.
- **Anchor everything.** Wind can scatter gear, blow out fires, and collapse shelters. Weight down tarps with rocks, dig fire pits below ground level, and keep your pack secured.
- **Protect your fire.** Build a windscreen from rocks or logs on the windward side of your fire. Without protection, wind will either blow your fire out or spread it dangerously.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Wind chill only affects exposed skin and underdressed bodies — it does not make water freeze faster or lower the actual air temperature. But for a human being, wind chill is the temperature that matters, because it determines how fast your body loses heat.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## 5. At or On the Water

Water emergencies — capsized boats, flash floods, unexpected water crossings — create immediate danger because cold water saps body heat 25 times faster than cold air at the same temperature. In 50°F water, you may have less than an hour before incapacitation.

**Immediate actions:**

- **Get out of the water as fast as possible.** Swim to shore, climb onto a capsized boat, grab floating debris. Every minute in cold water accelerates hypothermia.
- **If you cannot get out, assume the HELP position.** Heat Escape Lessening Position: draw your knees to your chest, cross your arms over your chest, and keep your head above water. This protects the high-heat-loss areas (head, armpits, groin) and can extend your survival time significantly.
- **If in a group in the water, huddle together.** Face each other in a tight circle with arms around each other. This reduces heat loss for everyone.
- **Once out of the water, treat for hypothermia immediately.** Remove wet clothing, insulate from the ground, and warm the person with body heat, dry clothing, or fire.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Capsized Canoe on a Cold Lake" >}}
Your canoe flips on a windy lake. The water is 55°F and shore is 200 yards away.

- **Step one:** Stay with the canoe if possible — it is more visible to rescuers than a swimmer's head. Hold onto the hull.
- **Step two:** If you must swim, use a slow, steady stroke to conserve energy. Do not remove your shoes — they provide some insulation.
- **Step three:** Once on shore, immediately strip off wet clothing and wring it out. Put on any dry clothing from your waterproof bag (this is why dry bags matter).
- **Step four:** Build a fire as fast as you can. Hypothermia is already setting in.
- **Step five:** If a fire is not possible, get into a sleeping bag or wrap in an emergency blanket and have a partner share body heat.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}
