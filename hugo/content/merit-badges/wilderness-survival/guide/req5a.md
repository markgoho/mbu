Describe the steps you would take to survive in the following exposure conditions:
- Cold and snowy
- Wet
- Hot and dry
- Windy
- At or on the water
{{</* /drg/requirement */>}}

Exposure is the #1 killer in wilderness survival situations. Whether you're facing bitter cold, intense heat, or cold wet conditions, your response is always the same: **minimize heat loss or gain, find or build shelter, and signal for rescue**. This requirement asks you to describe your survival strategy for five different exposure scenarios. Your counselor will present scenarios and expect you to talk through the steps clearly.

The five exposure conditions you need to handle are:
- Cold and snowy
- Wet
- Hot and dry
- Windy
- At or on water

## Cold and Snowy Conditions

### **The Hazard**
Hypothermia (dangerously low core temperature) can set in within hours in cold conditions, especially if you're wet or exhausted. Frostbite (freezing of extremities) follows. Wind makes everything worse.

### **Your Survival Strategy**

**Immediate (First 30 minutes):**
1. **Stop and assess.** Don't panic or run around—that burns energy and increases heat loss.
2. **Find or build shelter immediately.** Cold is your enemy. A hastily built shelter is better than a slowly perfected one.
3. **Get dry.** Remove wet clothing if you can replace it. If not, wring out layers and insulate yourself against wind.

**Shelter Options (in order of speed):**
- **Find existing shelter:** Rock overhang, dense conifer forest, or abandoned structures.
- **Build a snow cave:** Dig into a snowbank. A snow cave is phenomenal insulation—the inside can be 30°F warmer than outside.
- **Build a windbreak:** Stack branches or create a lean-to with branches and bark.
- **Create a human nest:** Pile leaves, branches, or pine needles. It sounds crude, but it works—insulation is insulation.

**Maintain Warmth:**
4. **Build a fire if possible.** Use your ferro rod or matches. Even a small fire makes a huge difference.
5. **Eat high-calorie food.** Your body burns calories to generate heat. Eat frequently—every few hours.
6. **Keep moving gently.** Don't exhaust yourself, but gentle movement (gathering firewood, insulating your shelter) generates warmth.
7. **Stay dry.** Once you have shelter and heat, your priority is staying dry and warm until rescue.

**Signaling:**
8. **Make signals visible.** Bright cloth, debris arranged in SOS, or smoke from your fire can be seen for miles in snowy terrain.

{{</* drg/safety-first */>}}
In cold and snowy conditions, your morale will test you. You'll be cold, uncomfortable, and scared. Remember: rescue teams will search your planned route. Your job is to survive the night, signal in the morning, and wait. You're not trying to hike out in darkness.
{{</* /drg/safety-first */>}}

## Wet Conditions (Rain, Streams, Moisture)

### **The Hazard**
Wet cold is more dangerous than dry cold. Wet clothing loses insulation rapidly. Even in mild weather, wet + wind = hypothermia. Rain also reduces visibility, making rescue harder.

### **Your Survival Strategy**

**Immediate:**
1. **Find shelter from rain.** Under a rock overhang, dense evergreen forest, or inside a fallen tree.
2. **Stop getting wetter.** If you're soaked, change into dry clothes if you have them. If not, wring out layers.
3. **Build a fire for warmth and morale.** In wet conditions, finding tinder that burns even when damp is critical—use the cotton-vaseline tinder from your survival kit.

**Shelter Building:**
4. **Create a waterproof shelter.** A lean-to with a roof that sheds water is better than an open camp. Use bark, branches, and your emergency tarp if you have one.
5. **Elevate yourself off the ground.** Wet ground drains heat from your body. Build a bed of branches or leaves inside your shelter.
6. **Seal gaps.** Pack leaves or mud into gaps to reduce wind and rain penetration.

**Fire Building in Wet Conditions:**
7. **Gather dry wood before darkness.** Look inside a dead tree or under its branches—the interior is often dry even after heavy rain.
8. **Feather dry wood:** Use your knife to shave thin curls from the inside of dry wood. These burn even when the outside is damp.
9. **Keep the fire small but hot.** A small, hot fire is better than a large, wet, smoking fire.

**Managing Water:**
10. **Have water available but find ways to purify it.** Boiling is your best option (rainwater, stream water). Boil for 1 minute to kill most pathogens.

## Hot and Dry Conditions (Desert)

### **The Hazard**
Dehydration is the primary killer in desert conditions. Heat exhaustion and heatstroke follow. High sun exposure causes severe burns. The environment is hostile.

### **Your Survival Strategy**

**Immediate:**
1. **Find or create shade.** Direct sun in a desert will kill you faster than almost anything. Rock overhang, dead tree, or even a hastily rigged emergency tarp are life-savers.
2. **Reduce activity during peak heat (11am–3pm).** Rest in shade, conserve sweat (which requires water).
3. **Assess your water situation.** How much water do you have? How long must you survive? Plan accordingly.

**Shelter and Heat Management:**
4. **Build or improve shelter.** Cover yourself from direct sun. A simple frame of branches with cloth draped over it works.
5. **Wear light-colored, loose clothing.** It reflects heat and lets air circulate. Remove layers if needed, but avoid sunburn.
6. **Create ventilation in your shelter.** Stagnant, hot air inside a shelter will cook you. Allow some airflow while keeping out direct sun.

**Water Conservation:**
7. **Drink water strategically.** Drink enough to prevent heat exhaustion, but don't gulp large amounts. Sip regularly.
8. **Avoid caffeine and alcohol.** Both increase dehydration.
9. **Avoid salty food without water.** Salt increases thirst and dehydration.
10. **Find water sources.** Look for low-lying areas, vegetation, animal tracks (often lead to water), or seepage areas near rock formations.
11. **Purify questionable water.** Boil if possible, or use tablets/filters.

**Signaling and Rescue:**
12. **Make your location visible.** Arrange bright cloth or reflective materials. A signal mirror can be seen for miles in clear desert air.
13. **Stay put.** The temptation to walk toward imagined water sources is strong—resist it. Rescue teams will search your planned route. You're more likely to be found if you stay visible.

{{</* drg/did-you-know */>}}
In the desert, walking decreases your survival chances. A lost person who hikes looking for rescue often wanders 10+ miles from their last known location. Rescue teams can't search everywhere. Staying put, visible, and conserving energy doubles your survival odds.
{{</* /drg/did-you-know */>}}

## Windy Conditions

### **The Hazard**
Wind combines with any temperature to amplify heat loss. A 20°F day with 20mph wind feels like -10°F (wind chill). High winds also make fire-building difficult and increase the risk of falling branches.

### **Your Survival Strategy**

**Immediate:**
1. **Get out of the wind.** Even a slight slope, rock formation, or forest can block wind significantly.
2. **Build windproof shelter.** Face your shelter away from prevailing winds. Use dense materials (branches, leaves, snow) to block wind penetration.
3. **Secure your shelter.** High winds can destroy improvised shelters. Lash branches together and weight down loose parts.

**Protective Measures:**
4. **Dress in layers and seal gaps.** Wind penetrates loose clothing. Layer up, then create a windproof outer layer.
5. **Protect extremities.** Hands, feet, ears, and nose lose heat fastest in wind. Cover them completely.
6. **Increase calorie intake.** Wind increases energy needs. Eat more frequently.

**Fire and Safety:**
7. **Build a fire windbreak.** A wall of rocks or logs on the windward side protects your fire and reflects heat toward you.
8. **Secure large branches overhead.** Dead branches blown down by wind are a hazard. Clear your shelter area.

**Signaling:**
9. **Secure your signals.** Wind will blow away loose cloth or debris. Tie signals down or arrange them in patterns that persist.

## Water Emergencies (Capsized Boat, Water Stranding)

### **The Hazard**
Cold water causes shock and hypothermia in minutes, even in "mild" water temperatures. Being stranded on water isolates you from land resources and increases rescue time.

### **Your Survival Strategy**

**Immediate (First Minutes—Critical):**
1. **Stay with the boat.** A capsized boat is larger and easier to spot than a person in water. Even if the boat is damaged, hold onto it.
2. **Stay calm.** Panic increases heart rate and accelerates hypothermia. Use box breathing to control panic.
3. **Get out of the water if possible.** Even sitting on an overturned boat is warmer than being submerged.
4. **Huddle together.** If multiple people are in the water, huddle together to share warmth (huddling reduces heat loss by 20%).

**Signaling for Rescue:**
5. **Make yourself visible.** Use bright clothing, a whistle, or a signal mirror. Boats and aircraft can spot you if you signal.
6. **Activate emergency systems.** Use an EPIRB (Emergency Position Indicating Radio Beacon) if you have one, or a satellite communicator.
7. **Signal patterns.** Three blasts on a whistle = distress. Waves of your arms = distress. Bright orange equipment = visibility.

**If You Reach Land:**
8. **Get out of wet clothing.** Change into dry clothes or replace wet layers immediately.
9. **Build shelter and fire** as you would in any wilderness survival situation.
10. **Create signals visible from water.** Aircraft and boat rescuers will search the area. Make yourself visible.

## The Universal Survival Principle

Across all five conditions, the strategy is the same:
1. **Assess and calm yourself.** Stop, think, act.
2. **Find or build shelter immediately.** Shelter is priority #1.
3. **Build a fire if possible.** Fire provides warmth, signals, and water purification.
4. **Signal for rescue.** Rescuers know to search your planned route—make yourself findable.
5. **Wait.** You're not hiking out in an emergency situation. You're surviving until rescue arrives.

## Before You Move On

Your counselor will present scenarios: "It's getting dark, you're stranded, and it's 30°F and raining. Walk me through your plan." Be ready to describe specific steps for the condition you face. You should be able to discuss each of the five exposure types with confidence and detail.
