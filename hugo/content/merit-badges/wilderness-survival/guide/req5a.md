{{< drg/requirement number="5a" >}}
Shelter. Describe the steps you would take to survive in the following exposure conditions: Cold and snowy, Wet, Hot and dry, Windy, At or on the water.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Every environment presents a different threat to your body. Your response strategy changes depending on what's killing you. This page covers all five exposure conditions, describing the specific priorities and actions for each.

{{< drg/image src="images/five-exposure-environments.avif" alt="A comparison of five wilderness environments: snowy forest, rainy mountain trail, desert canyon, windy open ridge, and lake shoreline at dusk" >}}

## Cold and Snowy

Cold and snowy conditions are the classic wilderness survival scenario. Hypothermia (covered in Req 1a) and frostbite are the primary threats.

**Step 1 — Protect from wind.** Wind dramatically accelerates heat loss. Find or build a windbreak as your first priority. Even a dense stand of conifers significantly reduces wind exposure.

**Step 2 — Insulate from the ground.** Snow and cold ground conduct heat away from your body much faster than air does. Sit and sleep on insulation — a sleeping pad, your pack, boughs, leaves, anything that creates a barrier. A few inches of insulation beneath you is worth more than extra layers on top.

**Step 3 — Build or find shelter.** Snow itself is an excellent insulator — a properly constructed snow shelter maintains around 32°F inside even in −30°F conditions. A debris hut or a snow trench can save your life. See Req 5d for detailed construction guidance.

**Step 4 — Build fire.** Cold-weather fire building requires dry tinder, which is challenging in snow. Look for dead branches still attached to standing trees (these stay drier than ground wood). The dry underside of bark, the inner wood of split logs, and birch bark are good tinder sources in wet-cold conditions.

**Step 5 — Prevent frostbite.** Keep extremities covered and check them regularly. Ears, nose, fingers, and toes are most vulnerable. Early frostbite (frostnip) causes redness and stinging — rewarm gently with body heat. Do not rub.

## Wet

Wet conditions are deceptive — 50°F in the rain doesn't feel life-threatening, but wet clothing loses up to 90% of its insulating value. Hypothermia is fully possible in summer temperatures.

**Step 1 — Get dry immediately.** If clothing is wet, the priority is to change into dry layers or wring out what you have as thoroughly as possible. Even damp-but-wrung clothing insulates better than soaked clothing.

**Step 2 — Get out of the rain.** Find natural overhead cover (rock overhangs, dense conifer canopy) or construct a waterproof barrier. A large plastic bag, tarp, or poncho — even improvised from large leaves — can be enough to stay dry while building a better shelter.

**Step 3 — Build fire despite the rain.** Building fire in wet conditions is one of the most important and challenging survival skills. Focus on finding dry tinder (inside logs, under bark, in dead standing wood) and protecting the initial fire with your body or an improvised barrier until it establishes. See Req 6 for fire-starting techniques.

**Step 4 — Maintain warmth.** Once you're sheltered and as dry as possible, focus on insulation. Moving warms you — physical activity generates heat — but at the cost of sweat, which then chills you when you stop. Find the balance.

## Hot and Dry

In desert survival, the primary threats are reversed: heat, sun, and dehydration rather than cold and wet. The environment wants to cook you rather than freeze you.

**Step 1 — Stop moving during peak heat.** The desert rule is rest from 10 AM to 4 PM, travel during early morning and evening. Every step in 100°F sun burns water and generates metabolic heat faster than most people realize.

**Step 2 — Build shade.** Shade is shelter in the desert. Air temperature in the shade can be 20–30°F cooler than in direct sun. Find natural shade (canyon walls, rock overhangs, large rocks) or construct a simple lean-to from available material. Elevation above the ground also matters — a platform of rocks or branches keeps you out of radiative heat from the desert floor.

**Step 3 — Retain water.** This is the opposite of wet conditions, where water is a threat. Breathe through your nose (reduces exhaled moisture loss), avoid unnecessary talking, and if you have water, drink it. Rationing water while hiking in heat makes dehydration worse, not better.

**Step 4 — Signal actively.** Reflective surfaces (mirrors, foil blankets) are extremely effective in desert sun. Sand can be used to create large ground-to-air signals. Aircraft and desert highways are often visible from high ground.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
In a desert survival situation, do not eat unless you have water to spare. Digestion requires water, and eating without adequate hydration accelerates dehydration. This connects directly to the reasoning behind Priority 7 (food) in Req 2.

## Windy

Wind is a force multiplier for every other threat. In cold conditions, wind chill can drop the effective temperature by 40°F or more. In hot conditions, wind carries moisture away from your skin and can cause rapid dehydration. In wet conditions, wind drives rain through any gap in shelter.

**Step 1 — Find a windbreak.** This is almost always the first priority. Ridgelines, open meadows, and exposed slopes amplify wind. Valleys, dense forest, large rock formations, and cliff faces provide shelter. The windward side of any large feature blocks the wind; the leeward side (downwind) is where you want to be.

**Step 2 — Seal your clothing.** Wind penetrates loose clothing and strips heat. Cinch every adjustable layer: draw the hood tight, close neck gators, tuck in shirts. If you have rain gear, put it on — not because it's raining, but because its windproofing dramatically reduces heat loss.

**Step 3 — Build sheltered fire.** Wind-exposed fires blow sideways, burn fast, and are difficult to start. Build fire in a natural windbreak or construct a stone or log barrier. A small fire in a windbreak is more effective than a large fire in the open.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The wind chill chart used by meteorologists shows that a temperature of 20°F with 30 mph winds feels like −5°F to exposed skin. Frostbite can occur in 30 minutes under those conditions — faster than most people expect.

## At or on the Water

Water survival involves a different threat profile: drowning and immersion hypothermia are the immediate dangers, followed by the challenges of being stranded on water without resources.

**If you're in the water (unplanned immersion):**

**Step 1 — Don't panic.** Cold water triggers an involuntary gasp reflex. If you've just fallen in, let yourself surface before trying to swim. Breathe.

**Step 2 — Signal and stay visible.** Wave arms, shout, whistle if you have one. Rescuers are often nearby immediately after a capsize.

**Step 3 — H.E.L.P. or Huddle.** The **H.E.L.P.** position (Heat Escape Lessening Position) reduces heat loss: hug your knees to your chest, cross your arms over your chest. If others are in the water with you, huddle together — body contact slows heat loss for everyone.

**Step 4 — Stay with your boat.** An overturned vessel is a more visible rescue target than a floating person. A capsized canoe, kayak, or boat is also a flotation device. Cling to it rather than swimming to shore unless shore is very close.

**If you're stranded on water (on a vessel or improvised raft):**

Prioritize shelter from sun and wind, signaling, and water collection. Do not drink seawater or untreated river water — it accelerates dehydration. Collect rain water when possible.
