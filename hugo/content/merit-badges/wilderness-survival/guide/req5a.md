{{< drg/requirement number="5a" >}}
Describe the steps you would take to survive in the following exposure conditions: Cold and snowy, Wet, Hot and dry, Windy, At or on the water.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Every environment tries to kill you differently. Cold steals your heat. Heat steals your water. Wind multiplies both. Water steals your heat 25 times faster than air at the same temperature. Understanding how each exposure condition threatens you — and what to do about it — is the foundation of survival sheltering.

## Cold and Snowy

**Primary threats:** Hypothermia, frostbite, exhaustion from moving through snow

**Steps to survive:**

1. **Get out of the wind.** Wind chill is the real danger — a 20°F day with a 30 mph wind feels like -10°F on exposed skin. Find or build a windbreak immediately.

2. **Insulate from the ground.** Snow-covered ground will suck heat from your body through conduction. Pile evergreen boughs, leaves, or your pack beneath you. Never sit or lie directly on snow.

3. **Build or find shelter.** A snow trench, tree well, or quinzhee (hollowed-out snow mound) can maintain near-freezing temperatures inside even when it's well below zero outside. Snow is an excellent insulator — igloos stay around 32°F inside regardless of outside conditions.

4. **Start a fire if possible.** Build your fire on a platform of green logs to prevent it from melting down into the snow and extinguishing itself.

5. **Stay dry.** Sweat is your enemy in cold weather. If you're working hard enough to sweat (building shelter, gathering wood), remove a layer. Wet clothing loses up to 90% of its insulating value.

6. **Stay hydrated.** Cold suppresses your thirst reflex, but you're still losing water through breathing (that cloud you exhale is moisture leaving your body). Melt snow to drink — never eat snow directly, as it lowers your core temperature.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Snow is about 90% trapped air, which makes it one of nature's best insulators. A snow cave can be 50°F warmer than the outside air. That's why animals like grouse and ptarmigan burrow into snow to survive arctic nights.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Wet Conditions

**Primary threats:** Hypothermia (even in mild temperatures), trench foot, low morale

**Steps to survive:**

1. **Get dry or stay as dry as possible.** If you have rain gear, put it on before you're soaked — wet clothing loses insulating value dramatically. If you're already wet, wring out clothing and layer rain gear over it.

2. **Find or create overhead cover.** A tarp, space blanket draped over a ridge line, or even a thick canopy of evergreen branches can keep rain off you. In a survival situation, staying dry is more important than staying warm — because staying wet makes staying warm impossible.

3. **Build a fire.** This is harder in wet conditions but not impossible. Look for dry tinder under logs, inside dead standing trees, or in the centers of large dead branches. Birch bark burns even when wet due to its oil content. A fire dries your clothes and warms your core.

4. **Elevate yourself.** Don't sit in puddles or on wet ground. Build a platform from logs or branches. Even sitting on your pack is better than sitting in mud.

5. **Watch for hypothermia.** A 50°F rain with wind is one of the most dangerous exposure combinations. You can become hypothermic at temperatures well above freezing if you're wet and windblown. Monitor yourself and your group for the "umbles" — stumbling, mumbling, fumbling, grumbling.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Wet cold is more dangerous than dry cold. A calm 50°F rain can cause hypothermia faster than a dry, windless 20°F day. If you're wet and can't get dry, getting warm becomes your top priority.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Hot and Dry

**Primary threats:** Dehydration, heatstroke, sunburn, hyperthermia

**Steps to survive:**

1. **Find or create shade.** In desert and arid environments, direct sun can heat the ground surface to 150°F. Any shade — a rock overhang, a bush, a tarp — reduces your heat exposure significantly.

2. **Minimize activity during peak heat.** Do necessary work (shelter building, signaling) during early morning and late evening. Rest in shade from 10 AM to 4 PM. This single adjustment can cut your water needs in half.

3. **Conserve water — in your body.** The best place to store water is in your stomach, not your canteen. Drink what you have rather than rationing sips. Dehydration impairs your judgment before you realize it's happening.

4. **Protect your skin and head.** Cover up. Long sleeves and a hat actually keep you cooler than exposed skin in dry heat because they prevent moisture loss from evaporation and block UV radiation.

5. **Signal early and often.** In open desert terrain, signal mirrors are extraordinarily effective — a flash can be seen from over 10 miles away. Lay out ground signals in open areas.

6. **Stay off the ground.** In extreme heat, the ground radiates stored heat upward. Elevate yourself on brush or branches if possible.

{{< drg/tip >}}
In desert survival, your water requirements can exceed one gallon per hour during heavy exertion in full sun. By resting in shade during the hottest hours, you can reduce that to about a quart per hour — a four-fold improvement.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Windy Conditions

**Primary threats:** Wind chill, blown debris, hypothermia acceleration, fire hazards

**Steps to survive:**

1. **Find a windbreak immediately.** Wind accelerates heat loss from your body. A 40°F day with 30 mph wind has the same cooling effect as 13°F with no wind. Get behind terrain features, dense vegetation, fallen trees, or rock formations.

2. **Build shelter with wind direction in mind.** Your shelter opening should face away from the prevailing wind. Build low — a low-profile shelter catches less wind and retains more heat.

3. **Seal gaps.** Wind finds every opening. Pack gaps in your shelter with leaves, moss, mud, or snow. Even a thin layer of vegetation across the windward side makes a significant difference.

4. **Anchor everything.** Wind can scatter your gear, blow out your fire, and destroy a poorly anchored shelter. Weight down tarps with rocks, bury tent stakes deeply, and keep gear inside your shelter.

5. **Be cautious with fire.** Wind makes fires unpredictable and dangerous. Build a wind screen around your fire pit with rocks or logs. Keep fires small and controlled. Never leave a fire unattended in windy conditions.

## At or On the Water

**Primary threats:** Drowning, hypothermia from immersion, exposure to wind and spray

**Steps to survive:**

1. **Stay with your craft.** If a boat capsizes, stay with it. A capsized boat is more visible to rescuers than a swimmer, and it provides flotation. Climb on top of the hull if you can.

2. **Get out of the water as quickly as possible.** Water conducts heat away from your body 25 times faster than air. Even in 70°F water, you can become hypothermic within a few hours. In 50°F water, you have about an hour before incapacitation.

3. **Assume the HELP position.** If you're in the water and can't get out, adopt the Heat Escape Lessening Position: draw your knees to your chest, cross your arms, and press them against your chest. This protects the high-heat-loss areas (armpits, groin, sides of chest).

4. **Signal from shore.** Once on land, build a fire on the shoreline where it's visible from the water and air. Use bright-colored items or mirrors to attract attention.

5. **Treat for hypothermia immediately.** Remove wet clothing, wrap in dry layers or a space blanket, and warm gradually. Hot drinks (if available) help warm the core.
