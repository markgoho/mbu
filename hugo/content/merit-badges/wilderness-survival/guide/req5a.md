{{< drg/requirement number="5a" >}}
Describe the steps you would take to survive in the following exposure conditions: cold and snowy, wet, hot and dry, windy, and at or on the water.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Each environment kills in a different way. Cold kills through hypothermia, heat kills through dehydration and heat stroke, wet kills by compounding other problems, wind amplifies danger, and water presents drowning and cold shock. Your survival strategy changes based on the environment.

## Cold and Snowy

You can die of hypothermia in a few hours in cold, windy, wet conditions. Your body loses heat faster than it can produce it.

**What to do:**

1. **Find or build shelter immediately.** Snow can become an insulating layer. A snow cave provides excellent insulation (snow's air pockets trap heat). A simple lean-to using branches and evergreen boughs blocks wind.

2. **Get out of wet clothes.** Wet fabric loses 90% of its insulating value. If you get soaked, change into dry clothes immediately or wrap yourself in an emergency blanket or tarp.

3. **Build or find a fire.** Fire is your lifeline. Dried wood burns better than wet, so gather fuel from dead branches on trees (not from the ground). Start with small tinder (dry bark, leaves), add kindling, then larger fuel.

4. **Drink warm water.** Warm liquids help your core temperature. Avoid alcohol and caffeine (both increase heat loss).

5. **Stay active but don't sweat.** Exertion generates heat, but sweat then cools you down. Find the balance—enough activity to stay warm, but not so much that you sweat.

6. **Stay put.** Trying to hike out at night in snow is how people get lost. Build a camp, stay warm, and wait for rescue.

## Wet

Wet conditions are dangerous because water conducts heat away from your body 25 times faster than air. A rain situation compounds other problems.

**What to do:**

1. **Seek shelter from the rain.** A dense forest provides some shelter. A rock overhang or fallen tree offers more. If you have a tarp or emergency bivy, set it up immediately.

2. **Create a fire reflector.** Build a fire and place a rock wall or log behind it to reflect heat toward you. The heat reflects off surfaces and warms you more efficiently.

3. **Gather dry materials.** Look for dry wood in protected areas (under dense evergreens, in dead trees). Birch bark is excellent fire starter even when wet (the oils are waterproof). Cotton or dry leaves from inside cavities work well.

4. **Keep your fire going.** A small, hot fire is better than a large, smoking fire. Smoke means wet fuel. Feed it gradually with progressively larger pieces.

5. **Keep dry where possible.** Even if you're wet, keep your matches, lighter, and any fire-starter dry. Waterproof bags are worth their weight.

6. **Dry yourself gradually.** Once you have a fire, dry your clothes near it. Don't sit too close and scorch them—rotate them so all sides dry.

## Hot and Dry

The enemy is **dehydration and sun exposure.** You can become dangerously dehydrated in just a few hours without realizing it because sweat evaporates in the dry heat.

**What to do:**

1. **Find shade.** A rock overhang, dense vegetation, or even your emergency blanket stretched as a sunshade keeps direct sun off you and reduces heat stress significantly.

2. **Ration your water carefully.** Drink small amounts regularly, not huge gulps. It's better to sip continuously and preserve a small reserve than to gulp down water early and run out.

3. **Create a shelter for the night.** Nights in the desert can be surprisingly cold. Build a simple lean-to or use your emergency shelter to block wind and retain heat.

4. **Don't travel during peak heat (11 a.m.–4 p.m.).** If you need to move, travel in early morning and evening when it's cooler. This conserves water and prevents heat exhaustion.

5. **Cover your skin.** Wear long sleeves and a hat to reduce sun exposure and water loss. Light-colored clothing reflects heat; dark absorbs it.

6. **Look for water.** In deserts, water collects in rock hollows and sand washes. Look for vegetation—where there are plants, water is usually nearby underground.

## Windy

Wind multiplies danger by increasing heat loss (wind chill) and making it harder to build shelter or fire.

**What to do:**

1. **Build a windbreak.** Even a small wall of rocks or branches reduces wind chill significantly. Position your shelter perpendicular to the wind direction.

2. **Choose shelter location carefully.** Avoid exposed ridges and open areas. Look for natural windbreaks: rock formations, dense vegetation, or a depression in the ground.

3. **Secure your shelter.** Strong wind can destroy a poorly anchored shelter. Use rocks and rope to anchor it firmly.

4. **Build a fire in a protected location.** Light the fire on the downwind side of your shelter so smoke blows away but heat reflects back to you.

5. **Stay bundled up.** Wear all your layers. Wind can penetrate single layers—multiple layers trap warm air better.

6. **Stay hydrated.** You lose more water through respiration in cold, dry, windy conditions. Drink more than you think you need.

## At or On Water

Water emergencies present multiple hazards: drowning, cold shock, hypothermia, and disorientation.

**What to do (if capsized or in the water):**

1. **Stay with your vessel.** A boat or kayak is more visible to rescuers than your head. Hold onto it and call for help.

2. **Assume cold shock position.** If you fall into cold water, your body gasps involuntarily. Control your breathing and focus on staying calm. Most cold-water deaths happen in the first few minutes from cold shock or panic.

3. **Get out of the water if possible.** Even a partially submerged kayak is better than being fully immersed. Get as much of your body out of the water as you can.

4. **If swimming, conserve energy.** Thrashing around burns energy and accelerates heat loss. Float and wait for help if possible.

5. **Hypothermia sets in faster in water.** You can become hypothermic in water that feels "not that cold" (even 60°F). Seek warmth and shelter as soon as you're on land.

6. **A life jacket is non-negotiable.** Wear it every time you're in or near water. It keeps you afloat when exhausted and extends survival time.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Always wear a Coast Guard-approved life jacket in or near water. Swimming ability doesn't matter in cold water or in a panic situation. A life jacket keeps you alive.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

**What to do (if stranded on shore after water emergency):**

1. **Get warm and dry immediately.** Change out of wet clothes. Build a fire.
2. **Signal for rescue.** You're likely in an open area where rescuers can see you. Use bright colors and audible signals.
3. **Monitor for hypothermia.** Cold and wet is the combination that kills fastest.

## The Common Thread

In every environment, the priorities are: **Get to shelter, don't panic, signal if needed, maintain water and food intake, manage fire for warmth, and treat injuries.** The specific tactics change, but the framework stays the same.
