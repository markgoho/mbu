{{< drg/requirement number="5a" >}}
Describe the steps you would take to survive in the following exposure conditions: (1) Cold and snowy, (2) Wet, (3) Hot and dry, (4) Windy, (5) At or on the water.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This requirement asks you to think through five different environmental exposure scenarios and describe the survival steps for each. Every environment has its own hierarchy of threats — understanding which danger is most urgent in each setting is what drives good decisions.

{{< drg/image src="images/five-exposure-environments.avif" alt="Five illustrated scenes showing different survival environments: snowy forest, rainy trail, desert canyon, windy ridgeline, and a lake with overturned boat" >}}

## 1. Cold and Snowy

Cold and snowy conditions create the most lethal survival scenario for unprepared people. Hypothermia and frostbite can begin within minutes.

**Your survival steps:**

1. **Stop and assess using S.T.O.P.** Are you wet? Injured? How far from shelter or help?
2. **Get out of wind and precipitation immediately.** Wind plus cold creates wind chill that dramatically accelerates heat loss. Even moving to the lee (downwind) side of a tree cuts this dramatically.
3. **Insulate from the ground first.** The ground conducts heat away from your body 25 times faster than air. Pine boughs, a pack, dry leaves — anything between you and snow.
4. **Build a shelter.** In forested areas, a debris hut or lean-to with thick boughs. In open snow, a snow trench or quinzhee (snow mound with hollowed interior) provides effective windproof insulation.
5. **Get into dry clothes.** Wet clothing kills. Change layers if you have dry ones.
6. **Build a fire.** Warmth, signaling, morale, and water melting capability. Prioritize a location sheltered from wind.
7. **Signal.** Smoke is visible for miles in cold, clear winter air. Three fires in a triangle is the international distress signal.
8. **Stay put.** Movement in snow exhausts you quickly and can lead you further from trails and roads.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Frostbite affects fingers, toes, ears, and the nose first. Prevention: keep extremities dry and insulated. First aid for frostbite: warm the area gently against skin — do NOT rub, do NOT rewarm if there's any risk of refreezing. Refreezing frostbitten tissue causes much more severe damage than the original injury.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## 2. Wet

Wet conditions are dangerous because they degrade almost every type of insulation. Even at 50°F, a soaked hiker can develop hypothermia within hours.

**Your survival steps:**

1. **Prioritize staying dry above all else.** Use rain gear, improvise a tarp from a space blanket or trash bag.
2. **Find natural overhead cover.** Dense conifers shed rain well. Overhanging rock ledges provide excellent emergency shelter.
3. **Insulate from the ground.** Wet ground conducts heat away even faster than dry cold ground.
4. **Build fire — even in the rain.** This takes skill (see Req 6), but it's essential. Look for dry tinder under rock overhangs, inside hollow logs, or in your kit. Build a small platform of sticks to keep your fire lay off wet ground.
5. **Change wet clothing if dry clothes are available.** If not, keep moving enough to generate body heat.
6. **Build shelter from materials that shed water.** Bark, large leaves (like skunk cabbage in the Pacific Northwest), and dense conifer boughs all resist water.

{{< drg/tip >}}
In a rain poncho emergency, you can build a surprisingly effective rain shelter from a space blanket and paracord — string the paracord between two trees and drape the blanket over it at an angle so rain runs off to one side. You're dry in under five minutes.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## 3. Hot and Dry

Heat kills faster than most people realize. Heatstroke can develop within an hour of serious exertion in extreme desert heat. Your survival strategy flips many cold-weather instincts.

**Your survival steps:**

1. **Stop moving during the hottest part of the day.** In a desert survival situation, stay still from roughly 10am to 4pm. Travel in the morning and evening.
2. **Find shade immediately.** Rock overhangs, large boulders, canyon walls, vegetation — any shade reduces your core temperature and dramatically cuts sweat loss.
3. **Insulate from the hot ground.** Desert soil at midday can exceed 150°F. Sit on your pack or a folded sleeping pad.
4. **Wear all your clothing.** This seems counterintuitive, but loose, light-colored clothing reflects heat and slows sweat evaporation, extending your hydration by hours.
5. **Conserve water and sweat.** Move slowly and breathe through your nose rather than your mouth. Avoid alcohol, which accelerates dehydration.
6. **Signal from elevated positions in cooler hours.** Dawn and dusk are your signal windows when you're active.
7. **Look for water in low spots**, dry streambeds (dig down), cactus (pulp can be chewed, though beware false nutrition claims), or dew on vegetation in the morning.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
In extreme desert conditions, the human body can sweat up to 1.5 liters per hour. At that rate, even a person carrying a full 3-liter water supply is dehydrated within two hours if moving hard. Minimizing movement in desert survival situations is not laziness — it's mathematics.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## 4. Windy

Wind dramatically amplifies any cold or wet hazard (wind chill), but also creates independent risks — blowing debris, unpredictable fire behavior, difficulty hearing signals.

**Your survival steps:**

1. **Find wind shelter as first priority.** A person in 40mph wind at 40°F is experiencing conditions equivalent to 18°F. Drop into a ravine, behind a rock face, into dense trees — any barrier that breaks wind velocity.
2. **Stay low.** Wind speed decreases significantly near the ground. In exposed areas, traveling on all fours in gusts is safer than walking.
3. **Be cautious with fire.** Wind-blown sparks cause wildfires and can ignite your shelter. Build fires in a depression or sheltered hollow, and have a fire extinguisher plan (dirt, not water for most campfires).
4. **Anchor your shelter.** Any tarp or improvised shelter must be weighted down or staked firmly. An unsecured space blanket shelter becomes useless (and a choking hazard) in strong gusts.
5. **Protect your face and extremities.** Wind-driven particles (sand, ice crystals, debris) cause eye injury and frostbite. Use a buff, scarf, or any fabric to cover exposed skin.
6. **Signaling in wind:** Smoke signals are unreliable in high wind. Prioritize whistle, mirror, and ground-to-air symbols.

## 5. At or On the Water

Aquatic survival situations often involve capsizing, falling through ice, or being stranded on a raft or floatation device. Cold water immersion is the defining hazard.

**Your survival steps:**

1. **Don your PFD (personal flotation device) immediately.** If you don't have one, improvise flotation from an inflated dry bag, air-trapped clothing, or any buoyant material.
2. **Get out of the water as fast as possible.** Cold water (below 70°F) begins causing incapacitation in 30–60 minutes. Below 40°F, loss of swimming ability can occur in under 10 minutes.
3. **If you can't get to shore, assume H.E.L.P.** Heat Escape Lessening Position: draw your knees to your chest and cross your arms over your chest to slow core heat loss. Groups in water should huddle together.
4. **Don't try to swim unless shore is very close.** The energy and heat cost of swimming through cold water usually makes your situation worse. Float and preserve energy.
5. **Once on shore:** Get out of wet clothes, begin re-warming immediately, treat for hypothermia (see Req 1a). Build fire, improvise shelter.
6. **Signal from the water's edge.** Water reflects light and sound — a mirror flash or whistle carries well across open water to rescue boats or aircraft.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Capsized Canoe" >}}
You and a partner capsize in a lake two miles from shore. The water is 58°F — cold enough to cause real incapacitation risk.

- **Don't swim for it.** Two miles is too far in cold water. You may not make it.
- **Hold onto the canoe.** An overturned canoe still floats and provides a physical anchor. You're also much easier to spot from shore or air near a boat.
- **Assume H.E.L.P. position** if you must stay in the water.
- **Signal actively.** Use your whistle (should be on your PFD) and try to attract attention from anyone on shore.
- **Get to shore when it's close enough** — within a few hundred yards.
- **Treat for hypothermia immediately** once out of the water.
