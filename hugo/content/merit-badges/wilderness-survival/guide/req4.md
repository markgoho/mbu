You've learned *why* these kits matter (Req 1b) and what priorities they address (Req 2). Now it's time to build them and demonstrate that you know how to use every item.

The key word is **personal**. Your kit should reflect your health, allergies, and the activities you do most. A Scout with a nut allergy needs an EpiPen. A Scout who gets blisters easily needs blister pads. A Scout in a cold climate needs more insulating layers than one in a warm climate.

## Personal First-Aid Kit Components

Here's a minimal first-aid kit that covers most common backcountry injuries:

**Wound Care**
- Adhesive bandages (multiple sizes) — for minor cuts and scrapes
- Gauze pads (2x2" and 4x4") — for larger wounds
- Medical tape and athletic tape — holds gauze and prevents blisters
- Antibiotic ointment (Neosporin or similar) — prevents infection
- Tweezers — removes splinters and ticks

**Pain & Fever**
- Pain reliever (ibuprofen or acetaminophen) — for aches, fever, and severe pain
- Follow the age-appropriate dosage on the label

**Allergic Reactions & Itching**
- Antihistamine tablets (Benadryl) — for allergic reactions and itch relief
- EpiPen (if you have a serious peanut/bee allergy) — for life-threatening allergic shock

**Inflammation & Rashes**
- Hydrocortisone cream 1% — for insect bites, poison ivy, and rashes
- Ice pack or cold compress — for sprains and swelling

**Immobilization**
- Elastic bandage (ACE wrap) — for sprains and strains
- Triangular bandage — for slings and larger wraps
- Splint material (SAM splint or foam) — for fractures

**Blister Management**
- Blister pads or moleskin — for preventing and treating blisters
- This is one of the most important items because blisters end hikes

**Special Considerations**
- Sunscreen SPF 30+ — prevents sunburn and heat-related illness
- Insect repellent (DEET 10–30% or picaridin) — prevents tick-borne disease
- Prescription medications (if you take any) — keep in original container

**Organization**
- Waterproof zippered bag — keeps contents dry and organized
- Label everything with your name
- Check and replenish twice a year

## Personal Survival Kit Components

Your survival kit addresses the Seven Priorities. It's smaller and more focused than a first-aid kit:

**Shelter**
- Emergency bivy sack or emergency blanket — keeps you warm and visible
- Lightweight tarp (6' x 8') — provides shelter from rain and wind
- Total weight: under 1 pound

**Signaling**
- Signal mirror (2" x 3") — visible to aircraft up to 10 miles away
- Whistle — carries sound up to 1 mile in good conditions
- Bright emergency tape or flagging — marks your location
- Glow sticks — visible at night

**Fire**
- Waterproof matches (in a waterproof container)
- Lighter (butane or zippo-style)
- Ferro rod (ferrocerium rod) — works when wet
- Fire starter (cotton balls with petroleum jelly, char cloth, or commercial tabs) — ignites easily

**Cutting & Tool**
- Multi-tool or knife — for building shelter, cutting rope, and first aid
- Paracord (50+ feet) — for building shelters and creating signals

**Water & Food**
- Water purification tablets or small filter
- High-calorie emergency rations (energy bars, trail mix)
- Collapsible water container

**Navigation**
- Map of your area — know your destination before you go
- Compass — works without batteries
- GPS device (optional) — batteries eventually die, so don't rely solely on this

**Light**
- Headlamp or small flashlight — enables you to see and work after dark
- Glow stick — waterproof and doesn't require batteries

## Demonstrating Your Knowledge

When you show your kits to your merit badge counselor, be ready to explain:

1. **Why each item is there.** Don't just list contents—explain. "This elastic bandage addresses Priority 7 (First Aid) by letting me immobilize a sprained ankle so I don't make it worse."

2. **How to use each item.** Can you apply a blister pad? Remove a tick with tweezers? Assemble a splint? Use a signal mirror? Light a ferro rod in wet conditions? Demonstrate at least a few items.

3. **When you'd use each item.** Under what circumstances would you use the emergency blanket vs. the tarp? When would you use the whistle vs. the signal mirror?

4. **How the kits address the priorities.** Show how your kit covers all seven priorities. If you're missing something, explain why (e.g., "I have a lighter but not matches because I'm more experienced with a lighter").

5. **Your personal adaptations.** If you have specific allergies or health conditions, explain how your kit reflects your needs.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Demonstrating Your Survival Kit to Your Counselor" >}}
Your counselor asks: "Show me how you'd use your signal mirror."

- **Step 1:** Hold the mirror up to the sun or bright light, not looking directly at it.
- **Step 2:** Adjust your hand position until you see a bright reflection on a wall or tree.
- **Step 3:** "This reflection is visible to aircraft up to 10 miles away. If I were lost, I'd angle this reflection toward the sun and sweep it across the sky, looking for search planes."

This demonstrates both knowledge and skill.

## Packing Considerations

Both kits should fit in small pouches and weigh under 5 pounds combined. They're not your entire backpack—they're your **emergency supplies**. Everything should be:
- Waterproof or in waterproof containers
- Lightweight
- Multi-purpose when possible (e.g., duct tape wrapped around a pencil saves weight)
- Regularly checked and replaced if expired

## Where to Get Your Supplies

- **Pharmacy:** First-aid supplies, pain relievers, antihistamines
- **Outdoor retailers:** Survival kits, emergency blankets, signal mirrors, paracord
- **Online:** Pre-assembled survival kits (though assembling your own is better because you'll know every item)
