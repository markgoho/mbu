First Aid Kits. Put together a personal first aid kit and a personal survival kit. Show how items in the kits are used.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

You have already learned what the Scout Essentials are and how they connect to survival priorities (in [Req 1b](/merit-badges/wilderness-survival/guide/req1b/)). Now it is time to actually assemble two separate kits: one focused on treating injuries, and one focused on keeping you alive in an emergency. These are not the same kit, and understanding the difference matters.

## Personal First Aid Kit

Your first aid kit is designed to treat the injuries and conditions you studied in [Req 1a](/merit-badges/wilderness-survival/guide/req1a/). It should handle cuts, blisters, sprains, stings, and minor burns — the things most likely to happen on any outdoor trip.

### What to Include

{{< drg/checklist title="Personal First Aid Kit" subtitle="Items every Scout should carry" >}}
- Adhesive bandages (assorted sizes): For small cuts, scrapes, and blisters.
- Sterile gauze pads (3–4): For larger wounds that need coverage.
- Adhesive tape (1 roll): To secure gauze and splints.
- Elastic bandage (1 roll): For wrapping sprains and strains — the "C" in RICE.
- Moleskin or blister patches: Apply to hot spots before blisters form.
- Antiseptic wipes (6–8): Clean wounds to prevent infection.
- Antibiotic ointment packets (4–6): Apply to cleaned wounds before bandaging.
- Tweezers: For tick removal and splinter extraction.
- Safety pins (4): Secure bandages, improvise slings.
- Nitrile gloves (2 pairs): Protect yourself when treating others.
- Ibuprofen or acetaminophen: For pain and inflammation (carry what your parent approves).
- Antihistamine tablets: For allergic reactions to stings and bites.
- Insect sting relief pads: Reduce pain and itching from stings.
- Small scissors: Cut tape, moleskin, and clothing if needed.
- CPR breathing barrier: Allows safe rescue breathing.
- Emergency information card: Your name, allergies, medications, emergency contact.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/tip >}}
Package your first aid kit in a waterproof bag or container — a ziplock freezer bag works for a basic kit, or use a small dry bag. Wet bandages and soggy antiseptic wipes are useless when you need them most.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Personal Survival Kit

Your survival kit is a separate, compact collection of items designed to keep you alive if you are separated from your main pack or caught in an emergency. Think of it as insurance — it covers the critical priorities (shelter, fire, water, signaling) in the smallest possible package.

### What to Include

{{< drg/checklist title="Personal Survival Kit" subtitle="Emergency essentials that fit in a small tin or pouch" >}}
- Emergency space blanket: Reflects body heat; use as shelter, ground cover, or signal.
- Whistle (pealess): Three blasts = distress; louder and lasts longer than yelling.
- Fire starters (2–3 methods): Waterproof matches, ferro rod, or magnesium block plus cotton balls with petroleum jelly for tinder.
- Paracord (15 feet minimum): Shelter rigging, gear repair, splints, snare material.
- Signal mirror: Effective from miles away in sunlight.
- Water purification tablets (10): Backup when you cannot boil.
- Small flashlight or mini headlamp: Navigate after dark without tripping.
- Duct tape (3–4 feet, wrapped around a pencil): Repairs, blister treatment, splinting.
- Plastic bags (2 large): Carry water, protect gear, improvise rain cover.
- Pencil and paper: Leave notes for rescuers, mark your trail.
- Small compass: Backup navigation.
- Safety wire or fishing line (20 feet): Repairs, improvised snares.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

## How Each Item Gets Used

Your counselor will ask you to demonstrate how to use the items in both kits. Here are the key techniques to practice:

### First Aid Kit Demonstrations

- **Blister treatment:** Cut a donut shape from moleskin (hole in the center), place it around the blister so the moleskin absorbs friction while the blister itself is not compressed.
- **Wound cleaning:** Open an antiseptic wipe and clean outward from the wound in one direction. Apply antibiotic ointment, then cover with an appropriately sized bandage or gauze secured with tape.
- **Sprain wrapping:** Start the elastic bandage below the injured joint and wrap upward in overlapping layers. The wrap should be snug but not tight enough to cut off circulation — you should be able to slide a finger underneath.
- **Tick removal:** Grasp the tick with tweezers as close to the skin as possible. Pull straight up with steady pressure. Do not twist or jerk.

### Survival Kit Demonstrations

- **Fire starting with a ferro rod:** Scrape the rod with the striker to create sparks, directing them onto a nest of tinder (petroleum jelly cotton balls catch easily). Gently blow on the ember to build the flame.
- **Signal mirror:** Hold the mirror near your eye and sight through the aiming hole (if it has one). Tilt the mirror to flash reflected sunlight toward the target — a rescuer, aircraft, or distant point.
- **Space blanket shelter:** Tie paracord between two trees. Drape the space blanket over the cord with the reflective side facing inward (toward you) to reflect your body heat back.
- **Water purification:** Drop a tablet into a liter of water. Wait the time specified on the tablet packaging (usually 30 minutes to 4 hours depending on the type).

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
A signal mirror can be visible from over 10 miles away on a sunny day. During World War II, downed pilots used mirror signals to attract rescue aircraft in the Pacific. A simple piece of reflective material could be the most effective signaling device in your kit.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Kit Maintenance

Assembling your kits is not a one-time task. Before every outing:

- Check expiration dates on medications, ointments, and purification tablets
- Replace any items you used on the last trip
- Test your flashlight batteries
- Make sure your fire-starting materials are dry
- Verify that adhesive bandages and tape have not lost their stickiness
