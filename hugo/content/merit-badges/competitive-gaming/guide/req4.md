{{< drg/requirement number="4" >}}
Gamer Safety and Community Connection. Do the following:
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Gaming is social by design — you're rarely just playing a game, you're playing it with or against other people, often people you've never met. This requirement covers both sides of that: staying safe when you don't know who's on the other end of a match, and turning gaming into a real connection with the people you already know.

## Requirement 4a

{{< drg/requirement number="4a" >}}
Explain the benefits and risks of gaming online.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Online gaming connects you to friends across town or across the country, teaches teamwork under pressure, and for some players, builds genuine community around a shared hobby. It also opens the door to risks that don't exist when you're playing alone: strangers you can't verify, personal information you might share without thinking, and the harassment and toxicity you already looked at in Req 2a.

The goal here isn't to pick a side — it's to hold both truths at once. A game can genuinely improve your teamwork skills *and* expose you to a stranger trying to extract personal information from you. Recognizing both is what makes you a safer, more thoughtful player.

## Requirement 4b

{{< drg/requirement number="4b" >}}
Research and list ten ways to stay safe when playing video games online.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Never share your real name, school, home address, or daily schedule with someone you only know from a game — even if they seem friendly and you've played together many times. People misrepresent who they are online more often in gaming spaces than almost anywhere else, precisely because trust builds quickly over shared matches.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

A few categories to research as you build your list of ten: account security (strong, unique passwords; two-factor authentication), privacy settings (who can see your profile, friend list, or location), communication settings (voice chat controls, block and mute tools), and in-game purchase safety (avoiding scams tied to skins, currency, or "free" items).

## Requirement 4c

{{< drg/requirement number="4c" >}}
Play one of your favorite games with your parent or guardian for at least 30 minutes. Demonstrate how to use privacy and safety settings, explain what you enjoy about the game, and let them play for part of the session.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This is a genuine show-and-tell, not a formality. Pick a game you're actually excited about, and treat your parent or guardian like a real co-player, not a spectator — hand over the controller and let them try it themselves.

{{< drg/checklist title="What to Cover During Your Session" subtitle="Walk your parent or guardian through each of these" >}}
- Where the privacy and safety settings live in the game's menu
- Who can see your profile, message you, or invite you to a match
- How to report or block another player if something goes wrong
- What you personally enjoy about the game — the mechanics, the story, the competition, or the people you play with
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

## Requirement 4d

{{< drg/requirement number="4d" >}}
Play an online or local multiplayer game with another Scout, friend, or community member in a cooperative or team-based setting. The match may be casual or competitive. During play, demonstrate positive communication, teamwork, and sportsmanship. Afterward, review the experience with your counselor and describe how cooperation and communication influenced your team's performance and overall enjoyment of the game.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

In Req 1c, you learned that coaching and communication are part of what makes a match feel like a real competitive event rather than casual play. This requirement puts that idea into practice — even a casual co-op match goes better when your team actually talks to each other.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Pay attention to *how* communication changes the game, not just whether you win. A callout that helps a teammate dodge an attack, or a quick "good move" after a clutch play, are exactly the kind of moments worth bringing up with your counselor afterward.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

Before your session, agree with your teammate on how you'll communicate — voice chat, text chat, or simply talking in the same room if you're playing locally. Afterward, be ready to describe a specific moment where teamwork made a real difference.
