Every competitive game runs on hardware, and that hardware shapes what's possible — how fast you can react, how smooth the game looks, and how much you can customize your setup. This requirement moves from theory (comparing platforms) to hands-on testing (playing across platforms) to design (building your own dream gaming PC on paper).

## Requirement 5a

{{< drg/requirement number="5a" >}}
Explain the difference between console gaming and PC gaming. List the advantages and disadvantages of each.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

**Consoles** — like PlayStation, Xbox, and Nintendo Switch — are built around fixed hardware that every owner shares. **PC gaming** runs on hardware you choose and can upgrade piece by piece.

{{< drg/checklist title="Console vs. PC: Quick Comparison" subtitle="Neither platform is objectively 'better' — it depends what you value" >}}
- **Consoles**: Lower upfront cost, no setup required, every player has identical hardware — but limited upgrade options and often a subscription fee for online play.
- **PC**: Fully customizable and upgradeable, typically better graphics and frame rates at the high end, access to mods — but higher upfront cost and more setup and maintenance responsibility.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

Competitive scenes often reflect this split, too — some esports titles are console-exclusive or console-first, while others (especially in the FPS and RTS genres) are built primarily around PC hardware, where precise mouse aim and high frame rates matter most.

## Requirement 5b

{{< drg/requirement number="5b" >}}
Play the same game on at least two different platforms. Describe the similarities and differences in gameplay and performance. Note: Any game may be used, including free games, on any platform (phone, tablet, PC, laptop, console, etc.).
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Pick a game available on at least two of your devices — a free-to-play title is a great choice here since it removes the cost barrier. As you play, pay attention to more than just "it looks different." Consider:

- **Controls**: Does a touchscreen, controller, or mouse-and-keyboard change how precisely you can play?
- **Performance**: Do you notice differences in frame rate, loading times, or visual detail?
- **Feel**: Does the same game feel faster, slower, or more or less competitive depending on the platform?

{{< drg/tip >}}
If you can, play both versions on the same day, back to back. The differences are much easier to notice when they're fresh in your mind rather than compared from memory a week apart.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Requirement 5c

{{< drg/requirement number="5c" >}}
Visit a PC builder website such as PCPartPicker or Micro Center. Design your ideal gaming PC and create a parts list that includes: central processing unit (CPU), motherboard, random access memory (RAM), hard drive, power supply, graphics card, fans, case. Discuss your selections with your counselor.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Building a PC on paper is one of the best ways to actually understand what each component does — because a builder tool will stop you if you pick parts that don't work together.

{{< drg/checklist title="What Each Part Actually Does" subtitle="Know this before you start picking parts" >}}
- **CPU (processor)**: Handles the game's logic, physics, and overall instructions — the "brain" of the system.
- **Motherboard**: Connects every other component together; must match your CPU's socket type.
- **RAM (memory)**: Temporary, fast storage the system uses while running — more RAM means smoother multitasking.
- **Storage (hard drive/SSD)**: Where your games and files live permanently; solid-state drives (SSDs) load games much faster than traditional hard drives.
- **Power supply (PSU)**: Delivers electricity to every component — must supply enough wattage for your other parts.
- **Graphics card (GPU)**: Renders the images you see on screen — usually the most important part for gaming performance.
- **Case and fans**: House everything and keep it cool — overheating parts slow down or shut off to protect themselves.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}
