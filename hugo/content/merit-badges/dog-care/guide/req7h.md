{{< drg/requirement number="7h" >}}
Briefly discuss the cause and method of spread, the signs and symptoms and the methods of prevention of rabies, parvovirus, distemper, and heartworms in dogs.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

These four diseases are among the most serious threats to dogs. Three of them are prevented by vaccination (as you learned in [Req 6a](/merit-badges/dog-care/guide/req6a/)), and one is prevented by monthly medication (as covered in [Req 6b](/merit-badges/dog-care/guide/req6b/)). Understanding how each disease works helps you appreciate why prevention is so critical.

### Rabies

**Cause:** The rabies virus (*Lyssavirus*), which attacks the central nervous system.

**How it spreads:** Through the saliva of an infected animal, almost always via a bite. The most common carriers in the U.S. are raccoons, bats, skunks, and foxes. An infected animal transmits the virus during the period when it is shedding virus in its saliva, which begins days before symptoms appear.

**Signs and symptoms:**
- **Prodromal stage (2--3 days):** Behavior change -- a friendly dog becomes anxious, or a shy dog becomes unusually affectionate. Fever, licking at the bite wound.
- **Furious stage:** Aggression, restlessness, snapping at imaginary objects, attacking other animals or objects. Disorientation and seizures.
- **Paralytic stage:** Paralysis beginning at the bite wound site and spreading. Drooping jaw, inability to swallow (causing the classic "foaming at the mouth"), eventual respiratory failure and death.

**Prevention:**
- Rabies vaccination is required by law in every U.S. state. First dose at 12--16 weeks, booster at 1 year, then every 1--3 years depending on state law.
- Avoid contact with wild animals, especially those behaving abnormally (a raccoon out during the day, a bat on the ground, a skunk approaching people).

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Rabies is nearly 100% fatal once symptoms appear -- in both dogs and humans. There is no cure. This is why rabies vaccination is the law, not just a suggestion. If your dog is bitten by a wild animal or an unknown dog, contact your veterinarian and local animal control immediately, even if your dog is vaccinated.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Parvovirus (Parvo)

**Cause:** Canine parvovirus type 2 (CPV-2), an extremely hardy virus that attacks rapidly dividing cells in the intestinal lining and bone marrow.

**How it spreads:** Through direct contact with infected dogs or contaminated feces, soil, surfaces, or objects. The virus is extraordinarily resilient -- it can survive in the environment for months to years and resists most household cleaners. Only bleach (1 part bleach to 30 parts water) reliably kills it.

**Signs and symptoms:**
- Severe, bloody diarrhea with a distinctive foul odor
- Profuse vomiting
- Loss of appetite and lethargy
- Rapid dehydration
- High fever followed by dangerously low body temperature
- Symptoms appear 3--7 days after exposure and progress rapidly

**Prevention:**
- Vaccination through the puppy series (DA2PP at 6--8, 10--12, and 14--16 weeks) and adult boosters
- Keep unvaccinated puppies away from unknown dogs and public areas where infected dogs may have been
- Clean contaminated areas with dilute bleach

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Parvovirus is so contagious that it can be carried on shoes, clothing, and hands. A person who walks through an area contaminated with parvo can bring the virus home to an unvaccinated puppy without ever touching an infected dog.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

### Distemper

**Cause:** Canine distemper virus (CDV), a close relative of the human measles virus.

**How it spreads:** Through airborne droplets (coughing, sneezing) and direct contact with an infected dog's saliva, urine, or blood. It can also spread through shared food and water bowls.

**Signs and symptoms:**
- **Early:** Fever, watery discharge from eyes and nose, coughing, lethargy, loss of appetite
- **Progressive:** Vomiting, diarrhea, thickened paw pads and nose ("hard pad disease")
- **Neurological:** Seizures, muscle twitches, paralysis, uncontrolled jaw movements ("chewing gum fits"). Neurological symptoms can appear weeks after initial illness and are often permanent.

**Prevention:**
- Vaccination (included in DA2PP) -- one of the most effective vaccines in veterinary medicine
- Avoid contact with unknown or unvaccinated dogs
- Isolate infected dogs immediately

### Heartworms

**Cause:** *Dirofilaria immitis*, a parasitic roundworm that grows up to 12 inches long and lives in the heart, lungs, and blood vessels.

**How it spreads:** Exclusively through mosquito bites. A mosquito bites an infected animal, picks up microscopic heartworm larvae (microfilariae), and injects them into the next dog it bites. The larvae mature inside the dog over 6--7 months, eventually reaching the heart and lungs.

**Signs and symptoms:**
- **Early (Class 1):** No symptoms or mild cough. This is why annual testing is critical -- by the time symptoms appear, significant damage has occurred.
- **Moderate (Class 2):** Persistent cough, exercise intolerance, fatigue
- **Severe (Class 3):** Difficulty breathing, bulging ribs from fluid in the chest, weight loss, fainting during exercise
- **Caval syndrome (Class 4):** Worms physically block blood flow in the heart. Emergency surgery is the only option, and many dogs do not survive.

**Prevention:**
- Monthly oral or topical heartworm preventive medication, given year-round
- Annual heartworm test (a simple blood draw at the vet)
- Reducing mosquito exposure (eliminate standing water, use pet-safe repellents)

### Summary Table

| Disease | Cause | Spread By | Fatal? | Prevention |
|---------|-------|----------|--------|-----------|
| Rabies | Virus | Bite (saliva) | Nearly always | Vaccination (required by law) |
| Parvovirus | Virus | Feces, contaminated surfaces | Up to 90% if untreated | Vaccination (DA2PP series) |
| Distemper | Virus | Airborne, direct contact | Up to 50%; survivors often have permanent damage | Vaccination (DA2PP series) |
| Heartworms | Parasitic worm | Mosquito bites | Fatal if untreated | Monthly preventive medication |
