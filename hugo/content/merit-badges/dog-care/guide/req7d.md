Show how to put on a simple dressing and bandage the foot, body, or head of your dog.

Bandaging a dog is trickier than bandaging a person for one simple reason: dogs do not understand why something is wrapped around them, and their first instinct is to chew it off. A good bandage must protect the wound, stay in place, and be applied without restricting circulation -- all while your patient wiggles, licks, and tries to remove your work.

### Bandaging Basics

Every bandage has three layers:

1. **Contact layer** -- A non-stick pad (like Telfa) placed directly over the wound. This protects the wound and prevents the bandage from sticking to raw tissue.
2. **Padding layer** -- Roll cotton or cast padding wrapped around the limb to cushion and absorb. This layer should be smooth, with no wrinkles or bunches that could create pressure points.
3. **Outer layer** -- Conforming gauze (like Kling) or self-adhesive wrap (like Vetrap or coflex) to hold everything in place.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
The most common bandaging mistake is wrapping too tightly. A bandage that is too tight cuts off blood circulation, causing swelling below the bandage and potentially killing tissue. After applying any bandage, check the toes or the area below the bandage for swelling, coldness, or discoloration every few hours. If you notice any of these signs, remove the bandage immediately and rewrap more loosely.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Bandaging the Foot (Paw)

Paw injuries are the most common reason you will need to bandage a dog. Cuts from glass, torn pads from hot pavement, and thorn punctures all happen regularly.

1. Clean the wound and apply antibiotic ointment
2. Place a non-stick pad over the wound
3. Wrap roll cotton between and around all the toes, including the dewclaw -- this prevents the toes from rubbing together and creating sores
4. Wrap conforming gauze from the toes upward, overlapping each layer by about half. Extend the bandage above the ankle (pastern) to prevent it from sliding off
5. Apply the outer layer (Vetrap or similar) with gentle tension -- it should conform to the leg without squeezing
6. Tape the top edge of the bandage to the fur with medical tape to prevent the dog from pulling it off
7. Cover the bottom with a waterproof layer (a small plastic bag, surgical glove, or commercial boot) for outdoor trips, then remove the cover indoors to let the bandage breathe

### Bandaging the Body (Torso)

Body bandages require a different approach because there are no limbs to wrap around:

1. Place the non-stick pad and padding over the wound
2. Wrap gauze or Vetrap around the entire torso, passing over the back and under the belly
3. Keep the wrap snug but not tight -- you should be able to slide two fingers under the bandage
4. For chest wounds, the bandage should not restrict breathing. Watch the dog's respiratory rate after applying
5. Use a t-shirt or commercial body suit over the bandage to prevent the dog from reaching it

### Bandaging the Head

Head bandages are the hardest to keep in place because dogs shake their heads constantly:

1. Apply the non-stick pad and padding to the wound
2. Wrap gauze under the chin and over the top of the head, then around the back of the skull
3. Leave the ears free unless the ear itself is injured -- wrapping ears flat against the head causes discomfort and the dog will fight the bandage
4. If the ear is the wound site, fold the ear against the top of the head and bandage over it, securing the wrap around the head
5. The bandage should be firm enough to stay in place during head shaking but loose enough that the dog can open its mouth slightly and breathe normally
6. Use an **Elizabethan collar** (cone) to prevent the dog from pawing the head bandage off

### Keeping Bandages On

Dogs are determined bandage removers. Here are strategies to keep your work intact:

{{< drg/checklist title="Bandage Protection Strategies" subtitle="Choose one or more as needed" >}}
- Elizabethan collar (cone of shame): The most reliable method for preventing chewing
- Bitter apple spray: Applied to the outer bandage layer to discourage licking
- T-shirt or onesie: For body bandages, a snug shirt prevents access
- Sock over paw bandages: An extra barrier that is easy to replace
- Supervision: Watch your dog closely for the first few hours after bandaging
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/tip >}}
Practice bandaging on a calm, healthy dog (or a stuffed animal) before you need to do it in an emergency. Fumbling with gauze rolls while an injured dog squirms is much harder than practicing the technique in a relaxed setting. Your counselor will want to see you demonstrate this skill smoothly.
{{< /drg/tip >}}
