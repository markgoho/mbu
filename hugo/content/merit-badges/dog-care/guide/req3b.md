{{< drg/requirement number="3b" >}}
Explain what "responsible pet ownership" means.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Responsible pet ownership means committing to meet *all* of a dog's needs -- not just the fun parts -- for its entire life. A dog is not a toy you can put away when you get bored. The average dog lives 10 to 13 years, and every one of those years comes with daily obligations. Before you say "I want a dog," you need to honestly ask, "Am I ready for a decade-long commitment?"

### The Five Freedoms

Animal welfare experts use a framework called the **Five Freedoms** to define the minimum standards of care every animal deserves. These were originally developed for farm animals in the United Kingdom but are now applied worldwide to all animals in human care, including dogs:

1. **Freedom from hunger and thirst** -- Provide fresh water at all times and a nutritionally complete diet appropriate to the dog's age, breed, and activity level.
2. **Freedom from discomfort** -- Provide appropriate shelter and a comfortable resting area. A doghouse must be insulated in cold climates; a dog should never be left outside in extreme heat without shade and water.
3. **Freedom from pain, injury, and disease** -- Provide preventive veterinary care (vaccinations, parasite control, dental care) and prompt treatment for illness or injury.
4. **Freedom to express normal behavior** -- Provide sufficient space, exercise, and mental stimulation for the dog to act like a dog -- running, sniffing, playing, and socializing.
5. **Freedom from fear and distress** -- Treat the dog with kindness, avoid punishment-based training, and provide a stable home environment.

{{< drg/tip >}}
When your counselor asks what responsible pet ownership means, use the Five Freedoms as your framework. It shows you understand that ownership goes far beyond providing food and a roof.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### The Financial Commitment

One of the most overlooked aspects of responsible ownership is cost. Dogs are not cheap to care for properly.

| Expense | Annual Estimate |
|---------|----------------|
| Food (quality kibble or raw diet) | $250--$700 |
| Routine veterinary care (exam, vaccines, preventatives) | $200--$600 |
| Grooming (professional, if needed) | $0--$800 |
| Flea, tick, and heartworm prevention | $100--$300 |
| Toys, treats, and supplies | $100--$300 |
| Training classes (group or private) | $100--$500 |
| Emergency veterinary care (average per incident) | $500--$5,000+ |
| Pet insurance (optional but recommended) | $300--$700 |

Over a 12-year lifespan, a dog can cost $15,000 to $50,000 or more. Responsible ownership means being prepared for those costs *before* bringing a dog home.

### Beyond the Basics

Responsible ownership also means:

- **Identification** -- Every dog should wear a collar with an ID tag and be microchipped. A microchip is a tiny device (about the size of a grain of rice) implanted under the skin that contains your contact information. Shelters scan every stray for a microchip -- it is the single best way to reunite a lost dog with its owner.

- **Licensing and registration** -- Most communities require dog licenses. This is not optional -- it is the law. License fees often fund animal control and shelter services.

- **Spaying or neutering** -- Unless you are a responsible breeder with a plan for every puppy, spaying or neutering prevents unwanted litters and reduces the number of dogs euthanized in shelters each year. You will learn more about this in Req 6f.

- **Cleanup** -- Pick up after your dog. Always. Dog waste spreads parasites and bacteria, pollutes waterways, and is the fastest way to make your neighborhood unfriendly to dog owners.

- **Confinement and control** -- Keep your dog on a leash or in a securely fenced area. Even the best-trained dog can bolt after a squirrel, and a loose dog can be hit by a car, bite someone, or get into a fight with another animal.

- **Planning for emergencies** -- Have a plan for who will care for your dog if you are hospitalized, travel, or can no longer keep the dog. Include your dog in your family's disaster preparedness plan.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="What If You Can No Longer Keep Your Dog?" >}}
Life changes -- families move, allergies develop, financial hardships happen. If you truly cannot keep your dog:

- **Contact the breeder or rescue organization first.** Many require you to return the dog to them rather than rehoming independently.
- **Reach out to breed-specific rescue groups.** They specialize in finding appropriate homes.
- **Never abandon a dog or surrender it to a random stranger.** Screen potential adopters carefully.
- **Give the shelter or new owner all veterinary records, behavioral notes, and favorite items.** This helps the dog transition.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Approximately 6.3 million companion animals enter U.S. animal shelters every year, according to the ASPCA. About 3.1 million of those are dogs. Responsible ownership -- including spaying/neutering, microchipping, and lifelong commitment -- directly reduces these numbers.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}
