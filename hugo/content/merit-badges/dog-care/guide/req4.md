{{< drg/requirement number="4" >}}
For two months, keep and care for your dog. Maintain a log of your activities during this period that includes these items: feeding schedule, types of food used, amount fed, exercise periods, training schedule, a weekly body weight record, grooming and bathing schedules, veterinary care, if necessary, and costs. Also include a brief description of the type of housing/shelter arrangements you have for your dog.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This is the heart of the Dog Care merit badge -- two full months of hands-on, daily care. Your counselor is not looking for a perfect dog at the end. They want to see that *you* showed up consistently, tracked what you did, and learned from the experience. The log is your proof.

### Setting Up Your Log

Your care log needs to capture specific information every day for eight weeks. Here is what to track:

**Daily entries:**
- Date and time of each feeding, including what food you used and how much
- Exercise type and duration (walks, fetch, dog park, swimming)
- Training sessions and what you worked on
- Any grooming done (brushing, nail check, ear cleaning)

**Weekly entries:**
- Body weight (weigh your dog the same day each week, at the same time of day for consistency)
- Notes on overall health and behavior changes

**As needed:**
- Veterinary visits (reason, diagnosis, treatment, cost)
- Bathing (date, products used)
- Any unusual events (illness, injury, behavioral changes)

**One-time (beginning of log):**
- Description of your dog's housing and shelter arrangements

### Feeding Your Dog

Feeding is more than filling a bowl. The right food, the right amount, and the right schedule depend on your dog's age, size, breed, and activity level.

**Types of dog food:**
- **Dry kibble** -- The most common and economical option. Look for a brand that meets AAFCO (Association of American Feed Control Officials) nutritional standards. The first ingredient should be a named protein source (chicken, beef, salmon), not a filler like corn or soy.
- **Wet/canned food** -- Higher in moisture and often more palatable, but more expensive and spoils faster once opened. Can be mixed with kibble.
- **Raw diets** -- Some owners feed raw meat, bones, and vegetables. This approach is controversial -- the AVMA advises against it due to bacterial contamination risks. If your family uses a raw diet, discuss safety protocols with your vet.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Never feed a dog chocolate, grapes, raisins, onions, garlic, xylitol (a sweetener found in sugar-free gum), macadamia nuts, or cooked bones. These are toxic to dogs and can cause serious illness or death. Keep all human food out of your dog's reach.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

**How much to feed:**
- Follow the feeding guidelines on the food package as a starting point, then adjust based on your dog's body condition
- Your vet can show you how to assess body condition -- you should be able to feel (but not see) the ribs, and the dog should have a visible waist when viewed from above
- Puppies eat 3--4 times per day; adults typically eat twice per day

### Exercise

Every dog needs daily exercise, but the type and amount depend on breed, age, and health:

- **Puppies** -- Short, frequent play sessions. Avoid long runs or jumps that stress growing joints. A general rule is 5 minutes of exercise per month of age, twice a day.
- **Adult dogs** -- Most need 30--60 minutes of exercise daily. High-energy breeds (sporting, herding) may need 60--120 minutes.
- **Senior dogs** -- Shorter, gentler walks. Swimming is excellent for older dogs with joint issues.

{{< drg/checklist title="Exercise Ideas" subtitle="Mix it up to keep things interesting" >}}
- Leash walks through the neighborhood
- Off-leash play in a securely fenced area
- Fetch with a ball or frisbee
- Swimming (if your dog enjoys water)
- Hiking on dog-friendly trails
- Agility courses (even homemade ones in the backyard)
- Puzzle toys and treat-dispensing balls for mental exercise

### Weighing Your Dog

Weekly weigh-ins help you spot trends. A sudden weight loss could signal illness; gradual weight gain means you may be overfeeding or underexercising.

- **Small dogs** -- Stand on a bathroom scale while holding your dog, then weigh yourself alone. The difference is your dog's weight.
- **Large dogs** -- Many veterinary clinics and pet stores have walk-on scales you can use for free.
- **Record every weight** in your log on the same day each week.

### Housing and Shelter

Your log should include a description of where your dog lives. This includes:

- **Indoor vs. outdoor** -- Most dogs should live primarily indoors with their family. Dogs are social animals and suffer when isolated in a yard.
- **Sleeping area** -- Describe the dog's bed, crate, or sleeping spot. A crate should be large enough for the dog to stand, turn around, and lie down comfortably.
- **Outdoor access** -- Describe the yard, fence type and condition, and any shelter available outside (doghouse, shade structure).
- **Safety** -- Note any hazards you have addressed: toxic plants removed, chemicals stored out of reach, pool fenced off.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Start your log on the first day and make entries a daily habit -- do not try to reconstruct two months of data from memory the night before your counselor meeting. Set a daily reminder on your phone or keep your log next to the dog food as a visual cue.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Tracking Costs

Keep a running total of every expense during the two-month period:

- Food (brand, size, price)
- Treats
- Veterinary visits (exam fee, vaccinations, medications)
- Grooming supplies or professional grooming fees
- Toys and equipment
- Licensing or registration fees

This cost tracking connects directly to what you learned about responsible ownership in Req 3b -- caring for a dog is a real financial commitment.
