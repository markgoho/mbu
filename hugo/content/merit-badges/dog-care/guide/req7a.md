{{< drg/requirement number="7a" >}}
Explain precautions to take in handling a hurt dog.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A gentle, friendly dog that has never shown a moment of aggression in its life can -- and will -- bite if it is in severe pain. This is not a character flaw. It is a survival instinct. When you approach an injured dog, you are approaching an animal that is scared, confused, and hurting. Your safety comes first, and the way you handle the dog determines whether you help or make things worse.

### Golden Rule: An Injured Dog May Bite

Even your own dog, the one that sleeps on your bed and licks your face, may snap or bite if you touch a broken leg or press on an internal injury. Pain overrides training. Accept this reality before you approach any injured animal.

### Precautions Before You Touch the Dog

{{< drg/checklist title="Before Approaching an Injured Dog" subtitle="Complete these checks first" >}}
- Ensure the scene is safe: Is the dog near traffic? Is there a danger to you (aggressive dogs nearby, broken glass, downed power lines)?
- Observe from a distance: Is the dog conscious? Breathing? Bleeding heavily? Can it stand?
- Approach slowly and calmly: Move toward the dog from the side, not head-on. A direct approach can be perceived as a threat.
- Speak softly: Use a low, calm voice. Say the dog's name if you know it. Avoid sudden movements or loud noises.
- Let the dog see and sniff you: Extend the back of your hand slowly. Watch for warning signs -- growling, lip curling, flattened ears, whites of the eyes showing.
- Do not stare directly into the dog's eyes: In dog body language, direct eye contact is a challenge or threat.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

### Warning Signs to Back Away

If the injured dog shows any of these signs, do not attempt to handle it without additional help or a muzzle:

- Growling, snarling, or baring teeth
- Snapping at the air
- Stiff body with hackles raised
- Trying to crawl away from you
- Pupils dilated, whites of eyes visible ("whale eye")

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Your safety always comes first. If a dog is too aggressive to handle safely, call animal control or a veterinarian for help. A seriously injured dog may need to be sedated by a professional before it can be moved. Do not risk a severe bite that requires your own emergency room visit.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### How to Handle an Injured Dog

Once you have determined the dog can be safely approached:

- **Support the injury.** If a leg appears broken, do not try to splint it. Simply support the limb and prevent the dog from putting weight on it.
- **Move the dog on a flat surface.** Use a board, a blanket, or a large towel as a stretcher. Slide it under the dog gently. For small dogs, a box or laundry basket can work.
- **Minimize movement.** Keep the dog as still as possible, especially if you suspect a spinal injury (the dog cannot move its legs or has been hit by a car).
- **Keep the dog warm.** Cover the dog with a blanket or jacket. Shock causes rapid heat loss.
- **Control bleeding.** Apply direct pressure with a clean cloth to any wounds that are bleeding. Do not remove the cloth -- if it soaks through, add another layer on top.
- **Transport carefully.** Drive to the nearest emergency veterinary clinic. Have someone else drive while you stabilize the dog if possible.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Your Dog Is Hit While You Are on a Hike" >}}
You are on a trail and your dog steps on broken glass, cutting a paw badly. Here is what to do:

- **Stay calm.** Your dog reads your energy. If you panic, the dog panics.
- **Sit down next to the dog** and let it settle for a moment.
- **Apply pressure to the cut** with a clean cloth, bandana, or piece of clothing.
- **Wrap the paw** with whatever you have -- a spare sock, a bandana, even duct tape (over the cloth, never directly on fur).
- **Carry a small dog** or support a large dog as you walk back to the trailhead.
- **Go to the vet** as soon as possible to check for glass fragments and assess whether stitches are needed.
