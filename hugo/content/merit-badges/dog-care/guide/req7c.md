{{< drg/requirement number="7c" >}}
Explain how to treat wounds. Explain first aid for a dog bite.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This requirement covers two related skills: treating wounds *on* a dog and providing first aid to a *person* who has been bitten by a dog. Both are situations you may encounter as a dog owner, and knowing what to do in the first few minutes can prevent infection, reduce scarring, and even save a life.

### Treating Wounds on a Dog

Dogs get cuts, scrapes, and puncture wounds from running through brush, stepping on glass, playing too rough, or tangling with wildlife. Most minor wounds can be cleaned and monitored at home, but deeper wounds need veterinary care.

**For minor cuts and scrapes:**

1. **Restrain the dog gently.** If the dog is anxious or in pain, apply an emergency muzzle (as you learned in [Req 7b](/merit-badges/dog-care/guide/req7b/)).
2. **Stop the bleeding.** Apply direct pressure with a clean cloth or gauze for 5--10 minutes. Do not lift the cloth to check -- this breaks the clot.
3. **Clean the wound.** Flush with clean warm water or saline solution. Do not use hydrogen peroxide or rubbing alcohol -- both damage healthy tissue and slow healing. A dilute chlorhexidine solution (available at pet stores) is an excellent wound cleanser.
4. **Trim surrounding fur.** Carefully clip hair away from the wound edges to prevent it from sticking to the wound and introducing bacteria.
5. **Apply antibiotic ointment.** A thin layer of veterinary or triple antibiotic ointment helps prevent infection.
6. **Bandage if needed.** Cover the wound loosely with a non-stick pad and wrap with gauze. You will practice bandaging in the next requirement.
7. **Monitor for infection.** Check the wound daily for increasing redness, swelling, heat, discharge, or foul odor. If any of these appear, see your vet.

**See the vet immediately for:**
- Deep wounds that expose muscle, tendon, or bone
- Wounds that will not stop bleeding after 10 minutes of direct pressure
- Puncture wounds (especially from animal bites -- they seal over and trap bacteria inside)
- Wounds near the eyes, ears, or genitals
- Any wound caused by a wild animal

Keep a pet-specific first-aid kit accessible at home and when you travel with your dog. You will learn exactly what goes in that kit in [Req 7f](/merit-badges/dog-care/guide/req7f/).

### First Aid for a Dog Bite (on a Person)

Approximately 4.5 million dog bites occur in the United States each year. About 800,000 of those require medical attention. Knowing first aid for a bite helps you respond effectively whether you or someone else is bitten.

**Immediate first aid:**

1. **Move away from the dog** to prevent further bites. Do not attempt to restrain the dog unless it poses an ongoing threat to others.
2. **Assess the wound.** Dog bites range from minor scratches to deep punctures and tears.
3. **Control bleeding.** Apply direct pressure with a clean cloth.
4. **Wash the wound thoroughly.** Use soap and warm running water for at least 5 minutes. This is one of the most important steps -- thorough washing significantly reduces infection risk.
5. **Apply antibiotic ointment** and cover with a sterile bandage.
6. **Seek medical attention.** All but the most superficial bites should be evaluated by a doctor. Dog bites carry a high infection risk (bacteria like *Pasteurella*, *Staphylococcus*, and *Streptococcus* are common in dog saliva).

### What to Report After a Dog Bite

{{< drg/checklist title="Dog Bite Reporting" subtitle="Gather this information after any bite" >}}
- Identity of the dog's owner (if known)
- Dog's vaccination status (especially rabies)
- Description of the dog (breed, size, color, collar, tags)
- Circumstances of the bite (provoked or unprovoked)
- Location and time of the incident
- Contact local animal control to file a report
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

**Rabies concerns:**
- If the dog's rabies vaccination status is unknown, the victim may need post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) -- a series of rabies vaccines given over two weeks. PEP is 100% effective when administered promptly.
- If the dog is owned and up-to-date on rabies vaccinations, the dog will typically be quarantined for 10 days to confirm it does not develop rabies symptoms.
- If the bite was from a wild or stray animal, contact animal control immediately. Rabies is nearly 100% fatal once symptoms appear, so this is urgent.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Any bite from an unknown or unvaccinated animal, or any bite from a wild animal (raccoon, bat, skunk, fox), requires immediate medical evaluation for rabies risk. Do not wait to see if symptoms develop -- by the time symptoms appear, it is too late for treatment.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}
