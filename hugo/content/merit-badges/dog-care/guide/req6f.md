{{< drg/requirement number="6f" >}}
Discuss with your counselor the considerations and advantages of spaying or neutering your dog.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Spaying and neutering are among the most common surgical procedures performed on dogs. **Spaying** is the surgical removal of a female dog's ovaries and uterus. **Neutering** (also called castration) is the surgical removal of a male dog's testicles. Both procedures are performed under general anesthesia by a veterinarian and are considered routine -- most dogs go home the same day and recover within 10--14 days.

### Advantages of Spaying (Females)

**Health benefits:**
- **Eliminates the risk of pyometra** -- a life-threatening uterine infection that affects roughly 25% of unspayed females by age 10. Emergency surgery for pyometra costs $1,000--$5,000 and is not always successful.
- **Greatly reduces the risk of mammary cancer** -- If spayed before the first heat cycle, the risk drops to less than 0.5%. After the second heat, the protective effect diminishes significantly.
- **No unwanted pregnancies** -- A single litter can produce 4--12 puppies. Finding responsible homes for every puppy is a serious commitment, and unwanted litters contribute to shelter overpopulation.

**Behavioral benefits:**
- Eliminates heat cycles (which occur roughly every 6 months and last 2--3 weeks), including bleeding, restlessness, and attraction of male dogs from the neighborhood
- Reduces roaming behavior -- unspayed females in heat will try to escape to find a mate

### Advantages of Neutering (Males)

**Health benefits:**
- **Eliminates the risk of testicular cancer** -- one of the most common cancers in intact male dogs
- **Reduces the risk of prostate problems** -- Benign prostatic hyperplasia (enlarged prostate) affects the majority of intact males over age 5

**Behavioral benefits:**
- Reduces urine marking (territorial spraying) inside the home
- Reduces roaming behavior -- intact males can detect a female in heat from miles away and will go to extreme lengths to reach her, including jumping fences and running into traffic
- May reduce inter-dog aggression, though this varies by individual and breed
- Does not change the dog's fundamental personality -- a playful dog stays playful, a protective dog stays protective

### Considerations and Timing

The right age to spay or neuter is not one-size-fits-all. Veterinary research continues to evolve on this topic:

- **Traditional recommendation:** Spay/neuter at 6 months of age
- **Current research:** For large and giant breeds, some studies suggest waiting until the dog is fully grown (12--24 months) may reduce the risk of certain joint disorders and cancers. The growth plates in larger breeds close later, and early removal of sex hormones may affect bone development.
- **Talk to your vet** about the best timing for your specific dog's breed, size, and lifestyle

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
A single unspayed female dog and her offspring, if none are spayed or neutered, can theoretically produce over 67,000 puppies in just six years. This exponential math is why spaying and neutering is considered the most effective tool for reducing shelter overpopulation.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

### The Population Problem

The connection between spaying/neutering and animal welfare is direct:

- Approximately 3.1 million dogs enter U.S. shelters every year
- About 390,000 shelter dogs are euthanized annually -- many are healthy and adoptable, but there simply are not enough homes
- Every litter of puppies, whether planned or accidental, contributes to this problem unless every puppy is placed in a committed, permanent home

### Cost

- Spaying typically costs $200--$500 at a private veterinarian
- Neutering typically costs $150--$400
- Low-cost spay/neuter clinics and community programs often reduce the cost to $50--$150
- Many shelters and rescue organizations spay/neuter dogs before adoption, with the cost included in the adoption fee
