{{< drg/requirement number="6c" >}}
Explain the importance of dental care and tooth brushing to your pet's health.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

By age three, over 80% of dogs have some form of dental disease. That is not a typo -- the vast majority of adult dogs are walking around with gum infections, tartar buildup, or loose teeth that cause chronic pain. Dogs rarely show obvious signs of dental pain because instinct tells them to hide weakness. Your dog could be suffering right now and you would never know unless you looked in its mouth.

### Why Dental Health Matters

Dental disease in dogs is not just about bad breath. Bacteria from infected gums enter the bloodstream and can damage the heart, liver, and kidneys. Studies have shown that dogs with severe periodontal disease live an average of 1.3 to 3 years less than dogs with healthy mouths. Dental care is not cosmetic -- it is life-extending.

**The progression of dental disease:**

1. **Plaque** forms on teeth within hours of eating -- a sticky film of bacteria
2. Plaque hardens into **tartar** (calculus) within 24--48 hours if not removed
3. Tartar pushes under the gumline, causing **gingivitis** (red, swollen, bleeding gums)
4. Untreated gingivitis progresses to **periodontitis** -- infection destroys the bone and tissue holding teeth in place
5. Teeth loosen and fall out; bacteria enter the bloodstream

### How to Brush Your Dog's Teeth

Brushing your dog's teeth is the single most effective way to prevent dental disease. Aim for daily brushing, but even three times per week provides significant benefit.

**What you need:**
- A soft-bristled toothbrush designed for dogs (or a finger brush for smaller dogs)
- Dog-specific toothpaste -- **never use human toothpaste**, which contains fluoride and foaming agents that are toxic to dogs if swallowed
- Dog toothpastes come in flavors like chicken, beef, and peanut butter to make the experience more appealing

**Step-by-step:**

1. **Start slow.** Let your dog taste the toothpaste from your finger for a few days before introducing the brush.
2. **Lift the lip.** Gently lift one side of the lip to expose the teeth and gums.
3. **Brush in small circles.** Focus on the outside surfaces of the teeth (where plaque accumulates most) and along the gumline.
4. **Work in sections.** You do not need to do the entire mouth in one session at first. Start with the front teeth and gradually work toward the back molars over several sessions.
5. **Keep it positive.** Talk in a calm, cheerful voice. End with a treat or play. If your dog struggles, stop and try again later -- forcing the issue creates a negative association that makes future brushing harder.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Start brushing when your dog is young. Puppies who get used to having their mouths handled accept tooth brushing easily as adults. If you are starting with an adult dog, build up gradually over weeks -- one tooth at a time if needed.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Other Dental Care Methods

While brushing is best, these supplementary methods help:

- **Dental chews** -- Products like veterinary-approved dental chews help scrape plaque off teeth. Look for the VOHC (Veterinary Oral Health Council) seal of acceptance, which means the product has been proven effective in clinical trials.
- **Dental diets** -- Specially formulated kibble with larger pieces that require more chewing and mechanically clean teeth.
- **Water additives** -- Antimicrobial solutions added to drinking water that reduce bacterial growth. These are supplements, not replacements for brushing.
- **Professional dental cleaning** -- Your vet should examine your dog's teeth at every annual checkup. When tartar buildup is significant, a professional cleaning under anesthesia is the only way to clean below the gumline where the real damage occurs. Professional cleanings typically cost $200--$700.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Dogs have 42 adult teeth -- 10 more than humans. Puppies start with 28 baby teeth that begin falling out around 4 months of age. If you find tiny teeth on the floor, that is perfectly normal.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

### Signs of Dental Problems

Watch for these warning signs that your dog may need veterinary dental care:

- Bad breath that goes beyond normal "dog breath"
- Red, swollen, or bleeding gums
- Yellow or brown tartar buildup on teeth
- Difficulty eating or dropping food
- Pawing at the mouth
- Drooling more than usual
- Loose or missing teeth
- Reluctance to have the head or mouth touched

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
If your dog suddenly stops eating, drools excessively, or shows pain when you touch its face, see your veterinarian promptly. These could be signs of a broken tooth, abscess, or advanced dental disease that needs professional treatment.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}
