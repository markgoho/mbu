{{< drg/requirement number="6e" >}}
Discuss with your counselor any seasonal conditions (like hot summers, cold winters, or extreme humidity) where you live that need to be considered for your dog.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Your dog cannot tell you it is too hot, too cold, or struggling with the humidity. It relies on you to recognize the signs and take action. The seasonal challenges you face depend entirely on where you live, so this requirement asks you to think specifically about *your* climate -- not a textbook climate.

### Hot Weather Hazards

Dogs do not sweat through their skin the way humans do. Their only real cooling mechanisms are panting and limited sweat glands in their paw pads. This makes them far more vulnerable to overheating than you might expect.

**Heatstroke** is the most serious hot-weather risk. It happens when a dog's body temperature rises above 104 degrees Fahrenheit (40 degrees Celsius) and can no longer cool itself. Heatstroke can cause organ failure and death within minutes.

**High-risk dogs:**
- Brachycephalic breeds (flat-faced dogs like Bulldogs, Pugs, Boston Terriers) -- their shortened airways make panting less efficient
- Thick-coated breeds (Huskies, Malamutes, Bernese Mountain Dogs)
- Overweight dogs
- Senior dogs and puppies
- Dogs with heart or respiratory conditions

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
**Never leave a dog in a parked car.** On an 85-degree day, the inside of a car reaches 120 degrees in 30 minutes -- even with the windows cracked. Dogs die in hot cars every year. If you see a dog in distress in a parked car, call 911 immediately. Many states have laws protecting people who break a car window to rescue an animal.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

**Hot weather precautions:**
- Walk your dog in the early morning or evening, not during the heat of the day
- Test pavement with the back of your hand -- if you cannot hold it on the surface for 5 seconds, it is too hot for paw pads. Asphalt can reach 150+ degrees on a 95-degree day
- Provide constant access to fresh, cool water
- Offer shade whenever the dog is outdoors
- Consider a cooling vest, cooling mat, or kiddie pool for extreme heat days
- Never shave a double-coated breed -- the undercoat actually insulates against heat as well as cold. Shaving can cause sunburn and permanent coat damage

**Signs of heatstroke:**
- Excessive panting, thick drooling
- Bright red tongue and gums
- Staggering, disorientation
- Vomiting or diarrhea
- Collapse

If you suspect heatstroke, move the dog to shade or air conditioning, apply cool (not ice-cold) water to the body, and get to a veterinarian immediately.

### Cold Weather Hazards

**Hypothermia** occurs when a dog's body temperature drops below normal (below 99 degrees Fahrenheit). Frostbite typically affects the ears, tail, and paw pads first.

**High-risk dogs:**
- Small breeds and toy breeds with little body mass
- Short-coated or single-coated breeds (Greyhounds, Chihuahuas, Whippets)
- Senior dogs and puppies
- Lean, low-body-fat breeds

**Cold weather precautions:**
- Limit outdoor time in temperatures below 20 degrees Fahrenheit for small and short-coated breeds
- Use a dog coat or sweater for breeds that need it -- this is not just fashion; it is functional warmth
- Protect paws from ice, snow, salt, and chemical deicers with booties or paw wax. Deicing chemicals can burn paw pads and are toxic if licked
- Wipe paws and belly after winter walks to remove salt and chemicals
- Provide a warm, draft-free sleeping area indoors
- Keep antifreeze locked away -- it has a sweet taste that attracts dogs but is extremely toxic, even in small amounts

### Humidity

High humidity reduces the effectiveness of panting because moisture-laden air does not evaporate saliva from the tongue as efficiently. In the Southeast and Gulf Coast states, a combination of heat and humidity creates dangerous conditions even at temperatures that would be safe in drier climates.

- Use the "heat index" rather than the air temperature alone to gauge risk
- Reduce exercise intensity on humid days
- Watch for signs of overheating at lower temperatures than you would in dry climates

### Regional Hazards Beyond Temperature

Depending on where you live, other seasonal concerns may include:

- **Wildfire smoke** (Western states) -- Smoke irritates dogs' respiratory systems just like it does yours. Keep walks short and stay indoors on poor air quality days
- **Thunderstorms** -- Many dogs have noise phobia. Provide a safe, enclosed space (a crate covered with a blanket, a bathroom) during storms
- **Seasonal allergies** -- Dogs get allergies too. Spring and fall pollen can cause itchy skin, sneezing, and ear infections

{{< drg/tip >}}
For your counselor discussion, research the specific seasonal hazards in *your* area. If you live in Arizona, talk about extreme summer heat. If you live in Minnesota, focus on winter cold. Your counselor wants to know you understand what *your* dog faces, not just general facts.
{{< /drg/tip >}}
