{{< drg/requirement number="7e" >}}
Explain what to do if a dog is hit by a car.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A dog hit by a car can have injuries you cannot see -- ruptured organs, internal bleeding, a cracked pelvis, spinal damage. Even a dog that gets up and walks after being struck may have life-threatening internal injuries that do not show symptoms for hours. Every car-strike victim needs emergency veterinary care, no matter how "fine" the dog appears.

### Immediate Response

{{< drg/be-prepared title="A Dog Is Hit by a Car" >}}
Follow these steps in order:

1. **Protect yourself first.** Do not run into traffic. Make sure the road is safe before approaching.
2. **Alert the driver.** If the driver stopped, ask them to turn on hazard lights to warn other vehicles. If you are alone, use your phone's flashlight or wave a bright-colored item.
3. **Approach the dog carefully.** An injured dog is likely in shock and may bite defensively. Use the precautions from [Req 7a](/merit-badges/dog-care/guide/req7a/) -- move slowly, speak softly, approach from the side.
4. **Apply a muzzle if safe to do so.** Use the emergency muzzle technique from [Req 7b](/merit-badges/dog-care/guide/req7b/) unless the dog is vomiting, unconscious, or having difficulty breathing.
5. **Do not move the dog unless it is in immediate danger** (in the road with traffic). If you must move the dog, slide a flat surface (blanket, board, car floor mat) under the body and carry it as level as possible to avoid aggravating spinal injuries.
6. **Control visible bleeding** with direct pressure using a clean cloth.
7. **Keep the dog warm.** Cover with a blanket or jacket. Shock causes rapid heat loss.
8. **Transport to an emergency vet immediately.** Keep the dog as still as possible during the ride. If available, lay the dog on a blanket on the back seat with someone stabilizing it.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

### Understanding Shock

Shock is the body's response to catastrophic injury -- blood pressure drops, organs do not get enough oxygen, and the body begins shutting down. A dog in shock may show:

- Pale or white gums (press on the gum with your finger -- healthy gums return to pink within 2 seconds; slow return indicates shock)
- Rapid, shallow breathing
- Weak, rapid pulse
- Cold ears and paws
- Glazed, unfocused eyes
- Weakness or collapse

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Shock kills. If a dog shows signs of shock, keep it warm, keep it still, and get to a veterinarian as fast as possible. Do not try to give food or water to a dog in shock -- the body cannot process it, and the dog may aspirate if it vomits.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Internal vs. External Injuries

External injuries (cuts, road rash, broken bones) are visible and distressing, but internal injuries are often more dangerous because they are invisible:

| External (Visible) | Internal (Hidden) |
|---|---|
| Lacerations, abrasions | Ruptured spleen, liver, or bladder |
| Broken limbs | Diaphragmatic hernia (organs pushed into chest cavity) |
| Road rash | Pulmonary contusion (bruised lungs) |
| Tooth damage | Pelvic fractures |
| Eye injuries | Internal bleeding |

This is why **every** dog hit by a car must see a vet, even if it walks away seemingly unharmed. A ruptured bladder or slow internal bleed can kill hours later.

### Prevention

The best treatment for a car strike is preventing it from happening:

- Always keep your dog on a leash near roads
- Ensure your yard fence has no gaps, holes, or sections where the dog can dig under
- Train a reliable recall (come command) -- but never trust it near traffic. Even well-trained dogs can bolt after a squirrel
- Use reflective gear (collar, leash, vest) when walking at dawn, dusk, or night
- Never let your dog ride untethered in the bed of a pickup truck

Program your emergency vet's phone number into your phone right now. In an emergency, you do not want to waste time searching. Most communities have 24-hour emergency veterinary hospitals -- know where yours is before you need it.
