{{< drg/requirement number="3c" >}}
Explain what issues (including temperament) must be considered when deciding on what breed of dog to get as a family pet.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Picking a dog because it looks cute in a photo is like choosing a college based on its logo. The breed that *looks* perfect may be completely wrong for your family's lifestyle, living situation, and experience level. Getting this decision right prevents heartbreak for both the family and the dog.

### Temperament

Temperament is the dog's overall personality and behavioral tendencies -- how it typically reacts to people, other animals, noise, and new situations. It is the single most important factor in choosing a family pet.

- **Energy level** -- A Border Collie needs 2+ hours of vigorous exercise daily. A Basset Hound is content with a leisurely walk. If your family is active and outdoorsy, a high-energy breed will be a great match. If you prefer quieter evenings at home, a lower-energy breed will be a better fit.
- **Tolerance for children** -- Some breeds (Labrador Retriever, Golden Retriever, Beagle) are famously patient with kids. Others (some terriers, herding breeds that nip heels) may not tolerate the unpredictable behavior of young children.
- **Sociability with other pets** -- If you have cats, small animals, or other dogs, some breeds coexist beautifully while others have strong prey drives that make multi-pet households challenging.
- **Reactivity** -- How does the breed typically respond to strangers, loud noises, or unfamiliar situations? A naturally calm, confident dog is easier for most families than a breed prone to anxiety or territorial aggression.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Meet the breed before you commit. Visit dog shows, breed-specific rescue events, or reputable breeders and spend time with adult dogs -- not just adorable puppies. Adult dogs show you the temperament you will live with for the next decade.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Living Space

Your home environment dramatically limits -- or expands -- your breed options:

- **Apartment or small home** -- Toy breeds, some smaller hounds, and calm medium breeds can do well. Avoid large, high-energy breeds that need room to run.
- **Suburban home with a fenced yard** -- Most breeds can thrive here with proper exercise. The yard is a bonus, not a replacement for walks and interaction.
- **Rural property** -- Working, sporting, and herding breeds often do best with space to roam. But even rural dogs need containment to prevent roaming, chasing livestock, or encountering wildlife.

### Climate

Your local weather matters more than many families realize:

- **Hot climates** -- Brachycephalic breeds (flat-faced dogs like Bulldogs, Pugs, and Pekingese) overheat easily and can die from heatstroke. Thick-coated breeds like Huskies suffer in sustained heat. Short-coated, lean breeds tend to handle heat better.
- **Cold climates** -- Thin-coated breeds like Greyhounds and Chihuahuas need coats and booties in winter. Double-coated breeds like Huskies and Malamutes are built for cold.

### Allergies

No dog is truly "hypoallergenic," but some breeds produce less dander and shed minimally, which helps allergy sufferers. Poodles, Portuguese Water Dogs, Bichon Frises, and Schnauzers are commonly recommended for families with allergies. Before committing, spend time around the breed to test for reactions.

### Grooming Requirements

Grooming needs range from almost nothing to a part-time job:

| Coat Type | Grooming Level | Example Breeds |
|-----------|---------------|----------------|
| Short, smooth | Low -- weekly brushing, occasional bath | Beagle, Boxer, Dalmatian |
| Double coat | Moderate -- weekly brushing, heavy seasonal shedding | Labrador, Husky, German Shepherd |
| Long, silky | High -- daily brushing to prevent mats | Afghan Hound, Yorkshire Terrier |
| Wiry | Moderate-High -- hand-stripping or professional grooming | Airedale, Wire Fox Terrier |
| Curly/non-shedding | High -- professional grooming every 4--6 weeks | Poodle, Bichon Frise |

### Experience Level

Some breeds are forgiving of beginner mistakes; others require experienced handlers:

- **First-time owners** -- Consider breeds known for trainability and forgiving temperaments: Labrador Retriever, Golden Retriever, Cavalier King Charles Spaniel, Poodle
- **Experienced owners** -- More challenging breeds like Akitas, Belgian Malinois, or Cane Corsos reward skilled handling but can overwhelm beginners

### Lifespan and Health Issues

- **Smaller breeds** tend to live longer (12--16 years) than larger breeds (8--12 years). Giant breeds like Great Danes may only live 7--10 years.
- **Breed-specific health issues** can mean significant veterinary costs. Bulldogs frequently need surgery for breathing problems. German Shepherds are prone to hip dysplasia. Research the common health issues for any breed you are considering.

### Where to Get Your Dog

{{< drg/checklist title="Ethical Sourcing Checklist" subtitle="Evaluate where you get your dog" >}}
- Reputable breeders: Health-test their dogs, welcome visits, provide contracts, take dogs back if needed
- Breed-specific rescues: Specialize in one breed and screen adopters carefully
- Animal shelters: Great source for mixed breeds and purebreds alike
- Avoid puppy mills: Large-scale breeding operations prioritizing profit over animal welfare. Signs include: selling through pet stores, no health testing, no facility visits allowed, always having puppies available
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/be-prepared title="The Whole Family Must Agree" >}}
A dog affects everyone in the household. Before choosing a breed, make sure the whole family discusses:

- **Who will do the daily care?** Feeding, walking, grooming, and training are daily commitments.
- **Is anyone allergic?** Spend time with the breed first.
- **What happens during vacations?** You need a boarding plan or a trusted pet sitter.
- **Can the family afford it?** Review the cost table from Req 3b honestly.
- **What if a family member is afraid of dogs?** This must be addressed before bringing a dog home.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}
