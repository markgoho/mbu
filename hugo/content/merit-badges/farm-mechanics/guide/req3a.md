{{< drg/requirement number="3a" >}}
Replace the handle of any tool found on the farm.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A tool with a broken or split handle is unsafe and frustrating. The good news: replacing a handle is a straightforward task that teaches you about tool construction and restoration. You will disassemble a tool, fit a new handle, and secure it so it lasts for years.

### Selecting Your Tool

Choose a tool with a clearly damaged handle:

- **Hammer** with a cracked wooden handle (most common)
- **Axe** or **hatchet** with a splintered handle
- **Shovel** or **spade** with a bent or broken shaft
- **Hoe** or **rake** with a loose or split handle
- **Screwdriver** or **chisel** with a broken grip

Avoid tools where the handle damage is the least of the problems (if the hammer head is also rusted, this might not be the best first project). Start with a tool where the handle is clearly the issue.

### Understanding Tool Handle Attachment

Different tools attach handles differently:

**Wedged and Pinned** (Hammers, Axes, Hatchets)

The handle is driven through a hole in the head. A wooden or metal wedge is then driven into a slot in the handle, expanding it to lock it tightly in place. Some tools also have a metal pin through the head and handle for additional security.

**Threaded Socket** (Many Hand Tools)

The handle screws into the tool head (e.g., a file handle or a brush with a threaded socket).

**Adhesive + Ferrule** (Chisels, Screwdrivers)

The handle is glued into a metal ferrule (a metal band that prevents splitting), then the ferrule is seated on the tool head.

**Press-Fitted** (Some Modern Tools)

The handle is pressed forcefully into the head, relying on friction to hold it.

### Step-by-Step: Replacing a Hammer Handle

This process applies to most wedged-and-pinned tools:

**Step 1: Remove the Damaged Handle**

- Secure the hammer head in a vise so it does not slip
- If there is a visible metal wedge at the top of the handle, tap it out carefully with a punch and hammer
- If there is a metal pin, drill it out or drive it out with a punch
- Once the wedge and pin are removed, the handle should come free; wiggle it and pull it out
- Clean any remaining wood from the hammer head with a file or wire brush

**Step 2: Inspect the Head**

- Look for damage to the handle hole (cracks, enlarged holes, rust)
- If the hole is enlarged, you might need to use a handle with a larger eye, or the tool may not be salvageable
- Clean rust from the hammer head with a wire brush or emery cloth
- If the head is damaged or very rusty, consider replacing the whole tool instead of just the handle

**Step 3: Select and Prepare a New Handle**

- Replacement handles are available at farm stores and hardware stores
- They come in various materials: wood, fiberglass, or composite
- Wood is traditional and feels good but requires more care in wet environments
- Fiberglass or composite handles are durable and weather-resistant
- Choose the right size — the handle should slide into the head with firm pressure, not loosely
- If the new handle is slightly oversized, you can sand or file it to fit

**Step 4: Fit the Handle to the Head**

- Test-fit the handle in the head without driving it all the way
- It should require firm pressure but not damage the handle
- Mark where the top of the head sits on the handle (this ensures consistent depth)
- If the fit is too tight, use fine sandpaper or a rasp to gently taper the handle
- If the fit is too loose, the tool will not be safe — do not proceed

**Step 5: Drive the Handle into the Head**

- Place the head on a sturdy surface
- Align the handle straight (not at an angle)
- Using a rubber mallet or a block of wood and a hammer (to protect the handle), drive the handle into the head with firm, even strikes
- As you drive, keep checking alignment — stop if it becomes crooked
- Drive until the head is fully seated (the bottom of the head reaches the mark you made in Step 4)

**Step 6: Install the Wedge**

- A wooden wedge is often included with replacement handles; if not, you can split a wooden shim or cut one from hardwood
- Insert the wedge lengthwise into the slot in the handle, at the top
- Tap it in carefully with a hammer until it is snug
- The wedge will split the handle slightly, expanding it to lock the head tightly
- Do not over-strike and split the handle excessively; the goal is just to expand it enough to lock

**Step 7: Drive the Metal Pin (if applicable)**

- Some tools have a metal pin that goes through the head and handle, usually perpendicular to the wedge
- If your tool had a pin, drill a new hole through the head and handle (carefully — hold the head firmly)
- Tap the pin in from the other side using a punch; tap until it sits flush

**Step 8: Trim and Sand**

- If the handle extends above the head, saw it off flush or slightly above
- Sand the top smooth and round the edges
- Check that the wedge and pin are fully seated and do not protrude

**Step 9: Test and Condition**

- Swing the tool gently to feel for any looseness or imbalance
- If it is loose, the handle fit was not tight enough — you may need to refit it
- If it feels solid, oil the wooden handle (if it is wood) with boiled linseed oil to protect it from moisture
- Fiberglass or composite handles can be left as-is or sealed with clear epoxy

Take before and after photos of your tool. Document the damage on the original handle and the final result. This gives your counselor a clear picture of the work you did. It also makes a great record of your skill development.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Common Issues and Fixes

**The Handle Is Stuck and Won't Come Out**

If the old handle is stuck:
- Try soaking the head with water or light oil for a few hours to soften any adhesive or corrosion
- Tap the handle carefully from side to side to loosen it
- As a last resort, you can carefully drill out the handle in sections, but be very careful not to enlarge the hole in the head

**The New Handle Is Slightly Loose**

- You can wrap athletic tape or thin cloth around the end of the handle (a few layers) to increase diameter slightly
- Then drive it in again
- The tape compresses, creating a tighter fit

**The Head Is Cracked**

If the hammer or axe head has cracks, handle replacement will not fix the underlying problem. The head itself is damaged and unsafe. Consider whether it is worth repairing or if a new tool is better.

### Tool Handle Materials Comparison

| Material | Advantages | Disadvantages | Best For |
|----------|-----------|--------------|----------|
| Wood | Traditional, comfortable, absorbs shock | Rots in wet, splinters, can split | Hammers, axes, garden tools |
| Fiberglass | Durable, weather-resistant, light | Less comfortable, can be slippery | Tools exposed to weather |
| Composite (wood/resin) | Durable, comfortable feel | More expensive | Professional-grade tools |
