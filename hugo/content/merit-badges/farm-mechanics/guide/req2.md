Explain how power is produced or transferred in a:
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This requirement covers three interconnected systems that work together to make farm equipment move and do useful work:

- **Diesel engine** — how fuel becomes rotational power
- **Hydraulic system** — how pressurized fluid creates force
- **Transmission or other power system** — how engine power reaches the wheels and implements

Understanding these three systems is the foundation of farm mechanics. When a tractor pulls a plow, a combine harvests grain, or a loader lifts a hay bale, all three systems are working together.

## 2a. Diesel Engine

{{< drg/requirement number="2a" >}}
Diesel engine
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Nearly every piece of farm equipment with an engine uses diesel fuel rather than gasoline. There is a good reason: diesel engines produce more torque (pulling force) per gallon of fuel, they last longer under heavy loads, and diesel fuel is less volatile than gasoline — an important safety advantage around hot machinery.

### The Four-Stroke Diesel Cycle

A diesel engine produces power through four strokes of a piston inside a cylinder. Each stroke is one movement of the piston — either up or down.

**Stroke 1 — Intake:** The piston moves down, creating a vacuum that draws clean air (not a fuel-air mixture like gasoline engines) into the cylinder through the intake valve.

**Stroke 2 — Compression:** The intake valve closes and the piston moves up, compressing the air into a very small space. This compression heats the air to over 1,000°F — hot enough to ignite fuel without a spark plug. This is the key difference between diesel and gasoline engines: diesels use compression ignition, not spark ignition.

**Stroke 3 — Power:** At the top of the compression stroke, a fuel injector sprays a precise amount of diesel fuel into the superheated air. The fuel ignites instantly, creating a rapid expansion of gases that forces the piston down with tremendous force. This is the stroke that produces power.

**Stroke 4 — Exhaust:** The exhaust valve opens and the piston moves up again, pushing the burned gases out of the cylinder. Then the cycle repeats.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
A diesel engine compresses air to roughly 20 times its original volume — compared to about 10 times in a gasoline engine. This extreme compression is what makes diesel engines so efficient, but it also means every component must be built heavier and stronger. That is why diesel engines weigh more than gasoline engines of similar power.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

### Why Diesel Dominates on the Farm

| Feature | Diesel Engine | Gasoline Engine |
|---|---|---|
| Torque | Higher — better for pulling heavy loads | Lower |
| Fuel efficiency | 25–30% more efficient | Less efficient |
| Engine life | 10,000+ hours typical | 3,000–5,000 hours |
| Fuel safety | Less volatile, lower fire risk | More volatile |
| Spark plugs | None — compression ignition | Required |

## 2b. Hydraulic System

{{< drg/requirement number="2b" >}}
Hydraulic system
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Hydraulics are the muscle of farm equipment. Every time a tractor lifts its front-end loader, a combine raises its header, or an excavator swings its boom, hydraulic power is doing the work. The basic principle is beautifully simple: liquids cannot be compressed, so when you push on a liquid in a closed system, it transmits that force to wherever you want it.

### How a Hydraulic System Works

A farm hydraulic system has five basic components:

1. **Reservoir (tank)** — Holds the hydraulic fluid. Also allows air bubbles to escape and heat to dissipate.
2. **Hydraulic pump** — Driven by the engine, it pressurizes the fluid and pushes it through the system. The pump does not create pressure on its own — pressure builds when the fluid encounters resistance (like a heavy load on a cylinder).
3. **Control valve** — The operator uses levers or joysticks to direct fluid to specific cylinders or motors. The valve determines which way the fluid flows and how fast.
4. **Hydraulic cylinder (or motor)** — The working end of the system. A cylinder converts fluid pressure into straight-line (linear) force — like lifting a loader bucket. A hydraulic motor converts fluid pressure into rotational force — like spinning an auger.
5. **Lines and fittings** — Hoses, tubes, and connectors that carry fluid between components. These must handle pressures of 2,000–3,000+ PSI without leaking.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Hydraulic fluid under pressure is extremely dangerous. A pinhole leak can create an invisible jet of fluid moving faster than 600 feet per second — fast enough to penetrate skin and inject fluid into your body. This is called a hydraulic injection injury, and it is a medical emergency that requires surgery. Never use your bare hand to check for leaks. Hold a piece of cardboard near the suspect area and look for fluid spray patterns.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Pascal's Law in Action

The physics behind hydraulics is Pascal's Law: pressure applied to a confined fluid is transmitted equally in all directions. This means a small pump can create enormous force at the cylinder if the cylinder is larger in diameter than the pump piston. It is the same principle as a car jack — a small hand pump lifting a two-ton vehicle.

## 2c. Transmission and Power Transfer

{{< drg/requirement number="2c" >}}
Transmission or any other power system.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

The engine produces rotational power. The hydraulic system produces linear and rotational force for implements. But getting engine power to the wheels — and controlling speed and direction — is the job of the transmission and power transfer system.

### Types of Farm Transmissions

**Gear transmission (manual):** The oldest and simplest type. The operator selects a gear ratio using a shift lever. Lower gears multiply torque for heavy pulling; higher gears allow faster travel on roads. Many older farm tractors use a manual transmission with 8 to 16 forward speeds and 2 to 4 reverse speeds.

**Hydrostatic transmission:** Uses a hydraulic pump and motor instead of gears. The operator controls speed with a pedal or lever — push forward to go forward, pull back to reverse. Speed is infinitely variable (no fixed gear steps). Common on smaller tractors, skid steers, and riding mowers.

**Powershift transmission:** Uses multiple clutch packs to shift gears without stopping the tractor or releasing a clutch pedal. The operator pushes a button or moves a small lever to change speeds while under load. Standard on large modern tractors.

**Continuously Variable Transmission (CVT):** The most advanced option. Uses a combination of mechanical gears and hydrostatic elements to provide seamless, stepless speed changes from zero to maximum. The operator sets a desired speed, and the transmission automatically adjusts ratios to maintain it.

### The Power Take-Off (PTO)

The PTO is one of the most important — and most dangerous — power transfer systems on a tractor. It is a splined shaft at the rear (and sometimes front) of the tractor that transfers engine power directly to an attached implement. When you connect a hay baler, mower, auger, or feed mixer to a tractor, it is the PTO that makes the implement work.

PTO shafts typically spin at 540 or 1,000 RPM. At 540 RPM, the shaft completes nine full rotations per second. That is fast enough to wrap a loose pant leg around the shaft and pull a person in before they can react. PTO entanglement is one of the leading causes of fatal farm injuries.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="PTO Entanglement Emergency" >}}
A coworker's clothing gets caught in an unshielded PTO shaft while a tractor is running.

- **Step 1:** Shut off the tractor engine immediately. Do not try to pull the person free while the shaft is spinning.
- **Step 2:** Call 911 immediately. PTO entanglement injuries are almost always severe.
- **Step 3:** If the person is not breathing, begin CPR after the shaft has stopped.
- **Step 4:** Do not remove clothing wrapped around the shaft — let EMS handle it.

**Prevention:** Never operate a PTO without the safety shield in place. Never step over a spinning PTO shaft. Never wear loose clothing near a PTO. Always shut off the engine before connecting or disconnecting PTO-driven equipment.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}
