### Option a: Replace a Tool Handle

{{< drg/requirement number="3a" >}}
Replace the handle of any tool found on the farm.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Hammers, axes, hoes, shovels, rakes, and files all have handles that eventually crack, split, or loosen. Using a tool with a damaged handle is dangerous — a hammer head flying off mid-swing can cause serious injury. Replacing a handle is one of the most practical skills a farm mechanic can learn.

**General steps for replacing a wooden handle (e.g., a hammer or axe):**

1. Remove the old handle. Clamp the head in a vise and drive the old handle out from below using a punch and hammer. If the handle is wedged, you may need to drill out the wood.
2. Select the correct replacement handle. Handles come in different sizes and shapes for different tools — bring the tool head to the hardware store to match it.
3. Fit the new handle into the head. The handle should slide about three-quarters of the way into the eye (the hole in the head) by hand. It should be snug but not forced.
4. Seat the head. With the handle vertical and the head at the top, strike the bottom of the handle sharply against a hard surface. The inertia of the heavy head drives it down onto the handle.
5. Install wedges. Drive a wooden wedge into the slot at the top of the handle, then drive one or two metal wedges crosswise. The wedges expand the wood inside the eye, locking the head firmly in place.
6. Trim excess handle material flush with the top of the head.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Soak the top of a newly installed wooden handle in linseed oil overnight. The oil swells the wood fibers, creating an even tighter fit, and protects the wood from moisture that could cause it to shrink and loosen over time.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Option b: Organize a Tool Rack or Storage System

{{< drg/requirement number="3b" >}}
Organize a tool rack or a storage system for nails, bolts, nuts, and washers.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A disorganized shop wastes time and creates safety hazards. When you cannot find the right wrench, you use the wrong one — and round off a bolt head. When loose hardware is scattered in random coffee cans, a five-minute repair turns into a 30-minute scavenger hunt.

**Tool rack organization principles:**

- **Group by function.** Keep all wrenches together, all screwdrivers together, all pliers together.
- **Use shadow boards or outlines.** Trace each tool's shape on the pegboard so you can see at a glance when something is missing.
- **Frequently used tools go at arm's reach.** Put the 10mm wrench, adjustable pliers, and screwdrivers at chest height.
- **Heavy tools go low.** Sledgehammers, pry bars, and large wrenches belong on lower hooks or racks.

**Hardware storage system principles:**

- **Sort by type, then by size.** All bolts together, then separated by diameter and length. All nuts the same way.
- **Label everything.** Use a label maker or permanent marker. Labels should include both standard and metric sizes.
- **Use divided bins or small drawers.** Clear-front drawers let you see what is inside without opening them.
- **Separate standard from metric.** Mixing SAE and metric fasteners causes constant frustration.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The United States is one of only three countries in the world that has not fully adopted the metric system for everyday use (along with Myanmar and Liberia). That means American farm mechanics must keep two complete sets of tools — SAE (inch) and metric — because equipment from different manufacturers uses different fastener standards.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

### Option c: Dress a Mushroomed Chisel or Punch

{{< drg/requirement number="3c" >}}
Using a hand file, properly dress the mushroomed head of a chisel or punch.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

When you hit a cold chisel or punch with a hammer thousands of times, the struck end gradually deforms. The metal spreads outward and rolls over, creating a raised, ragged edge called a "mushroom." Those mushroomed edges are dangerously sharp — they can break off and send metal fragments flying, or they can cut your hand while you grip the tool.

**Dressing** means using a hand file to remove the mushroomed material and restore the head to a safe, slightly rounded shape.

**Steps to dress a mushroomed head:**

1. Clamp the chisel or punch securely in a bench vise with the mushroomed end up.
2. Put on safety glasses — filing can release small metal particles.
3. Select a flat mill file or a second-cut file.
4. Hold the file with both hands and push it across the mushroomed edge at about a 45-degree angle. File in one direction only — push strokes, never back and forth (back-dragging dulls the file teeth).
5. Rotate the chisel slightly in the vise and repeat until you have removed all the mushroomed material around the entire circumference.
6. The finished head should be slightly rounded or beveled — not razor-sharp, not flat. A slight bevel prevents future mushrooming from happening as quickly.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Never use a chisel or punch with a mushroomed head. The mushroomed edge can shatter on impact and send metal chips into your face or eyes. Inspect striking tools before every use and dress them as soon as you see any deformation.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Option d: Dress a Screwdriver Tip

{{< drg/requirement number="3d" >}}
Using a hand file, correctly dress a screwdriver tip.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A worn, rounded, or chipped screwdriver tip is useless — it slips out of screw slots, strips screw heads, and can gouge your hand or workpiece. Dressing a screwdriver tip restores it to the correct shape so it grips screw slots firmly and drives screws safely.

**What a properly dressed flat-blade screwdriver looks like:**

- The tip sides are parallel (not tapered to a wedge or point).
- The tip is flat and square across its width — not rounded or angled.
- The tip thickness is appropriate for the screw slots you use most often — thin enough to fit the slot, thick enough not to twist.

**Steps to dress a screwdriver tip:**

1. Clamp the screwdriver blade in a bench vise with the tip extending about one inch above the jaws.
2. Use a flat mill file. File the very tip flat and square — push the file straight across the end.
3. Then file each side of the tip to make the sides parallel. Do not create a tapered wedge shape — that is the most common mistake. The sides near the tip should be straight and parallel for about 1/16 inch.
4. Check your work by inserting the tip into a screw slot. It should fit snugly without rocking.
5. Lightly round the sharp corners with a few file strokes to prevent the tip from cutting into screw slots.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Do NOT use a bench grinder to dress a screwdriver tip unless you are very experienced. Grinders remove material fast and generate heat that can ruin the temper (hardness) of the steel. If the tip turns blue from grinding heat, the metal has softened and will not hold its shape. A hand file gives you much more control.
{{< /drg/tip >}}
