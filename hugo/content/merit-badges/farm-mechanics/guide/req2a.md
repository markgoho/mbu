{{< drg/requirement number="2a" >}}
Explain how power is produced or transferred in a diesel engine.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A diesel engine is a heat engine. It burns fuel inside a cylinder to create heat and pressure, then uses that pressure to push a piston. The piston motion spins a crankshaft, which ultimately spins the wheels or powers the hydraulic pump. Diesel engines power almost every farm tractor on earth.

### The Four Strokes of a Diesel Engine

Diesel engines in farm equipment typically operate on a four-stroke cycle: intake, compression, power, and exhaust. Each stroke represents one pass of the piston.

**Stroke 1: Intake**

The intake valve opens. The piston moves down the cylinder, creating a vacuum that pulls air into the chamber. Crucially, this is *only air* — no fuel yet. Diesel engines mix fuel and air differently than gasoline engines. The cylinder fills with clean, cold air. The intake valve then closes, trapping the air.

**Stroke 2: Compression**

Both valves are closed. The piston moves back up the cylinder, squeezing the air into a smaller and smaller space. As air is compressed, its temperature rises dramatically — sometimes to over 1,000°F. This heat is critical; it will ignite the fuel that is about to be injected. The compression ratio (the ratio of cylinder volume before and after compression) is much higher in diesel engines than gasoline engines, typically 14:1 to 25:1. This high compression is why diesels are so efficient and produce so much power.

**Stroke 3: Power**

At the top of the compression stroke, the fuel injector (a precision device controlled by the engine computer) sprays a fine mist of diesel fuel directly into the hot, compressed air. The fuel ignites instantly from the heat — no spark plug is needed. The combustion gases expand rapidly, pushing the piston down hard. This is where power is created. The force of this explosion is what ultimately moves the tractor or harvester.

**Stroke 4: Exhaust**

The exhaust valve opens as the piston reaches the bottom. The piston moves back up, pushing the burned gases out through the exhaust valve and out the muffler. The exhaust valve closes, and the cycle repeats. One complete four-stroke cycle produces one power stroke (the downward push on the piston).

### From Piston Motion to Wheel Motion

The piston moves up and down, but tractors need rotational motion. The crankshaft converts this linear motion into rotation:

**The Crankshaft**

Imagine the piston rod connected to an off-center point on a shaft (the crankshaft). As the piston moves down, it pulls that off-center point down, causing the shaft to rotate. As the piston moves up, it pushes the off-center point up, continuing the rotation. Multiple pistons (typically 4, 6, or 8 in a farm tractor) are connected to different parts of the crankshaft, spaced so that their power strokes overlap. This constant sequence of power strokes keeps the crankshaft spinning smoothly.

**Connecting Rod**

The connecting rod links the piston to the crankshaft. It must be strong enough to handle the tremendous forces (thousands of pounds) generated during combustion.

**Flywheel**

Connected to the end of the crankshaft, the flywheel is a heavy wheel that stores rotational energy. During the power stroke, it is driven by the piston. During the intake, compression, and exhaust strokes, it coasts and drives the crankshaft through those phases, keeping the engine running smoothly between power events.

### Fuel Injection: The Key to Diesel Power

In a gasoline engine, fuel and air mix before they enter the cylinder. In a diesel engine, fuel is injected into pure, hot, compressed air, creating a more complete and efficient burn. Here is why this matters:

**Precision Timing**

The fuel injector must spray fuel at exactly the right moment — typically just before the piston reaches the top of the compression stroke. Modern diesel engines use electronic fuel injection controlled by a computer. The computer reads engine speed, load, throttle position, and air temperature, then adjusts the timing and volume of fuel injection for maximum efficiency.

**Multiple Injections**

Advanced diesel engines (post-2000s) actually inject fuel multiple times per power stroke — a tiny pilot injection, then the main injection, then a post-injection. This controls combustion pressure and reduces emissions.

**Fuel Quality Matters**

Diesel fuel quality affects engine performance and longevity. Water in diesel fuel can cause corrosion. Sulfur content (before regulations limited it) caused deposits. Gelling in cold weather can clog fuel lines. This is why farm mechanics check fuel regularly and maintain fuel filters — contaminated fuel damages expensive fuel injection systems.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Diesel engines are more fuel-efficient than gasoline engines because of their higher compression ratio and the fact that they burn fuel completely (almost no fuel escapes unburned). A modern diesel tractor can achieve 25+ miles per gallon, while a gasoline engine of the same power might only achieve 15 mpg. Over a tractor's lifetime, this efficiency difference translates to thousands of dollars saved.

### Starting a Cold Diesel Engine

You have probably noticed that a diesel tractor sometimes makes a loud grinding noise when you first start it in winter. This is the starter motor cranking the engine to build enough compression to ignite the fuel. On very cold mornings, some diesel engines have glow plugs — heating elements that warm the combustion chamber before starting, making ignition easier. These glow plugs are checked and replaced as part of regular maintenance.

### Engine Efficiency and Horsepower

A diesel's power output depends on:

1. **Engine displacement** (total cylinder volume) — larger displacement = more fuel burned per cycle = more power
2. **RPM** (revolutions per minute) — higher RPM = more power strokes per minute, but also more wear
3. **Air density** — cooler, denser air burns fuel more completely, producing more power
4. **Fuel quality** — clean, proper-grade fuel burns more efficiently

Farm tractors are typically operated at moderate RPM (1,200–2,200) for economy and longevity, trading some peak power for fuel savings and reliability.

### Cooling System

A diesel engine running at power produces intense heat. The cooling system (radiator, water pump, thermostat) circulates coolant through the engine to absorb heat, then through the radiator to release it into the air. A thermostat maintains optimal operating temperature (usually 180–195°F). If the engine overheats, pistons expand and can seize in their cylinders. If it runs too cold, fuel does not burn completely, and deposits form. This is why checking coolant level is part of every preoperational checklist.

### Lubrication

Engine oil serves multiple purposes: it lubricates moving parts (piston, bearings, crankshaft), cools the engine, and carries away contaminants (particles, combustion products). Farm mechanics check oil level and change it regularly because dirty, thin oil cannot protect the engine. A $50 oil change prevents a $5,000 engine repair.
