{{< drg/requirement number="3c" >}}
Using a hand file, properly dress the mushroomed head of a chisel or punch.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A chisel or punch with a mushroomed head is a safety hazard. When you strike it with a hammer, the mushroomed metal can flake off like shrapnel, endangering your eyes and hands. Using a hand file, you can restore it to safe condition — a task that teaches patience, precision, and respect for tools.

### Understanding Mushrooming

When you strike a chisel or punch repeatedly with a hammer, the top of the tool (the part you hit) gradually deforms. Metal flows outward and upward, creating a "mushroom" shape — a lip that extends beyond the tool's normal diameter.

**Why does this happen?**

Metal at high velocity (from hammer strikes) deforms under pressure. The metal does not disappear; it flows outward like dough. This is why a well-used chisel or punch eventually looks like a tiny mushroom.

**Why is it dangerous?**

When you strike the mushroomed head again, the outer lip can splinter or flake off. These tiny chips can fly at high speed, striking your eye or embedded in your hand. Protective eyewear reduces the risk, but the best safety practice is to prevent mushrooming by dressing the tool before it becomes dangerous.

### Identifying a Mushroomed Head

Look at an old chisel or punch:

- The very top of the head has a flat surface that is wider than the tool's body
- The edge of this flat surface is not sharp — it is rounded or beveled
- If you have two of the same size tool (old and new), the old one's head is noticeably larger

If you are not sure, compare it to a new tool of the same type. The difference is obvious.

### Selecting a File

You will use a **hand file** — a tool with a roughened surface that cuts metal slowly and precisely. Files come in different sizes, cuts (coarseness), and shapes.

**For dressing a chisel or punch, you need:**

- **Bastard cut** or **second cut** file (medium coarseness) — removes material reasonably quickly without being too aggressive
- **Flat file** or **mill file** (rectangular cross-section) — fits the top of a chisel or punch well
- **Length: 8"–10"** — large enough to cover the work area in a few strokes
- **Handle**: A wooden handle protects your hand and provides control (never use a file without a handle — the tang is sharp and can puncture your palm)

If your file does not have a handle, hardware stores sell replacement handles for a few dollars.

### Preparing Your Work

**Secure the tool:**

- Place the chisel or punch in a bench vise, with the head sticking up slightly above the vise jaw
- Ensure it is tight — if it shifts during filing, you will take uneven bites and create an uneven surface

**Inspect the file:**

- Wipe the file clean on a cloth
- Check that it is not clogged with metal particles (use a file card or wire brush if needed)
- Test the file on scrap metal to ensure it cuts smoothly

**Put on safety gear:**

- Safety glasses (metal particles will fly)
- Work gloves to protect your hands

### Hand Filing Technique

**The Grip:**

- Hold the file handle firmly in your dominant hand
- Place the fingers of your other hand lightly on top of the file near the tip (to guide direction, not push hard)
- The file should feel like an extension of your arm — relaxed but controlled

**The Stroke:**

- Stand at a slight angle to the bench, so you can apply even pressure throughout the stroke
- Begin with the file tip on the front edge of the mushroom
- Push the file forward and slightly downward across the mushroomed surface, following a straight path
- Apply firm but not excessive pressure — let the file do the work
- At the end of the stroke, lift the file completely and return to the start position (do not drag backward — you only cut on the forward stroke)
- The backward stroke is a recovery motion — it does no cutting

**The Pattern:**

- Start with horizontal strokes across the width of the head
- After 4–5 strokes, rotate the tool and file at a slight angle to create a crosshatch pattern
- This helps you see which areas are being leveled and identifies high spots

**Progress check:**

- Every 10–15 strokes, stop and inspect your work
- Look for an even surface
- High spots will remain bright (unfiled); you will progressively wear these down
- Continue filing until the entire top surface is uniform and level

### Expected Timeline

Dressing a mushroomed chisel head typically takes 30–60 minutes of patient work, depending on:

- **How severe the mushroom is** — a bad mushroom takes longer than a minor one
- **File coarseness** — a coarser file cuts faster but leaves rougher marks; you might need a finer file for the final finish
- **Your technique** — even, controlled strokes remove material faster than uneven ones

This is not a fast task, and rushing leads to uneven results. Budget time for patience.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Professional mechanics and machinists file tools by hand regularly. A machinist's file is one of the most important tools in the shop. The skill of reading a tool with your hands — feeling how the file cuts, adjusting pressure and angle by feel — develops over hundreds of hours of practice. This requirement introduces you to that skill.

### Finishing the Surface

After the major filing is done, you can optionally improve the finish:

**With a finer file:**

- Switch to a bastard cut or smooth cut file (finer than your working file)
- Make a few final passes to smooth the surface and remove file marks

**With sandpaper (optional):**

- Wrap medium-grit sandpaper (100–150 grit) around a block
- Sand the top surface with circular motions
- This removes file marks and creates a satin finish
- Finish with fine grit (220 grit) for a smooth, polished look

**Final inspection:**

- Look at the edge of the head from the side — it should be sharp and square, not rounded
- Look from above — the top should be flat and even, with no high spots
- Run your finger (carefully!) across the top — it should feel relatively smooth

### The Completed Tool

A properly dressed chisel or punch:

- Has a flat, level head surface
- Has a clean edge all around (no mushroom)
- Is safe to strike repeatedly without risk of flaking
- Can be used for years with regular maintenance

### Completing the Requirement

Document your work with your counselor:

1. What tool did you choose (chisel or punch)?
2. Describe the mushrooming damage before you started
3. What file did you use (size, coarseness)?
4. How long did it take?
5. What was the most challenging part?
6. How does the tool look and feel now?
7. What would you do differently next time?

Take before and after photos if possible. Let your counselor feel the finished surface and test the flatness.

### Common Filing Mistakes

| Mistake | What Goes Wrong | How to Fix |
|---------|-----------------|-----------|
| Uneven pressure | One side gets worn more, top becomes uneven | Make short passes, check alignment often |
| File too coarse | Leaves deep scratches that take a long time to smooth | Finish with a finer file |
| Tool not secured | Chisel shifts in vise, top becomes angled | Tighten the vise more; support the handle too |
| Cutting on the backstroke | Wastes energy and dulls the file faster | Only push forward; lift and return on the backstroke |
| Filing too fast | Corners become rounded, mushroom recurs quickly | Slow down; steady, even strokes |
