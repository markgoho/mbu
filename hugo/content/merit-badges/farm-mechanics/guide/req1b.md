A well-designed farm shop is more than a building with tools inside it. It is a workspace engineered for safety, efficiency, and flexibility — a place where you can overhaul a diesel engine, weld a broken implement bracket, and organize thousands of fasteners, all without tripping over extension cords or breathing toxic fumes.

## What Makes a Good Farm Shop?

Before you start drawing, think about what a farm shop needs to do. It must be large enough to fit a tractor or combine inside. It must have proper ventilation, adequate lighting, and enough electrical circuits to run welders, compressors, and power tools. And it must be organized so that every tool has a home and every safety device is within reach.

### Size and Layout

Most working farm shops are at least 40 feet wide by 60 feet long — large enough for one or two machines and a work area around them. The floor should be concrete (easy to clean, supports heavy equipment) with a slight slope toward a drain for washing.

Key zones in a well-designed shop:

- **Vehicle bay** — The open center area where machines are brought in for service. Needs overhead clearance for raised implements and loader arms.
- **Workbench area** — Along one or two walls. Includes a heavy-duty workbench, bench vise, and good task lighting.
- **Tool storage** — Pegboards, tool chests, and cabinets organized by tool type. Every tool has a labeled spot.
- **Parts and hardware storage** — Bins or drawers for nuts, bolts, washers, pins, filters, and belts.
- **Welding area** — Separated or screened from the main area. Fire-resistant curtains or screens protect other workers from UV light and sparks.
- **Fluid storage** — A dedicated area for oil, coolant, fuel, and hydraulic fluid. Ideally on a spill containment pallet.

When drawing your shop plan, use a bird's-eye view (floor plan) at a consistent scale. Graph paper makes this much easier — let each square represent one or two feet. Label every item clearly, and use a legend if you run out of room for labels.

### Mandatory Safety Devices and Features

Your shop plan must include these safety elements. These are not optional — they are required by safety codes and common sense.

{{< drg/checklist title="Mandatory Safety Features" subtitle="Include all of these in your shop plan" >}}
- Fire extinguisher(s): At least one ABC-rated extinguisher near each exit. A second one near the welding area.
- First aid kit: Mounted on the wall in a visible, accessible location.
- Eye wash station: Within 10 seconds of any area where chemicals are used.
- Ventilation system: Exhaust fans or an overhead ventilation system to remove fumes, dust, and welding smoke.
- Adequate lighting: Overhead fluorescent or LED lighting throughout, plus task lighting at workbenches.
- Clearly marked exits: At least two exits on opposite sides of the shop, with exit signs.
- Electrical panel: Accessible, labeled, and never blocked. All circuits should have ground-fault protection.
- Grounding rod: For the building's electrical system and for static discharge when working with flammable fluids.
- No-smoking signs: Posted at every entrance and near fuel and chemical storage.
- Floor markings: Yellow lines or painted zones marking walkways, fire extinguisher locations, and the swing area for bay doors.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

### Additional Equipment Worth Including

Beyond the mandatory safety items, a well-equipped farm shop will also have:

- **Air compressor** — Powers pneumatic tools (impact wrenches, blow guns, nail guns) and inflates tires.
- **Bench grinder** — For sharpening tools and dressing metal. Should have eye shields and tool rests properly adjusted.
- **Hydraulic press** — For pressing bearings, bushings, and pins in and out.
- **Welding equipment** — Arc welder (stick or MIG) with welding table, helmet, and gloves.
- **Parts washer** — A solvent tank for cleaning greasy parts before inspection or reassembly.
- **Overhead hoist or engine crane** — For lifting heavy components like engines, transmissions, and axle assemblies.
- **Drain pan and used-oil storage** — For collecting and properly disposing of used engine oil, coolant, and hydraulic fluid.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The average American farm has about $250,000 worth of machinery. A properly equipped shop that allows the farmer to do routine maintenance and minor repairs in-house can save $10,000 to $20,000 per year in dealer service charges — and avoid the costly downtime of waiting for a service truck during the busy season.
