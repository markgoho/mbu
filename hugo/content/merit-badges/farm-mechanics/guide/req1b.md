A well-designed farm shop is not just about having tools — it is about creating an environment where people work safely, efficiently, and confidently. Your plan does not need to be an architectural drawing; it just needs to show that you understand the safety principles that make a farm shop functional and compliant.

## Essential Farm Shop Layout Principles

Think about the flow of work. People should be able to move around without tripping over tools or walking in front of rotating machinery. Materials should be stored so they are easy to access but do not block exits. Hazardous materials should be locked away from curious hands (especially important if children visit the farm).

### Zones Within a Farm Shop

A well-organized farm shop has distinct zones:

**Work Zone:** Where equipment is serviced or repaired. This is the heart of the shop. It needs space around the equipment so you can walk around, access fasteners, and have room for tool carts.

**Tool & Parts Storage:** Hand tools stored in a tool rack or cabinet. Fasteners (bolts, nuts, washers) organized in labeled drawers. Parts organized by equipment type.

**Fluid & Chemical Storage:** Engine oil, hydraulic fluid, antifreeze, solvents, welding rod, and any flammable liquids locked in a cabinet. These must be kept away from ignition sources.

**Power Tool Area:** Drill press, grinder, welder, compressor — machines that stay in one place and are used frequently. These should be positioned away from the main walkway to avoid distracting other workers.

**Cleanliness & Washup:** A work sink with clean water and hand soap. Paper towels or clean rags. A place to dispose of oily rags safely (in a metal bin, not loose in the shop).

**Emergency & First Aid:** First-aid kit mounted on a wall where everyone can find it. Fire extinguisher(s) positioned where they can be reached quickly.

## Mandatory Safety Devices & Features

Every farm shop must include these non-negotiable items:

### Fire Suppression

**Fire extinguishers:** Every farm shop must have at least one Class B (for flammable liquid) fire extinguisher rated for the size of your shop. A 5-10 lb ABC multi-purpose extinguisher is typical for a home or small farm shop. Larger commercial shops may need multiple extinguishers.

- Mount extinguishers on a wall near an exit, at eye level, so they are immediately visible in an emergency.
- Know which extinguisher is which: Class A (ordinary combustibles like wood), Class B (flammable liquids like gasoline and oil), Class C (electrical fires).
- For a farm shop, Class B is mandatory; ABC multi-purpose is best because it handles most farm workshop fires.

**No smoking rule:** Post a "No Smoking" sign. Gasoline, diesel, and hydraulic fluid vapors are extremely flammable. One spark can cause an explosion.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Never use water on a grease fire or flammable liquid fire. Water causes the burning liquid to splatter and spread. Use a Class B fire extinguisher or smother the fire by cutting off oxygen (sliding a lid over a pan, closing an oven door, or blanketing the fire with sand). For fires involving electrical equipment, use a Class C extinguisher.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Ventilation

**Exhaust fans or windows:** Engine exhaust, welding fumes, and solvent vapors accumulate in enclosed spaces and become hazardous. A good farm shop has either windows that can be opened or mechanical exhaust fans that pull fumes out.

- If you run an engine inside the shop (for testing or warming up), always position it so the exhaust points toward a window or fan.
- Natural ventilation (open door and window) is the simplest solution for small shops.
- Larger shops with welding or engine work benefit from a ceiling exhaust fan and makeup air inlet.

### Electrical Safety

**GFCI outlets:** Ground Fault Circuit Interrupter outlets detect unusual current and shut off power instantly, preventing electrocution. Install GFCI outlets in any area near water (washup sink area) and in damp locations.

**Adequate lighting:** A well-lit shop is a safe shop. You cannot see what you are working on if you are squinting in shadows. Install overhead lights (fluorescent or LED), plus movable work lights for detailed tasks.

**Grounding for power tools:** All corded power tools should be three-pronged (grounded) or double-insulated. Do not use a three-pronged tool with a two-pronged adapter — this defeats the grounding protection.

### Floor Safety

**Non-slip flooring:** Concrete is the standard, but it gets slippery when wet or oily. Paint the shop floor with anti-slip epoxy paint, or apply stick-on non-slip tape to walkways.

**Clear walkways:** Mark the main pathways with yellow paint so people do not trip over hoses, cables, or tools left on the floor. Keep walkways at least 3 feet wide.

**Drain for spills:** If you are doing work that involves liquids (draining oil, flushing radiators), a floor drain or sloped floor directing fluids toward a collection point prevents them from accumulating and becoming a slip hazard.

### Access & Emergency Exits

**Two exits minimum:** In case of fire or emergency, people need an alternative way out. A farm shop should have at least two exits — typically a main door and a side or back door.

**Exit signs and emergency lighting:** Exits should be marked and lit so people can find them in an emergency. Even a simple glow-in-the-dark arrow pointing to the exit is better than nothing.

### First Aid & Injury Response

**First-aid kit:** Mounted on a wall in an easily accessible location. The kit should include bandages, antiseptic, gauze, tape, tweezers (for splinters), and pain relief medicine. Keep it stocked — people actually use it.

**Emergency contact numbers:** Post the farm shop phone number, poison control, the nearest hospital, and your local emergency number (911) on a wall so they are immediately available if someone is injured.

### Material Safety & Hazard Communication

**Safety Data Sheets (SDS):** Keep SDS documents for every hazardous material in your shop — oil, coolant, solvents, welding flux, diesel fuel. Store them in a binder or cabinet where workers can access them if they need information quickly.

**Proper storage for hazardous materials:** Locked cabinet (or separate locked building) for flammable liquids. Keep them away from heat sources and ignition sources. Store incompatible materials separately (oxidizers away from flammable liquids, acids away from bases).

**Labeling:** Every container should be clearly labeled with its contents. Do not store diesel in an old gasoline can — confusion like this causes fires or poisoning.

## Design Your Farm Shop Plan

Your plan should show:

1. **Overall layout:** Roughly rectangular or L-shaped outline showing the boundaries of the shop.
2. **Work zones:** Label areas for equipment service, tool storage, chemical storage, and power tool area.
3. **Mandatory safety devices:** Mark on your plan where you would place:
   - Fire extinguisher (with the type labeled: e.g., "10 lb ABC")
   - First-aid kit
   - Emergency exits (doors with arrow and label "EXIT")
   - Ventilation (windows or exhaust fan)
   - GFCI outlets or electrical panel
   - Eyewash station (if applicable)

4. **Walkways:** Draw main traffic patterns and label them as clear pathways.
5. **Storage:** Show where hand tools, parts, and hazardous materials would be stored.
6. **Lighting:** Note where overhead and work lights would be located.

Your plan does not need to be fancy — it can be drawn on plain paper. But it should demonstrate that you understand the function of each area and why each safety device is positioned where it is. Be ready to explain your choices to your counselor.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="You Discover a Fuel Spill in Your Shop" >}}
While getting ready to work on a tractor, you notice gasoline leaking from a fuel can onto the concrete floor. What do you do?

- **Step 1:** Do NOT ignore it or think you will clean it up later. Do it now.
- **Step 2:** Stop anyone from using electrical tools or creating sparks in that area.
- **Step 3:** Put on gloves and grab absorbent material (sand, cat litter, oil-absorbent pads).
- **Step 4:** Spread the absorbent material over the spill and let it soak for a few minutes.
- **Step 5:** Sweep up the saturated material and place it in a metal container (not a trash can — use a designated spill kit container or metal drum).
- **Step 6:** Ventilate the area by opening windows or running the exhaust fan.
- **Step 7:** Repair or replace the fuel can so the leak does not happen again.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

## Summary

A safe farm shop is a reflection of the person who runs it. It says: "I respect my tools. I care about the people who work here. I take safety seriously." When you design your plan, think about what a counselor or a farm safety inspector would want to see. Cover the mandatory items, show that you understand the layout principles, and be ready to explain why each safety device is important.
