Do ONE of the following:
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This requirement gets you working directly on a machine. Choose **one** of the three options below. Each one covers a different aspect of equipment maintenance — hands-on service, systematic inspection, or seasonal preparation. Your choice may depend on what equipment is available to you and the time of year.

- **Option a** — Perform a full service (grease, oil, filters, radiator)
- **Option b** — Create and execute a preoperational checklist
- **Option c** — Prepare a machine for winter storage

### Option a: Full Engine Service

{{< drg/requirement number="4a" >}}
On an engine-powered machine: Grease all fittings, change the oil and oil filter, clean the air filter, clean the radiator fins, and replace the fuel filters.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This option is the most hands-on. You will perform five common maintenance tasks that every engine-powered machine needs regularly. Together, these tasks keep the engine cool, clean, lubricated, and efficiently fueled.

**Greasing fittings:** Grease fittings (also called zerk fittings) are small nipples found on pivot points, bearings, and joints throughout the machine. Using a grease gun, pump grease into each fitting until you see clean grease begin to emerge from the joint. This pushes out old, contaminated grease and ensures the joint stays lubricated. Check the operator's manual for a diagram showing all grease points — some machines have 20 or more.

**Changing oil and oil filter:** Engine oil lubricates moving parts, carries away heat, and suspends contaminants. Over time, oil breaks down and the filter fills with debris. To change them:

1. Run the engine briefly to warm the oil (warm oil drains faster and carries more contaminants out).
2. Position a drain pan under the engine. Remove the drain plug and let the oil drain completely.
3. Remove the old oil filter. Apply a thin film of new oil to the gasket on the new filter, then install it hand-tight.
4. Replace the drain plug and tighten to the specified torque.
5. Fill the engine with the correct type and amount of fresh oil (check the manual).
6. Start the engine, let it idle for a minute, then shut it off. Check the oil level and top off if needed.

**Cleaning the air filter:** The air filter prevents dust, chaff, and debris from entering the engine. Farm environments are extremely dusty, so air filters clog faster than in a car. Remove the filter element and tap it gently to dislodge loose debris. If it is a paper element, blow compressed air through it from the inside out. If it is a foam pre-filter, wash it with soap and water, let it dry, and lightly oil it before reinstalling.

**Cleaning radiator fins:** The radiator dissipates engine heat. When chaff, bugs, and dust clog the fins, the engine overheats. Use compressed air or a garden hose (low pressure) to blow or wash debris off the radiator fins. Always blow from the engine side outward — the same direction air flows when the engine is running.

**Replacing fuel filters:** Diesel fuel filters trap water and particles that would damage the fuel injection system. Most diesel engines have a primary (water separator) and secondary (final) fuel filter. Replace both according to the maintenance schedule. After installing new filters, you may need to bleed air from the fuel system — the operator's manual will explain the procedure.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Used engine oil is a known carcinogen with prolonged skin contact. Always wear nitrile gloves when changing oil. If oil contacts your skin, wash thoroughly with soap and water. Dispose of used oil at a recycling center — never dump it on the ground or in the trash.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Option b: Preoperational Checklist

{{< drg/requirement number="4b" >}}
For any engine-powered machine, create a preoperational checklist; include checking the engine coolant, engine oil, hydraulic and/or transmission fluid, and battery voltage (using a voltmeter). Using your checklist, conduct a preoperational check of that machinery or equipment.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Professional operators check their machines before every use — not because they expect to find a problem, but because finding a problem before you start working is always cheaper and safer than finding it in the middle of a field.

**Building your checklist:**

Your checklist must include at least these four items, but a thorough checklist should cover much more.

{{< drg/checklist title="Preoperational Checklist Template" subtitle="Check before every use" >}}
- Engine oil level: Pull the dipstick, wipe it clean, reinsert, pull again. Level should be between the two marks.
- Engine coolant level: Check the overflow reservoir (never open a hot radiator cap). Level should be between MIN and MAX marks.
- Hydraulic fluid level: Check the sight glass or dipstick on the hydraulic reservoir. Top off if low.
- Transmission fluid level: Check per the operator's manual — some require the engine running in neutral.
- Battery voltage: Using a voltmeter set to DC volts, touch the red probe to the positive terminal and the black probe to the negative terminal. A fully charged 12V battery should read 12.6V or higher. Below 12.0V indicates a battery that needs charging.
- Tire pressure/condition: Check for proper inflation, cuts, or embedded objects.
- Lights and signals: Test headlights, taillights, turn signals, flashers, and warning lights.
- Belts and hoses: Look for cracks, fraying, or leaks.
- Fuel level: Sufficient for the planned work.
- Safety devices: ROPS (Roll-Over Protective Structure) in place, seat belt functional, PTO shield installed.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/tip >}}
Using a voltmeter is straightforward, but here is the one detail most people miss: test the battery with the engine OFF. A running engine charges the battery through the alternator, so you will see 13.5–14.5V with the engine on. That tells you the alternator is working, but it does not tell you the battery's actual condition. Test with the engine off first, then with it running.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Option c: Winter Storage Preparation

{{< drg/requirement number="4c" >}}
Prepare any farm machine for winter storage.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Machines that sit idle through winter can suffer corrosion, flat-spotted tires, fuel system contamination, rodent damage, and battery failure. Proper winter preparation prevents all of these problems.

**Key steps for winter storage:**

1. **Clean the machine thoroughly.** Remove mud, crop residue, and grease buildup. Moisture trapped in debris causes corrosion.
2. **Change the oil and filters.** Used oil contains acids and contaminants that corrode internal engine parts during storage. Fresh oil protects.
3. **Fill the fuel tank and add stabilizer.** A full tank reduces condensation (water formation inside the tank). Fuel stabilizer prevents diesel fuel from degrading and forming varnish over the winter months.
4. **Grease all fittings.** Fresh grease prevents moisture from entering bearings and pivot points.
5. **Check and top off coolant.** Ensure the antifreeze concentration is adequate for your coldest expected temperatures. Use a coolant hydrometer or refractometer to test.
6. **Disconnect or maintain the battery.** Either remove the battery and store it in a warm, dry location, or connect a trickle charger (battery maintainer) to keep it at full charge. A dead battery in freezing temperatures can crack its case.
7. **Inflate tires to the maximum recommended pressure.** Underinflated tires develop flat spots when sitting on cold concrete for months.
8. **Cover exposed hydraulic cylinder rods.** Chrome cylinder rods can pit and corrode if left exposed to moisture. Apply a thin coat of grease or wrap with a protective sleeve.
9. **Block openings where rodents can enter.** Mice love nesting in air cleaner boxes, exhaust pipes, and cab upholstery. Stuff steel wool into openings.
10. **Store under cover if possible.** A shed or tarp protects from rain, snow, and UV damage.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Diesel fuel left untreated in a tank over winter can grow a slimy biological contaminant called "diesel algae" (actually a fungus called *Hormoconis resinae*). This organism lives at the boundary between fuel and water at the bottom of the tank. It clogs fuel filters and can damage injectors. Fuel stabilizer and water-removing additives prevent it.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}
