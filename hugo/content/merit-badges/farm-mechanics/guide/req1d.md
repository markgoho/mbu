Describe what a safety data sheet (SDS) is and tell why it is used. Obtain the SDS for any engine coolant, oil, grease, fuel, hydraulic or transmission fluid, or other flammable or hazardous materials you use in meeting the requirements for this merit badge.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Imagine you spill a gallon of hydraulic fluid on your skin and it starts burning. Or you accidentally inhale fumes from a parts-cleaning solvent and feel dizzy. What do you do? How serious is it? What should a doctor know if you end up in the emergency room? The Safety Data Sheet — or SDS — answers all of these questions for every chemical product in your shop.

## What Is a Safety Data Sheet?

An SDS is a standardized document that provides detailed information about a chemical product's hazards, safe handling procedures, and emergency response measures. Every manufacturer of a hazardous chemical is required by law (OSHA's Hazard Communication Standard) to produce an SDS for their product and make it available to anyone who uses it.

An SDS is not the same as a product label. The label gives you a summary. The SDS gives you the full picture — composition, health effects, fire-fighting measures, spill cleanup, disposal requirements, and more.

### Why the SDS Matters

The SDS matters because farm shops are full of chemicals that can hurt you if mishandled:

- **Engine oil** contains additives that are harmful with prolonged skin contact
- **Antifreeze/coolant** (ethylene glycol) is highly toxic if swallowed — and it tastes sweet, which makes accidental ingestion a real risk
- **Diesel fuel** is a skin irritant and its vapors are harmful to breathe
- **Hydraulic fluid** can cause skin and eye irritation
- **Brake cleaner** contains solvents that can damage your nervous system with repeated exposure
- **Battery acid** (sulfuric acid) causes severe chemical burns on contact

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Ethylene glycol antifreeze is responsible for thousands of poisoning cases each year in the United States — many involving pets and small children who are attracted to its sweet taste. Some states now require manufacturers to add a bittering agent to make coolant taste terrible, reducing accidental ingestion.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## The 16 Sections of an SDS

Every SDS follows the same globally standardized format with 16 sections. You do not need to memorize all 16, but you should know where to find the most critical information.

| Section | Title | What It Tells You |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Identification | Product name, manufacturer, emergency phone number |
| 2 | Hazard(s) Identification | Health hazards, signal words, hazard pictograms |
| 3 | Composition | Chemical ingredients and concentrations |
| 4 | First-Aid Measures | What to do if you inhale, swallow, or get it on your skin or in your eyes |
| 5 | Fire-Fighting Measures | What type of fire extinguisher to use, special hazards when burning |
| 6 | Accidental Release Measures | How to clean up a spill safely |
| 7 | Handling and Storage | How to use and store the product safely |
| 8 | Exposure Controls/PPE | What PPE to wear when handling the product |
| 9 | Physical/Chemical Properties | Appearance, odor, boiling point, flash point |
| 10 | Stability and Reactivity | What conditions or chemicals could cause a dangerous reaction |
| 11 | Toxicological Information | Health effects from short-term and long-term exposure |
| 12–16 | Environmental, Disposal, Transport, Regulatory, Other | Disposal methods, shipping rules, and regulatory classifications |

### The Sections You Need First

In an emergency, go straight to **Section 4 (First-Aid Measures)**. It tells you exactly what to do for skin contact, eye contact, inhalation, and ingestion.

Before you start a job, check **Section 8 (Exposure Controls/PPE)** to know what protection you need — gloves, goggles, respirator, or all three.

## How to Obtain an SDS

Getting an SDS is straightforward:

1. **Check the container.** Many products include a web address or QR code that links directly to the SDS.
2. **Search the manufacturer's website.** Most manufacturers (Shell, Valvoline, John Deere, Chevron, etc.) have SDS databases where you can search by product name.
3. **Use a free SDS database.** Websites like the one linked below aggregate SDS documents from thousands of manufacturers.
4. **Ask your dealer.** Equipment dealers can provide SDS documents for the fluids they sell and recommend.

{{< drg/checklist title="SDS Requirement Checklist" subtitle="Steps to complete this requirement" >}}
- Pick a product you will use for this merit badge: coolant, oil, grease, fuel, hydraulic fluid, or transmission fluid.
- Find and obtain the SDS for that specific product (brand and product name matter — the SDS for Valvoline 15W-40 is different from Shell Rotella 15W-40).
- Read Sections 1, 2, 4, and 8 carefully.
- Be ready to explain to your counselor what the SDS is, why it is important, and what you learned from the one you obtained.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/tip >}}
Keep a binder of printed SDS documents in your farm shop, organized alphabetically by product name. In an emergency, you need to find the right SDS in seconds, not minutes. Many farms also keep a digital copy on a shop computer or tablet.
{{< /drg/tip >}}
