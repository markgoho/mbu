Machines are the backbone of modern farms. Tractors, combines, balers, pumps, and irrigation systems do the heavy lifting — but only when they work. A broken piece of equipment can stall an entire operation for days, costing thousands of dollars and threatening a harvest. Behind every working farm is someone who knows how to keep these machines running.

## Then and Now

**Then:** Fifty years ago, farm mechanics was part tradition, part necessity. A farmer had to be a generalist — fixing their own equipment from manuals, adapting parts, learning through trial and error. Farm shops were simple: a workbench, a few hand tools, and time. Fewer specialized parts existed, so mechanics improvised and made repairs last.

**Now:** Modern farm equipment is sophisticated. Diesel engines have electronic fuel injection and computer diagnostics. Hydraulic systems operate with precision. Equipment connects to satellite GPS and adjusts automatically. Today's farm mechanic combines hands-on troubleshooting with diagnostic technology. They attend specialized training, follow manufacturers' specifications, and use torque wrenches and diagnostic scanners. The stakes are higher — downtime costs real money — but so is the expertise and earning potential.

## Get Ready!

This badge teaches you how farm equipment actually works and how to keep it safe and operational. You will learn why preventive maintenance matters, how power systems transfer energy, and what real mechanics do on a working farm. By the end, you will understand that farm mechanics is not just fixing broken things — it is preventing problems before they happen.

## Kinds of Farm Equipment

Farm equipment comes in many varieties, each with unique systems and maintenance needs. As you work through this badge, you will encounter machines like these:

### Tractors

The workhorse of the farm. Modern tractors have powerful diesel engines (often 100–400+ horsepower), hydraulic systems for lifting implements, transmissions with multiple gears, and increasingly, electronic controls. From small garden tractors to 4-wheel-drive monsters, they all follow the same mechanical principles.

### Combines & Harvesters

Self-propelled machines that cut, thresh, and clean grain in one pass. They combine multiple systems: engines for power, hydraulics for moving parts, belts and chains for transmission, and grain handling augers and conveyors. They are complex and expensive, making preventive maintenance critical.

### Balers

Machines that compress cut hay or straw into compact bales for easy transport and storage. They use hydraulic pressure to compress material, rollers and belts to move material through the machine, and mechanical linkages to eject finished bales. They are relatively simple but must run reliably during the narrow harvest window.

### Irrigation Systems

Pumps and pipes that deliver water across fields. They rely on proper fluid levels, filter condition, and seal integrity. Whether powered by diesel engines or electric motors, they demand regular maintenance to avoid mid-season failure.

### Grain Dryers & Mills

Stationary equipment that processes harvested grain. Grain dryers use heating systems, fans, and precise temperature controls. Mills grind grain into feed or flour. Both depend on clean air filters, proper cooling, and safe electrical systems.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Working with farm equipment carries real hazards: rotating shafts can trap clothing, hydraulic pressure can cause serious injury, diesel fumes are toxic, and sharp implements cut easily. Throughout this badge, you will learn how to recognize these hazards and work safely around them.

## Your Path Through the Badge

You will start with safety — the foundation for all farm mechanics work. Then you will learn how power is generated and transferred through engines and hydraulic systems. Next, you will get your hands dirty with hands-on tool skills and engine maintenance. Finally, you will explore the profession and discover whether farm mechanics might be in your future.
