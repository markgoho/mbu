### Option a: Tightening Hydraulic Fittings

{{< drg/requirement number="6a" >}}
Tightening hydraulic fittings.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Hydraulic fittings connect hoses, tubes, and components throughout the hydraulic system. Over time, vibration and thermal cycling can loosen fittings, causing leaks that reduce system performance and waste expensive hydraulic fluid. Properly tightening a hydraulic fitting is a precision task — overtightening is just as dangerous as undertightening.

As you learned in [Req 2](/merit-badges/farm-mechanics/guide/req2/), hydraulic systems operate at extreme pressures. A fitting that is not properly torqued can blow out under pressure, spraying fluid and potentially causing an injection injury.

**Step-by-step procedure:**

1. **Shut down the engine and relieve pressure.** Move all hydraulic controls through their full range with the engine off to release trapped pressure. Wait for the system to cool.
2. **Identify the fitting type.** Common types include JIC (37-degree flare), O-ring boss (ORB), and pipe thread (NPT). Each has different tightening specifications.
3. **Inspect the fitting.** Look for visible damage, stripped threads, or a damaged sealing surface. A damaged fitting must be replaced, not just tightened.
4. **Hand-tighten first.** Thread the fitting in by hand until it is snug. This ensures proper thread alignment and prevents cross-threading.
5. **Mark the fitting and body.** Use a paint marker or permanent marker to draw a line across the fitting and the port body. This reference mark tells you exactly how far you have turned the fitting.
6. **Tighten to specification.** Use the correct wrench size (never an adjustable wrench on hydraulic fittings — it can round the hex). Tighten the fitting the specified number of flats past hand-tight:
   - JIC fittings: typically 1 to 1.5 flats past hand-tight
   - ORB fittings: typically 1 flat past hand-tight
   - Always check the manufacturer's torque specification if available
7. **Start the engine and pressurize.** Run the system at low RPM and operate the controls slowly. Watch the fitting for any sign of leakage.
8. **Verify with cardboard.** Hold a piece of cardboard near the fitting to check for leaks you cannot see. Never use your bare hand.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Never tighten a hydraulic fitting while the system is under pressure. The fitting can blow out, releasing a high-pressure jet of hot fluid. Always shut down the engine, relieve pressure, and wait for the system to cool before working on any hydraulic connection.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Option b: Checking the Air Filter

{{< drg/requirement number="6b" >}}
Checking the air filter.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

The air filter is your engine's first line of defense against the dust, chaff, pollen, and debris that fill the air on a working farm. A clogged filter starves the engine of air, reducing power and fuel efficiency. A damaged filter lets unfiltered air into the engine, where abrasive particles destroy cylinder walls, pistons, and valves — damage that costs thousands of dollars to repair.

**Step-by-step procedure:**

1. **Locate the air filter housing.** On most tractors, it is a large cylindrical or rectangular canister near the top of the engine, connected to the intake manifold by a rubber hose or duct.
2. **Release the housing latches or remove the cover.** Some use spring clips; others use wing nuts or bolts. Note how the cover attaches so you can reassemble it correctly.
3. **Remove the primary filter element.** This is usually a large cylindrical paper element. Pull it straight out — do not twist or tilt it, which could knock loose debris into the intake.
4. **Inspect the element.** Hold it up to a bright light. If you can see light through the pleats, the filter still has some life. If it is uniformly dark and no light passes through, it needs replacing. Also check for tears, holes, or collapsed pleats — any physical damage means immediate replacement.
5. **Check for a pre-filter or inner safety filter.** Many farm engines have a foam pre-filter (outer wrap) that catches large particles before they reach the paper element. Some also have a smaller inner safety filter that protects the engine if the primary filter fails. Inspect both.
6. **Clean if serviceable.** Tap the paper element gently against a hard surface to dislodge loose dust. For more thorough cleaning, blow compressed air through the element from the inside out (clean side to dirty side) at no more than 30 PSI. Wash foam pre-filters with soap and water, dry completely, and lightly coat with filter oil.
7. **Check the sealing surfaces.** Inspect the rubber gaskets on the filter and the sealing surfaces inside the housing. Any gap means unfiltered air enters the engine.
8. **Reassemble.** Install the filter element, replace the cover, and secure all latches. Make sure the housing is airtight.

{{< drg/tip >}}
During harvest, check the air filter daily. Grain dust and chaff can clog a filter in just a few hours of heavy operation. Some machines have a restriction indicator on the air filter housing — a colored window or gauge that changes from green to red when the filter is clogged. Check this indicator during your preoperational inspection (see [Req 4](/merit-badges/farm-mechanics/guide/req4/)).
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Option c: Cleaning with a Wire-Brush Wheel

{{< drg/requirement number="6c" >}}
Cleaning a work piece with a wire-brush wheel.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A wire-brush wheel mounted on a bench grinder is one of the most useful tools in a farm shop. It removes rust, old paint, scale, hardened grease, and corrosion from metal parts, preparing them for inspection, welding, painting, or reassembly. But a spinning wire wheel is also one of the more dangerous tools in the shop — loose wires can break free and embed in skin like tiny needles, and work pieces can be grabbed by the wheel and thrown.

**Step-by-step procedure:**

1. **Inspect the wire wheel.** Before turning on the grinder, check the wire brush for broken, bent, or missing wires. A damaged wheel should be replaced. Check that the wheel is securely mounted and the arbor nut is tight.
2. **Adjust the tool rest.** The tool rest should be within 1/8 inch of the wire wheel. A large gap allows the work piece to jam between the rest and the wheel.
3. **Put on full PPE.** This is non-negotiable:
   - Safety goggles (not just glasses — wires can fly around side shields)
   - Face shield over the goggles for extra protection
   - Leather work gloves
   - Long-sleeved shirt with sleeves buttoned at the wrist
   - Hearing protection if the grinder is loud

4. **Secure the work piece.** For small parts, use locking pliers (Vise-Grips) to hold the piece — never hold a small part with bare fingers near a spinning wheel. For larger parts, a firm two-handed grip is acceptable.
5. **Turn on the grinder.** Let the wheel reach full speed before touching the work piece to it.
6. **Apply the work piece to the lower half of the wheel.** Always work on the part of the wheel that is spinning downward toward the tool rest. If you work on the upper half, the wheel can grab the work piece and throw it.
7. **Use light, even pressure.** Let the wire tips do the work. Pressing too hard bends the wires and reduces cleaning effectiveness. Move the work piece slowly across the wheel face.
8. **Inspect your work.** When the surface is clean bare metal, you are done. If rust or scale remains in pits, the wire wheel has done its job — further cleaning requires chemical treatment or sandblasting.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Never wear loose clothing, jewelry, or work without eye protection when using a wire-brush wheel. Broken wires fly off the wheel at high speed and can penetrate skin or eyes. Always stand to the side of the wheel — not directly in front of it — when starting the grinder, in case the wheel disintegrates.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}
