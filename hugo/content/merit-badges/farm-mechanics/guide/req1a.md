{{< drg/requirement number="1a" >}}
Discuss with your counselor the safety equipment, tools, and clothing used while checking or repairing farm equipment. Use this equipment, tools, and/or clothing (when needed or called for) in meeting the requirements for this merit badge.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Before a mechanic touches any farm equipment, they put on the right gear. This is not theater — it is a survival skill. A spinning shaft, a pressurized fluid line, or a piece of flying metal treats everyone the same.

### Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

Farm equipment work demands specific PPE suited to the hazards you face:

**Eye Protection**

Safety glasses or goggles are non-negotiable. Flying sparks during grinding, metal chips from drilling, and hydraulic fluid spray all target your eyes. When working with chemicals or pressure-cleaning equipment, use chemical splash goggles. For grinding operations, a full face shield over safety glasses offers maximum protection.

**Hand Protection**

Leather work gloves protect against cuts, burns, and pinches. Nitrile gloves prevent oil absorption into skin and work under leather gloves when handling solvents or harsh chemicals. Never wear loose gloves around rotating equipment — they can catch and pull your hand into the machinery.

**Respiratory Protection**

Diesel fumes contain nitrogen oxides and particulates that irritate lungs. When working on engines or in enclosed spaces, wear a dust mask rated N95 or higher. For spray painting or solvent exposure, use a respirator with organic vapor cartridges. Proper fit is critical — if the mask does not seal to your face, it offers no protection.

**Footwear**

Steel-toe boots protect toes from dropped tools and heavy equipment. Choose boots with oil-resistant soles, since you will be walking on slippery floors coated with hydraulic fluid or grease. Never work in sneakers or sandals around farm equipment.

**Hearing Protection**

Diesel engines and hydraulic pumps produce noise above 85 decibels, which damages hearing permanently. Foam earplugs inserted correctly reduce noise by 20–30 decibels. Over-ear earmuffs are easier to put on and take off but sometimes leave gaps. Double protection (plugs + muffs) is best during high-noise work.

**Body Coverage**

Long sleeves and long pants prevent burns from hot engine parts and cuts from sharp edges. Cotton or flame-resistant material is better than synthetics, which melt. Avoid loose clothing or dangling fabric that could catch on rotating parts.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Keep a well-organized PPE station in your farm shop — a hook for safety glasses, a bin for gloves, earplugs in a labeled container, and a boot rack. If PPE is easy to grab, you are more likely to use it every time.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Safety Tools

Certain tools exist specifically to keep you safe:

**Torque Wrench**

Bolts must be tightened to a precise specification — too loose and they vibrate apart, too tight and they break. A torque wrench ensures you apply exactly the right pressure. For hydraulic fittings, this is critical: over-tightening can crush the fitting; under-tightening can leak pressurized fluid.

**Pressure Gauge**

Hydraulic and pneumatic systems operate under pressure. A gauge lets you measure pressure safely before disconnecting lines. Never guess at pressure — you could be hit by a spray of hot fluid.

**Lockout/Tagout Devices**

When you stop an engine for maintenance, you must prevent someone else from accidentally starting it while you are working. Lockout devices physically prevent engine start (lock the key out of reach), and warning tags alert others that maintenance is in progress.

**Fire Extinguisher**

Engine fuel, hydraulic fluid, and grease are all flammable. A Type ABC multipurpose fire extinguisher (suitable for ordinary combustibles, flammable liquids, and electrical fires) should hang near your work area. Know how to use it: aim at the base of the flame, squeeze, and sweep side to side.

### The Machine Itself as a Tool

Always remember: the equipment is a tool, and it demands respect. Before you work on a machine:

- **Turn off the engine** and remove the key.
- **Relieve all hydraulic pressure** by cycling the hydraulic controls or using a pressure relief valve.
- **Wait for hot surfaces to cool** — radiators and engine blocks can stay hot for hours.
- **Secure the machine** so it cannot roll or fall on you (block tires, engage parking brakes, use jack stands).

{{< drg/be-prepared title="You Are About to Work on a Tractor" >}}
Your neighbor asks you to help check her tractor before spring planting. Here is the safe way to start:

- **Step one**: Make sure the engine is completely off and cool. Turn the ignition to OFF, remove the key, and wait at least 15 minutes.
- **Step two**: Put on all required PPE: safety glasses, work gloves, steel-toe boots, long sleeves. You are not starting the tractor, so you can skip hearing protection for now.
- **Step three**: Locate the battery disconnect or kill switch. Make sure it is in the OFF position.
- **Step four**: Check the tractor's position. If it is on a slope, block the wheels. Do not work under the tractor unless you have used jack stands to support it securely.
- **Step five**: Review the work you are about to do with your counselor or a mentor. Ask: "What could go wrong here?" Listen to their answer.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

### Communication & Knowledge

The most important safety tool is knowledge. Ask questions, read the manual, and never assume. If you do not know why you are doing something or what could go wrong, stop and ask. Your counselor is there to help — that is part of their role.
