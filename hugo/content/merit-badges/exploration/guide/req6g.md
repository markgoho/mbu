{{< drg/requirement number="6g" >}}
Determine team selection. Identify who is essential for the expedition to be successful and what skills are required by the expedition leader.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

When NASA selects astronauts for a space mission, they do not just look for the best pilots or the smartest scientists. They look for people whose skills complement each other, who communicate well under pressure, and who can live and work together in tight quarters for months. Your expedition team is smaller, but the same principles apply.

## Who Needs to Be There?

Start with the requirements of your expedition, not with who is available. Different expeditions need different skills.

### Required Roles

Every expedition needs these roles filled (one person can fill multiple roles):

**Expedition Leader** — The person ultimately responsible for decisions, safety, and the group's adherence to the plan. For your merit badge expedition, this should be a qualified adult as specified in Req 7a — a trained expedition leader, teacher, museum representative, or other experienced person.

**Data Collector(s)** — The people doing the core work of the expedition: recording observations, taking measurements, collecting samples, photographing subjects. This is where you and your fellow Scouts contribute.

**Navigator** — The person responsible for knowing where the team is, following the planned route, and ensuring the team stays on schedule. They carry the map, GPS, and compass.

**First Aider** — At least one person should be designated as the primary first-aid responder. They carry the first-aid kit and have the training to use it.

**Safety Officer** — The person monitoring conditions (weather, terrain, group fatigue) and calling attention to hazards. On a small team, the expedition leader often fills this role.

### Optional Roles

Depending on your expedition, you might also benefit from:

- **Photographer/Documentarian** — Someone focused on photo and video documentation
- **Subject Matter Expert** — A teacher, naturalist, or specialist who can help identify specimens or interpret observations
- **Communications Officer** — The person responsible for check-ins with your base contact

{{< drg/tip >}}
On a small Scout team, every person will wear multiple hats. The key is to assign roles explicitly — do not assume someone else is watching the weather, tracking time, or carrying the first-aid kit. Write down who is responsible for what.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## What Makes a Good Expedition Leader?

The requirement specifically asks about the skills an expedition leader needs. Here are the essential ones:

### Decision-Making Under Pressure

Conditions change. Weather moves in, someone gets hurt, the trail is blocked. A good leader makes calm, informed decisions — and is willing to call off the expedition if safety requires it.

### Communication Skills

A leader must clearly explain the plan, delegate tasks, give constructive feedback, and listen to the team. On an expedition, miscommunication can lead to missed data, wasted time, or safety incidents.

### Knowledge of the Subject

The leader does not need to be an expert in your specific topic, but they should understand the expedition's objectives well enough to make good decisions about priorities and timing.

### Wilderness and Outdoor Skills

Navigation, weather reading, first aid, Leave No Trace principles, and the ability to manage logistics in the field are all important leader skills — many of which you are developing through Scouting.

### Adaptability

Plans rarely survive contact with reality. The best leaders adjust on the fly while keeping the team focused on the primary objective. If a planned survey site turns out to be flooded, a good leader pivots to the secondary site rather than forcing the team through unsafe conditions.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Ernest Shackleton famously posted this newspaper advertisement when recruiting for his Antarctic expedition: "Men wanted for hazardous journey. Small wages, bitter cold, long months of complete darkness, constant danger, safe return doubtful. Honour and recognition in case of success." He reportedly received over 5,000 applications. The best teams are built on shared commitment to the mission.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Building Your Team

{{< drg/checklist title="Team Selection Steps" subtitle="Use this process to build your expedition team" >}}
- List the roles your expedition requires.
- Identify which skills each role needs.
- Match available people to roles based on their skills and interests.
- Ensure at least one qualified adult is on the team (per Req 7a).
- Confirm that every team member can commit to the full expedition schedule.
- Brief all team members on their roles, the plan, and the safety procedures.
- Make sure team members are physically capable of the planned activities.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

## Team Size

For a Scout-scale expedition, a team of 3–6 people is usually ideal. Fewer than three creates safety risks (what if one person is injured and another needs to stay with them?). More than six can be difficult to coordinate and may disturb the environment you are studying.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="When Team Members Cannot Make It" >}}
What if someone drops out at the last minute?

- **Reassign their roles** immediately — do not leave a gap in navigation or safety.
- **Reassess whether the expedition can still succeed** with fewer people. Can you still collect the data you need?
- **Postpone if necessary.** It is better to delay than to go with an incomplete or unsafe team.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}
