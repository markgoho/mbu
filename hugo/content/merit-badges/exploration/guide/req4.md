{{< drg/requirement number="4" >}}
Real-Life Exploration. Do ONE of the following:
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

For this requirement, you choose **one** of three options. Each one asks you to research a different aspect of real-world exploration and share what you learn with your counselor and unit. Read through all three options below to find the one that interests you most.

## Option A: Profile a Living Explorer

Learn about a living explorer — someone who is actively conducting exploration today. Create a short report or presentation (verbal, written, or multimedia) on this person's objectives and the achievements of one of their expeditions.

### Where to Start

Choose an explorer whose work genuinely interests you. Here are some living explorers to consider:

- **Jane Goodall** — Primatologist who has spent over 60 years studying chimpanzees in Tanzania. Her work reshaped our understanding of animal intelligence and behavior.
- **Sylvia Earle** — Oceanographer and National Geographic Explorer-in-Residence who has led over 100 deep-sea expeditions and advocates for ocean conservation.
- **Robert Ballard** — Ocean explorer who discovered the wreck of the Titanic and pioneered the use of remotely operated vehicles for deep-sea exploration.
- **Nainoa Thompson** — Hawaiian navigator who revived traditional Polynesian wayfinding navigation, sailing across the Pacific using only stars, waves, and winds.
- **Jill Pruetz** — Primatologist studying chimpanzees in Senegal who documented the first known use of weapons by non-human primates.
- **Edith Widder** — Marine biologist who designed deep-sea cameras that captured the first video of a giant squid in its natural habitat.

{{< drg/tip >}}
You do not have to pick a famous explorer. A local university professor studying cave ecosystems or a wildlife biologist tracking endangered species in your state is just as valid. Your counselor may even help you connect with a researcher for an interview.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Building Your Report

{{< drg/checklist title="Explorer Profile Checklist" subtitle="Include these elements in your report or presentation" >}}
- Who is the explorer and what field do they work in?
- What was the objective of one specific expedition or research project?
- What did the expedition achieve or discover?
- What challenges did the explorer face?
- How has their work contributed to our understanding of the world?
- What qualities (from Req 3c) does this explorer demonstrate?
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

## Option B: Research a Scientific Expedition

Learn about an actual scientific exploration expedition. Research the mission objectives and the expedition's most interesting or important discoveries. Explain how the information helped scientists answer important questions.

### Expeditions to Consider

- **Mars Perseverance Rover Mission (2020–present)** — NASA's most advanced Mars rover is searching for signs of ancient microbial life, collecting rock samples for future return to Earth, and testing technologies for future human missions.
- **HMS Challenger Expedition (1872–1876)** — The founding voyage of modern oceanography, covering 70,000 nautical miles and discovering over 4,700 new species.
- **Apollo 11 (1969)** — The first crewed Moon landing, which returned lunar soil samples and proved that humans could travel to and work on another world.
- **The Human Genome Project (1990–2003)** — An international lab-based expedition to map every gene in human DNA.
- **NOAA Okeanos Explorer missions** — Ongoing deep-sea expeditions using ROVs to explore the ocean floor, with live video streams available to the public.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Connecting Expedition to Answers" >}}
Your counselor will ask how the expedition helped answer important questions. Structure your response like this:

- **The question:** What did scientists want to know before the expedition?
- **The method:** How did the expedition try to answer it?
- **The answer:** What did they discover?
- **The impact:** How did the answer change the field or lead to new questions?
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

## Option C: Explore Laboratory Research

Learn about types of exploration that take place in a laboratory or scientific research facility (medicine, biology, chemistry, physics, astronomy). Explain how laboratory research and exploration are similar to field research and exploration.

### Lab Exploration Examples

- **CERN Large Hadron Collider** — The world's largest particle physics laboratory, where scientists discovered the Higgs boson in 2012 by accelerating particles to near-light speed.
- **Genetic Sequencing Labs** — Facilities that decode DNA to understand diseases, trace human ancestry, and develop gene therapies.
- **Astronomical Observatories** — Ground-based telescopes and the scientists who operate them, searching for exoplanets, black holes, and gravitational waves.
- **Medical Research Facilities** — Labs developing vaccines, cancer treatments, and surgical techniques.

### Lab vs. Field: Finding the Parallels

Both lab and field exploration follow the scientific method: question, hypothesis, experiment, analysis, conclusion. Here are key similarities:

| Element | Field Exploration | Lab Exploration |
|---------|------------------|-----------------|
| **Objective** | Answer a question about the natural world | Answer a question through controlled experiment |
| **Planning** | Route, permits, supplies, safety | Protocol design, materials, safety |
| **Data collection** | Specimens, measurements, observations | Experimental results, instrument readings |
| **Risk management** | Weather, terrain, wildlife | Chemical hazards, equipment failure, contamination |
| **Documentation** | Field notebooks, GPS logs, photos | Lab notebooks, data files, imaging |
| **Peer review** | Share findings for verification | Publish results for replication |

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Alexander Fleming discovered penicillin in 1928 when he noticed that mold had contaminated one of his bacterial cultures — and the bacteria near the mold had died. He could have thrown out the contaminated dish. Instead, he investigated. That moment of curiosity in a London laboratory saved an estimated 200 million lives.
