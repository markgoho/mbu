Plan the mission. Create an expedition agenda or schedule. List potential documents or permits needed.

Now that you know *what* you want to accomplish, it is time to plan *how* — creating a detailed schedule and understanding what legal requirements or permissions you might need.

## Create an Expedition Schedule

A detailed schedule helps you:
- Anticipate how long each task will take
- Identify potential bottlenecks
- Allocate resources wisely
- Communicate clearly with your team

### Daily Schedule

Create a day-by-day breakdown of your expedition:

**Day 1 (travel/setup):**
- 8:00 AM — Depart base camp
- 10:30 AM — Arrive at site
- 11:00 AM — Set up base camp, safety briefing
- 1:00 PM — Lunch
- 2:00 PM — Initial site reconnaissance, mark survey areas
- 5:00 PM — Return to camp, document observations
- 6:00 PM — Team dinner, review next day's plan

**Day 2 (field work):**
- 7:00 AM — Breakfast
- 8:00 AM — Begin data collection in Area 1
- 12:00 PM — Lunch
- 1:00 PM — Continue in Area 1 or begin Area 2
- 5:00 PM — Return to camp, process samples
- 7:00 PM — Data entry and team meeting

**Day 3 (field work continuation):**
- [Similar pattern]

**Final day (wrap-up):**
- 9:00 AM — Final samples/data collection
- 12:00 PM — Pack equipment, break camp
- 2:00 PM — Begin travel home
- 5:00 PM — Arrive home

### Key Planning Questions

- **How much travel time?** Account for getting to and from your site.
- **How long per task?** If you are collecting water samples, how long does each sample take?
- **Buffer time?** Add extra time for unexpected delays (weather, equipment issues, slower progress than expected).
- **Rest and meals?** Do not schedule 16-hour days. Your team needs sleep and sustenance.
- **Data processing?** Some field work (pressing plant specimens, labeling samples, recording data) happens in camp, not in the field.

## Permits & Legal Requirements

Many explorations require permits, permissions, or compliance with regulations. Planning ahead prevents problems.

### Questions to Ask

1. **Is the land public or private?**
   - Public land (national forests, parks, BLM land) often requires permits for research
   - Private land requires owner permission

2. **Is there a managing agency?**
   - National parks have specific rules and permit requirements
   - State forests may require research permits
   - Local parks often have simpler requirements

3. **Are you collecting specimens?**
   - Removing plants, rocks, or artifacts usually requires permits
   - Different jurisdictions have different rules

4. **Are you near water?**
   - Stream restoration projects need permits
   - Wetland work usually needs environmental permits

5. **Is there scientific research involved?**
   - Universities require Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval for human research
   - Animal research needs approval

6. **Are there endangered species involved?**
   - Work affecting endangered species needs federal permits

### Common Permits & Permissions

**Scientific Research Permits:**
- Research permits from land managing agencies
- Environmental impact assessments
- State wildlife permits (if studying animals)

**Access Permits:**
- National park backcountry permits
- Private land owner permission (written, if possible)
- Local recreation permits

**Collection Permits:**
- Plant collection permits
- Archaeological artifact permits
- Rock/mineral collection permits

**Specialized Permits:**
- River permit (for float trips)
- Cave exploration permit
- Marine research permit

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Start the permit process EARLY — sometimes 6–12 weeks before your expedition. Do not wait until the last minute. Some permits take a long time to process.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### How to Find Out What You Need

1. **Contact the land managing agency** — Call the park office, forest service office, or BLM office and explain your project. Ask what permits you need.

2. **Check the website** — Most agencies list permit requirements online.

3. **Look at similar projects** — If others have done similar explorations in the same area, learn what permits they obtained.

4. **Talk to your counselor** — Your merit badge counselor may have experience and can advise you.

5. **Contact universities** — If your project is science-based, contact the university closest to your site. They often know local requirements.

### Building Your Permit Timeline

Create a calendar showing when to apply for permits:

- **12 weeks before:** Identify permits needed and application requirements
- **10 weeks before:** Submit permit applications
- **6 weeks before:** Follow up on permit status
- **4 weeks before:** Confirm permits received, resolve any issues
- **2 weeks before:** Final confirmation that all permits are in place
