Plan the mission. Create an expedition agenda or schedule. List potential documents or permits needed.

Once you have clear objectives (from Req 6a), the next step is building the plan that will get you there. A mission plan is your roadmap — it tells you where you are going, when you will be there, and what paperwork you need to make it happen legally and safely.

## Building an Expedition Schedule

A good expedition schedule breaks the entire project into phases, not just the time you spend in the field.

### Phase 1: Pre-Expedition Research (weeks before)

Before you set foot on site, gather background information. What is already known about your study area? What similar research has been done? What species, geological features, or historical records should you expect to encounter? This research sharpens your plan and prevents you from "discovering" something that is already well-documented.

### Phase 2: Preparation (days before)

Assemble and check equipment. Confirm permits and permissions. Brief your team on the plan, the objectives, and the safety procedures. Pack supplies. Charge batteries. Print maps.

### Phase 3: Field Work (expedition day)

This is the heart of the expedition. Your field schedule should include:

- **Arrival and setup time** — getting to the site, establishing a base
- **Data collection windows** — the blocks of time dedicated to your primary objective
- **Break and meal times** — you need energy to do good work
- **Contingency buffer** — extra time for unexpected finds, weather delays, or equipment issues
- **Pack-out and cleanup** — leaving the site as you found it (or better)

### Phase 4: Post-Expedition (days after)

Analyze your data, organize your notes and photos, and write your report (you will need this for Req 8c). This phase is where raw observations become real findings.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Build your schedule backward from your objective. If your goal is to count bird species at dawn and dusk, your schedule must get you to the site before sunrise. If you need to collect water samples at three locations along a stream, estimate travel time between sites and work backward from your expected departure time.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Sample Expedition Schedule

Here is a sample one-day expedition schedule for a stream ecology survey:

| Time | Activity |
|------|----------|
| 6:00 AM | Meet at trailhead, gear check, safety briefing |
| 6:30 AM | Hike to Site 1 (upstream) |
| 7:00 AM | Water quality testing, macro-invertebrate sampling |
| 8:30 AM | Hike to Site 2 (mid-stream) |
| 9:00 AM | Water quality testing, macro-invertebrate sampling |
| 10:30 AM | Break, field lunch, review data so far |
| 11:00 AM | Hike to Site 3 (downstream) |
| 11:30 AM | Water quality testing, macro-invertebrate sampling |
| 1:00 PM | Photo documentation, final observations |
| 1:30 PM | Pack out, Leave No Trace check |
| 2:00 PM | Return to trailhead, debrief |

## Documents and Permits

Many exploration activities require permission, permits, or documentation. Failing to get these can end your expedition before it starts — or get you in legal trouble.

### Common Documents You May Need

- **Landowner permission** — If your expedition site is on private property, you need written permission from the owner.
- **Park or land management permits** — Many parks, forests, and wildlife refuges require research permits for data collection, specimen collection, or off-trail access. Contact the managing agency well in advance.
- **Scouting America activity permissions** — Your counselor can advise on any Scouting-specific permission forms needed.
- **Parental/guardian permission** — Required for any activity outside normal troop meetings.
- **Collection permits** — If you plan to collect specimens (plants, insects, rocks, water samples), some jurisdictions require permits. State fish and wildlife agencies regulate biological specimen collection.
- **Access agreements** — Some sites require liability waivers or research agreements.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Never assume you have permission to access a site or collect specimens just because it is outdoors. Federal lands, state parks, private property, and protected areas all have rules. Violating them can result in fines, confiscated specimens, and embarrassment for you and your troop. When in doubt, ask first.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### How to Get Permits

1. **Identify the land manager.** Is the site managed by the National Park Service, US Forest Service, state parks, a county, a private landowner, or someone else?
2. **Contact them early.** Permit processing can take days or weeks. Explain that you are a Scout working on a merit badge and describe your planned activities.
3. **Keep copies.** Bring copies of all permits and permission letters with you on expedition day.

{{< drg/checklist title="Mission Planning Checklist" subtitle="Steps to complete before finalizing your plan" >}}
- Define your expedition timeline (pre-expedition, field day, post-expedition).
- Build a detailed field-day schedule with time blocks.
- Identify who manages the land where you will explore.
- Research what permits or permissions you need.
- Contact landowners or agencies and request permission.
- Confirm all documents are in hand before departure.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}
