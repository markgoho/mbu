For this final requirement, you choose **one** of two options. Option A focuses on exploration-related careers; Option B explores how exploration can become a lifelong hobby. Read both options to decide which interests you more.

## Option A: Explore an Exploration Career

{{< drg/requirement number="9a" >}}
Explore careers related to this merit badge. Research one career to learn about the training and education needed, costs, job prospects, salary, job duties, and career advancement. Your research methods may include—with your parent or guardian's permission—an internet or library search, an interview with a professional in the field, or a visit to a location where people in this career work. Discuss with your counselor both your findings and what about this profession might make it an interesting career.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Exploration is not just something people used to do — it is a thriving professional field with careers across dozens of industries. Here are some paths to consider:

### Marine Scientist / Oceanographer

Study the ocean's physical, chemical, and biological properties. Marine scientists work on research vessels, at coastal labs, and with underwater vehicles to explore everything from coral reefs to deep-sea trenches.

- **Education:** Bachelor's degree minimum; master's or Ph.D. for research positions
- **Salary range:** $55,000–$100,000+ depending on experience and specialization
- **Employers:** NOAA, universities, private research firms, aquariums

### Wildlife Biologist / Field Ecologist

Study animals and ecosystems in their natural habitats. This career involves significant fieldwork — tracking animals, conducting surveys, and monitoring populations.

- **Education:** Bachelor's degree in biology or ecology; master's for advanced positions
- **Salary range:** $45,000–$80,000
- **Employers:** US Fish & Wildlife Service, state agencies, universities, conservation organizations

### Geologist / Geoscientist

Study Earth's materials, processes, and history. Geologists work in energy exploration, environmental consulting, mining, and academic research.

- **Education:** Bachelor's degree; master's or Ph.D. for research
- **Salary range:** $50,000–$120,000+ (energy sector salaries tend to be higher)
- **Employers:** Oil and gas companies, mining firms, environmental consultancies, USGS

### Astronomer / Astrophysicist

Study the universe beyond Earth — stars, galaxies, planets, dark matter, and the origins of the cosmos. This career combines observation, data analysis, and theoretical modeling.

- **Education:** Ph.D. typically required for research positions
- **Salary range:** $60,000–$130,000
- **Employers:** NASA, universities, observatories, national laboratories

### Archaeological Researcher

Study human history through material remains — artifacts, structures, landscapes, and biological evidence. Archaeologists work at excavation sites, in museums, and in laboratories.

- **Education:** Master's or Ph.D. for research positions; bachelor's for field technician roles
- **Salary range:** $40,000–$85,000
- **Employers:** Universities, museums, cultural resource management firms, government agencies

{{< drg/checklist title="Career Research Guide" subtitle="Address each of these in your discussion with your counselor" >}}
- What education and training does the career require?
- What are the costs of that education?
- What are the job prospects (is the field growing or shrinking)?
- What is the typical salary range?
- What are the day-to-day job duties?
- How does someone advance in this career?
- What about this career interests you personally?
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

## Option B: Exploration as a Hobby

{{< drg/requirement number="9b" >}}
Explore how you could use knowledge and skills from this merit badge to pursue a hobby. Research any training needed, expenses, and organizations that promote or support it. Discuss with your counselor what short-term and long-term goals you might have if you pursued this.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

You do not need a Ph.D. to be an explorer. Many people pursue exploration as a passionate hobby — and some hobbyists make contributions that rival those of professionals.

### Citizen Science

Participate in real scientific research by collecting and submitting data. Platforms like iNaturalist (species identification), eBird (bird observations), and Globe Observer (environmental monitoring) let you contribute to research from anywhere in the world.

### Caving (Spelunking)

Explore cave systems — mapping passages, documenting geological formations, and sometimes discovering new chambers. Local grottos (caving clubs) provide training and mentorship. The National Speleological Society is the primary organization.

### Scuba Diving

Explore underwater environments. Certification through PADI or SSI starts at age 10 (Junior Open Water) and age 15 (Open Water). Diving opens the door to reef exploration, underwater photography, and marine citizen science.

### Amateur Astronomy

Observe and photograph celestial objects with telescopes and cameras. Amateur astronomers have discovered comets, asteroids, and supernovae. Local astronomy clubs provide access to dark-sky sites and shared equipment.

### Geocaching and Orienteering

Navigate to specific coordinates or checkpoints using maps, compasses, and GPS. These activities build the navigation and outdoor skills that are core to field exploration.

### Fossil and Mineral Collecting

Search for fossils and minerals in legally accessible areas. Many states have public collecting sites. Local gem and mineral clubs organize field trips and provide identification help.

{{< drg/tip >}}
When discussing goals with your counselor, think in two timeframes. A short-term goal might be "join a local astronomy club and attend three star parties this year." A long-term goal might be "earn my PADI Advanced Open Water certification by age 16 and participate in a coral reef monitoring project."
{{< /drg/tip >}}

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
In 2023, an amateur astronomer in Japan named Hideo Nishimura discovered a new comet using a standard digital camera with a telephoto lens. Comet Nishimura (C/2023 P1) became visible to the naked eye and was one of the year's brightest celestial events. You do not need a billion-dollar telescope to contribute to space exploration.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}
