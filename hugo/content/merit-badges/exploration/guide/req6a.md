Every successful expedition begins long before anyone packs a bag. It begins with a question — and the clearer that question, the more focused and productive the expedition will be. Requirement 6 walks you through the eight essential steps of expedition planning. You will use all of these steps when you plan your own expedition in Requirement 7.

## Why Objectives Come First

Objectives are the foundation of every decision that follows. Your budget, your equipment, your team, your timeline, and your safety plan all flow from what you are trying to accomplish. Without clear objectives, an expedition is just a trip.

Think of it this way: if you tell your counselor "I want to go explore the woods behind the school," that is a nice idea but not an expedition. If you say "I want to document and identify every species of amphibian in the wetland behind the school during spring breeding season," now you have an objective that drives everything else — the timing, the equipment (field guide, nets, camera), the method (systematic survey), and the outcome (a species inventory).

## Writing Strong Objectives

Good expedition objectives share several characteristics:

### Specific

"Study nature" is vague. "Identify and photograph all flowering plant species within a one-mile section of the Blue Ridge Parkway during peak spring bloom" is specific. The more specific your objective, the easier it is to plan and to know whether you succeeded.

### Measurable

How will you know if you achieved your goal? A measurable objective has a clear endpoint. "Document at least 15 bird species" is measurable. "See some birds" is not.

### Achievable

Your objective should stretch you but remain realistic given your time, resources, skills, and the scope of this badge. Planning an expedition to Antarctica is not feasible; planning an overnight expedition to map the geology of a local river gorge is.

### Time-Bound

Set a timeframe. "Complete a one-day survey of insect diversity in the county park on April 15" is better than "study insects sometime this spring."

{{< drg/tip >}}
Professional expeditions often have both a **primary objective** (the main question they are trying to answer) and **secondary objectives** (additional data or observations they plan to collect along the way). For your expedition, having one strong primary objective and one or two secondary objectives is a great approach.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Examples of Scout-Scale Expedition Objectives

Here are some realistic expedition objectives that fit the scope of this badge:

| Expedition Type | Primary Objective | Secondary Objective |
|-----------------|-------------------|---------------------|
| Ecological survey | Count and identify all reptile species in a 2-acre wetland | Measure water temperature and pH at 5 locations |
| Historical investigation | Document and photograph all grave markers in a pre-1900 cemetery | Research 3 individuals buried there |
| Geological mapping | Identify rock types along a 3-mile creek bed | Collect 10 representative samples for identification |
| Urban ecology | Survey bird species using a downtown park over 4 mornings | Record feeding behaviors and preferred habitats |
| Water quality study | Test pH, dissolved oxygen, and turbidity at 5 points along a stream | Compare upstream and downstream of a bridge or development |

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Ernest Shackleton's 1914 Imperial Trans-Antarctic Expedition had a simple primary objective: cross the Antarctic continent from sea to sea. When that became impossible after the ship was crushed by ice, Shackleton set a new objective: get every crew member home alive. He succeeded — and that adaptive leadership is studied to this day.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}
