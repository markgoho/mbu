{{< drg/requirement number="6e" >}}
Determine communication and transportation needs. Plan how to keep in contact with your base or the outside world, and determine how you will communicate with each other on-site.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A group of researchers once spent three days lost in a Central American jungle because their GPS batteries died and they had no backup communication plan. They survived, but the expedition's data was lost and the entire team was dehydrated by the time a search party found them. Planning your communication and transportation is not just about convenience — it can be about survival.

## Transportation Planning

### Getting to the Site

Start with the basics: how does your team get from home to the expedition site?

- **Private vehicle** — The most common option for Scout-scale expeditions. Confirm that you have enough seats, that the driver knows the route, and that you have a backup plan if the vehicle breaks down.
- **Public transportation** — Buses, trains, or ferries may be options depending on location. Check schedules carefully — a missed return bus can strand your team.
- **Hiking in** — If your site requires a hike from the trailhead, factor in hiking time, distance, and elevation gain. Your schedule (from Req 6b) should account for travel time on foot.

### Moving Around On-Site

How will your team move between data collection points? If your sites are spread across a large area, you may need to plan rest stops, water refills, and time buffers. If the terrain is difficult (steep, brushy, or marshy), movement will be slower than you expect.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Always plan your return trip at the same time as your outbound trip. Running late is the leading cause of bad decisions in the field — people rush, skip safety steps, or try to hike in the dark. Build a hard departure time into your schedule and stick to it.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Communication Planning

Communication plans have two layers: staying in touch with the outside world, and communicating with each other on-site.

### External Communication

Someone who is NOT on the expedition should always know:

- Where you are going (exact location, trailhead, GPS coordinates)
- When you are leaving and when you expect to return
- Who is in your group
- What to do if you do not check in by a certain time

This person is your **emergency contact** or **base contact.** Give them a written copy of your itinerary.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Before you leave, establish a check-in schedule with your base contact. For example: "We will text at noon and again when we reach the trailhead on our way out. If you have not heard from us by 5:00 PM, call the park ranger station at this number." This simple protocol could save your life.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Communication Equipment Options

| Device | Range | Reliability | Notes |
|--------|-------|-------------|-------|
| Cell phone | Varies (depends on coverage) | Moderate — can lose signal in remote areas | Most accessible option; download offline maps |
| Two-way radios (walkie-talkies) | 1–5 miles typical | High within range | Great for on-site team communication |
| Whistle | ~1 mile (sound) | Very high — no batteries needed | Universal distress signal: 3 blasts |
| Satellite communicator (e.g., Garmin inReach) | Global | Very high | Sends SOS and text messages via satellite; worth renting for remote expeditions |
| Signal mirror | Line of sight | Moderate | Backup signaling in open terrain |

### On-Site Communication

If your team splits up to cover different observation points, you need a plan for staying in touch:

- **Two-way radios** are ideal for on-site communication. Assign channels and test the radios before you leave.
- **Buddy system** — Never let anyone work alone. Pairs stay within earshot of each other.
- **Pre-arranged signals** — Three whistle blasts mean "come to me." One long blast means "I am here."
- **Meeting times** — Set specific times and locations to regroup and share observations.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Lost Communication Protocol" >}}
What if your cell phone dies and your radios fail?

- **Stay calm.** Panicking wastes energy and leads to poor decisions.
- **Stay where you are** if you are lost — moving makes you harder to find.
- **Use your whistle** — three blasts repeated every few minutes is the universal distress signal.
- **Make yourself visible** — move to open ground, use bright clothing, use a signal mirror.
- **Trust your base contact** — if you established a check-in protocol, help will come when you miss your check-in time.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}
