{{< drg/requirement number="1b" >}}
Explain how approaches to exploration may differ if it occurs in the ocean, in space, in a jungle, or in a science lab in a city.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A deep-sea explorer and a lab researcher both ask questions, form hypotheses, and collect data — but one works at 36,000 feet below sea level in total darkness while the other works under fluorescent lights with a coffee mug on the bench. The environment shapes everything: the equipment, the risks, the timeline, and even how you communicate with the outside world.

## Ocean Exploration

The ocean is Earth's last great frontier. Exploring it means dealing with extreme pressure, near-total darkness below 200 meters, frigid temperatures, and the impossibility of breathing without equipment.

**Equipment:** Submersibles, remotely operated vehicles (ROVs), autonomous underwater vehicles (AUVs), sonar mapping systems, dive suits, underwater cameras, and sample collection tools designed to work at crushing pressures.

**Challenges:** Human divers can only reach about 130 feet on compressed air (deeper with specialized gas mixtures). Below that, robots do the work. Communication underwater relies on acoustic signals, which travel differently than radio waves. Expeditions are expensive — a single deep-sea submersible dive can cost tens of thousands of dollars.

**Timeline:** Ocean expeditions can last from a single day (a submersible dive) to months (a research vessel cruise). Data collection is often opportunistic — you study what you encounter.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Ocean exploration carries serious risks: decompression sickness ("the bends"), equipment failure at depth, hypothermia, and the remote locations of research vessels. Every dive requires meticulous safety protocols, backup systems, and trained support teams on the surface.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Space Exploration

Space is the most extreme environment humans have ever entered. There is no air, no pressure, and temperatures swing from hundreds of degrees in sunlight to hundreds below in shadow.

**Equipment:** Spacecraft, space suits, life support systems, telescopes, satellites, rovers, and mission control infrastructure. Every gram of equipment must be justified because launch costs are enormous.

**Challenges:** The distances are staggering. A radio signal takes about 13 minutes to reach Mars — so a rover cannot be joystick-controlled in real time. Astronauts face radiation exposure, bone density loss, muscle atrophy, and psychological stress from isolation. Most space exploration today is done by robotic probes because sending humans is so costly and dangerous.

**Timeline:** A mission to the International Space Station takes about six months. The Voyager 1 probe, launched in 1977, is still sending data from interstellar space — 47 years and counting.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The James Webb Space Telescope orbits the Sun about one million miles from Earth. It is so far away that if something breaks, no astronaut can go fix it — unlike the Hubble Space Telescope, which was repaired by Space Shuttle crews five times.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Jungle Exploration

Tropical jungles and rainforests are dense, humid, and teeming with life — much of it undiscovered. Jungle exploration is physically demanding and biologically rich.

**Equipment:** Machetes, GPS devices, specimen collection kits, camera traps, climbing harnesses (for canopy research), water purification systems, and insect repellent. Unlike ocean or space exploration, much of the equipment is relatively simple and portable.

**Challenges:** Navigation is difficult because dense vegetation blocks GPS signals and makes it hard to see landmarks. Diseases like malaria, dengue fever, and parasitic infections are constant threats. The climate is brutal — extreme heat, constant humidity, and torrential rain. Wildlife hazards range from venomous snakes to territorial primates.

**Timeline:** Jungle expeditions typically last days to weeks. Researchers often establish base camps and make daily forays into surrounding areas.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Jungle explorers often work closely with Indigenous communities who know the terrain, the plants, and the wildlife far better than any outsider. Ethical exploration means respecting this knowledge and partnering with local people rather than ignoring their expertise.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Science Lab Exploration

A laboratory in a city might not look like a place of exploration, but some of the most transformative discoveries in history were made at a lab bench. The discovery of penicillin, the sequencing of the human genome, and the detection of the Higgs boson particle all happened in laboratories.

**Equipment:** Microscopes, spectrometers, centrifuges, DNA sequencers, particle accelerators, computer models, and specialized software. Lab equipment can cost millions of dollars, but it is reusable and does not need to survive a jungle or the ocean floor.

**Challenges:** Lab exploration requires patience — experiments often fail, and results may take months or years. Funding is competitive. Ethical review boards must approve research involving animals or humans. And unlike field exploration, lab work can feel repetitive and isolating.

**Timeline:** A single experiment might take hours; a research program can span decades. The Large Hadron Collider at CERN took 10 years to build and has been running experiments for over 15 years.

## Comparing Approaches

| Factor | Ocean | Space | Jungle | Lab |
|--------|-------|-------|--------|-----|
| **Primary risk** | Pressure, drowning | Radiation, vacuum | Disease, terrain | Repetition, funding |
| **Communication** | Acoustic signals | Radio with delay | Satellite phone, radio | Phone, email, instant |
| **Cost per mission** | Very high | Extremely high | Moderate | Varies widely |
| **Human presence** | Limited depth | Very limited | Extensive | Full-time |
| **Data collection** | Sensors, samples, video | Remote instruments, rovers | Specimens, photos, notes | Instruments, models |
| **Duration** | Days to months | Months to decades | Days to weeks | Hours to years |

## The Common Thread

Despite all these differences, the fundamental process is the same in every environment: **define a question, plan the approach, gather data, analyze results, and share findings.** The environment changes the tools and the risks, but the intellectual framework of exploration stays constant.

When you plan your own expedition in Requirements 6 through 8, you will apply this same framework — adapted to whatever environment you choose to explore.
