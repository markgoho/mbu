{{< drg/requirement number="6h" >}}
Establish detailed recordkeeping (documentation) procedures. Plan the interpretation and sharing of information at the conclusion of the expedition.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Data that is not recorded is data that does not exist. You could make the most extraordinary observation in the field — a species never documented in your county, a rock formation that changes everything about the area's geology — but if you do not write it down systematically, it is just a memory. And memories fade, blur, and become unreliable remarkably fast.

## Why Documentation Matters

Professional scientists follow documentation procedures so strict that every notebook page is numbered, every entry is dated, and corrections are made by crossing out (not erasing) — because the original observation might matter later. You do not need to be that formal, but you should understand the principle: **the purpose of your expedition is to bring back knowledge, and knowledge lives in your records.**

## What to Document

### Field Notes

Your field notebook is the most important document you will produce. Every observation goes here — species seen, measurements taken, weather conditions, time, location, team member observations, and anything unexpected.

A good field entry includes:

- **Date and time** of every observation
- **Location** — GPS coordinates, or a clear description tied to a landmark
- **Weather conditions** — temperature, wind, cloud cover, precipitation
- **The observation itself** — what you saw, measured, heard, or collected
- **Context** — what was happening around the observation (habitat, nearby features, activity)

{{< drg/tip >}}
Write more than you think you need to. In the field, a detail seems obvious and unforgettable. Two weeks later, you will wish you had written it down. Record smells, sounds, colors, textures — not just species names and numbers.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Photographs and Video

Photos are powerful evidence, but only if they are organized. For each photo, record what it shows, where it was taken, and when. Some tips:

- Photograph a written label or sign before each new location to create visual "bookmarks" in your photo timeline.
- Include scale objects — a ruler, a coin, or a pencil — next to small subjects.
- Take wide shots for context and close-ups for detail.
- Photograph your data sheets at the end of each site visit as a backup.

### Data Sheets

Pre-printed data sheets save time and ensure consistency. Design your data sheets before the expedition with columns for each type of data you plan to collect. For example, a bird survey sheet might have columns for species, number observed, behavior, habitat, time, and location.

### Specimens and Samples

If you are collecting physical specimens (water samples, rock samples, plant cuttings — with permits), label each one immediately with a unique identifier, the date, the location, and the collector's name. A bag of unlabeled rocks is useless; a bag of labeled rocks is science.

## Documentation Procedures

Establish these procedures before you head into the field:

{{< drg/checklist title="Documentation Procedures" subtitle="Set these up before expedition day" >}}
- Assign a primary note-taker (and a backup).
- Design and print data sheets tailored to your objectives.
- Number all notebook pages in advance.
- Establish a photo naming or tagging convention.
- Decide how samples will be labeled and stored.
- Plan a mid-expedition data review (check for gaps while you can still fill them).
- Bring redundant recording methods (notebook + camera + data sheets).
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Charles Darwin's field notebooks from the Beagle voyage still exist and can be viewed online. His meticulous sketches, measurements, and observations — written in cramped handwriting on small pages — formed the foundation of *On the Origin of Species*, published 23 years after the voyage ended.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Sharing Your Findings

The last part of this requirement asks you to plan how you will interpret and share your information after the expedition. Exploration that stays in a notebook helps no one else.

### Interpretation

After the expedition, review all your data and ask:

- What patterns do you see?
- Did you answer your primary question?
- Were there unexpected findings?
- What would you do differently next time?

### Sharing

You will write a formal post-expedition report for Req 8c. Beyond that, consider:

- **Presenting to your unit** — Share your findings at a troop meeting. Use photos, maps, and your data to tell the story.
- **Contributing to citizen science** — If your data is relevant to a citizen science project (like eBird, iNaturalist, or a local water quality monitoring program), submit it.
- **Creating a display** — A poster or display board summarizing your expedition is a great way to share with your school, community, or council.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="When Your Data Does Not Match Your Hypothesis" >}}
You predicted that water quality would be worse downstream of a development — but your measurements show no significant difference. This is not a failure. In science, a result that disproves a hypothesis is just as valuable as one that confirms it. Report what you found honestly, discuss why your prediction may have been wrong, and suggest what further research could clarify the picture.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}
