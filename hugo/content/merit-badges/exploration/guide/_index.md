In 1911, Roald Amundsen reached the South Pole after a grueling race across Antarctic ice. In 1969, Neil Armstrong stepped onto the Moon. In 2012, James Cameron descended solo to the deepest point in the ocean. And right now, somewhere in the world, a scientist is peering through a microscope at a cell no one has ever seen before. Exploration is not just about places on a map — it is about pushing into the unknown wherever it exists.

The Exploration merit badge challenges you to understand what real exploration looks like, plan your own expedition, and actually carry it out. You will not just read about explorers — you will become one.

## Then and Now

### Then — Charting the Unknown

For most of human history, exploration meant physically going where no one from your civilization had been. Polynesian navigators crossed thousands of miles of open Pacific Ocean using only the stars, ocean swells, and the flight patterns of birds. European explorers of the 15th and 16th centuries — driven by trade, conquest, and curiosity — mapped coastlines, discovered continents (from their perspective), and connected civilizations that had been isolated for millennia.

By the early 20th century, the great geographic prizes were being claimed: the North Pole (1909), the South Pole (1911), the summit of Everest (1953). Many people assumed there was nothing left to explore.

- **Purpose:** Map unknown territory, find trade routes, claim resources
- **Mindset:** Go where no one has gone, plant a flag, come home a hero

### Now — A New Age of Discovery

That assumption was spectacularly wrong. Today's explorers are discovering more than ever — just in different ways. Oceanographers have mapped less than 25% of the ocean floor in detail. Astrobiologists are searching for signs of life on Mars and the moons of Jupiter. Cave explorers are finding new species in underground rivers. Geneticists are sequencing DNA to trace human migration routes that no historical record captured.

Modern exploration blends physical adventure with rigorous science. An expedition might involve hiking through a jungle with machetes *and* analyzing soil samples with a portable DNA sequencer. The explorer's toolkit has expanded from compass and sextant to satellite phone and mass spectrometer — but the driving force is the same: the need to know what is out there.

- **Purpose:** Answer questions, test hypotheses, expand human knowledge
- **Mindset:** Curiosity-driven, evidence-based, collaborative, and ethical

## Kinds of Exploration

Exploration takes many forms. The environment, the tools, and the questions change — but the core process of venturing into the unknown to gather new knowledge stays the same.

### Geographic Exploration

This is what most people picture when they hear "exploration" — trekking through jungles, scaling mountains, or crossing deserts. Geographic explorers chart unmapped territory, document remote ecosystems, and sometimes make first contact with isolated communities. Even today, there are cave systems, remote islands, and mountain ranges that remain poorly documented.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Scientists estimate that there are still more than 10,000 undiscovered caves in the United States alone. In 2019, cave divers in Mexico's Yucatan Peninsula connected two massive underwater cave systems, creating the longest known underwater cave at over 215 miles.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

### Ocean Exploration

More than 80% of the ocean remains unexplored and unmapped. Ocean explorers use submersibles, remotely operated vehicles (ROVs), sonar mapping, and deep-sea diving to study hydrothermal vents, coral reefs, deep-sea trenches, and underwater archaeology. Every deep-sea expedition seems to discover species that science has never documented.

### Space Exploration

From the Apollo missions to the Mars rovers to the James Webb Space Telescope, space exploration pushes the boundaries of human technology and ambition. Astronauts, mission planners, and ground-based scientists all play roles in this field. Even amateur astronomers contribute to space exploration by discovering asteroids and monitoring variable stars.

### Scientific and Laboratory Exploration

Not all exploration happens in remote places. A biologist studying a new virus under a microscope is exploring. A chemist synthesizing a compound that has never existed is exploring. A paleontologist carefully brushing sediment from a fossil is exploring. The key ingredient is not distance — it is the pursuit of knowledge that no one has yet uncovered.

{{< drg/tip >}}
When choosing your expedition topic for this badge, think broadly. Your exploration does not have to involve wilderness travel. Investigating the water quality of a local stream, documenting the bird species in an urban park, or researching the geology of a nearby rock formation all count as genuine exploration.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Archaeological and Historical Exploration

Archaeologists explore the human past by excavating sites, analyzing artifacts, and using technologies like ground-penetrating radar and carbon dating. Historical exploration also includes documenting oral histories, tracing migration patterns, and uncovering stories that have been overlooked or forgotten.
