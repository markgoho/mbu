{{< drg/requirement number="8" >}}
Go on an Expedition. Complete the following:
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This is the culmination of everything you have worked toward. You planned it, prepared for it, and now you are doing it. This requirement covers three parts:

- **Req 8a** — Undertake your actual expedition
- **Req 8b** — Practice outdoor ethics throughout
- **Req 8c** — Write a post-expedition report

## 8a. Conducting Your Expedition

{{< drg/requirement number="8a" >}}
With your parent or guardian's permission and under the supervision of your counselor or a counselor-approved qualified person, use the planning steps you learned in requirement 6 and the preparations you completed in requirement 7 to personally undertake an actual expedition to an area you have not previously explored.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This is where your plan meets reality. Here are principles that will help your expedition day go smoothly.

### Stick to the Plan — Mostly

Your schedule, safety procedures, and objectives are your guide. Follow them. But also recognize that real expeditions require flexibility. If you discover something unexpected at your first site, it might be worth adjusting your schedule to spend more time there — as long as you still meet your safety requirements and departure time.

### Collect Data Methodically

Follow your documentation procedures from Req 6h. Record every observation in your field notebook with date, time, and location. Take photographs. Fill in your data sheets. The quality of your post-expedition report (Req 8c) depends entirely on the quality of your field records.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Designate one team member to call out "checkpoint" at regular intervals — every 30 minutes or at each new site. At each checkpoint, pause and ask: "Are we recording everything? Is anyone running low on water? How is our timeline?" These brief pauses catch problems before they become crises.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Stay Safe

Monitor conditions throughout the day. Is the weather changing? Is anyone showing signs of dehydration, heat illness, or fatigue? Is the terrain more challenging than expected? Your safety plan (Req 6f) is not just for emergencies — it is a continuous awareness practice.

### Enjoy It

Amid the data collection and safety checks, remember to look up and take in what you are experiencing. You are doing something that most people never do — systematically exploring a place to learn something new about the world. That is worth savoring.

## 8b. Outdoor Ethics

{{< drg/requirement number="8b" >}}
Discuss with your counselor about the vital role of outdoor ethics in exploration, explaining how they promote responsible and mindful adventuring. Explain how you applied the outdoor code, Leave No Trace and Tread Lightly! principles during your expedition.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Exploration has a troubled ethical history. Early explorers often damaged the places they studied — collecting so many specimens that populations declined, introducing invasive species, and destroying cultural sites. Modern exploration is built on the principle that the explorer's first obligation is to leave the place as good as — or better than — they found it.

### The Outdoor Code

As a Scout, you know the Outdoor Code: "As an American, I will do my best to be clean in my outdoor manners, be careful with fire, be considerate in the outdoors, and be conservation-minded."

For an explorer, being conservation-minded takes on special meaning. Your expedition takes you to places specifically *because* they are ecologically or scientifically valuable. Damaging them defeats the purpose.

### Leave No Trace Principles

Apply all seven Leave No Trace principles during your expedition:

1. **Plan ahead and prepare** — You have already done this through Requirements 6 and 7.
2. **Travel and camp on durable surfaces** — Stay on trails when possible. Set up your base on rock, gravel, or established sites.
3. **Dispose of waste properly** — Pack out all trash, including food scraps. If you collect water samples, dispose of unused chemicals properly.
4. **Leave what you find** — Unless your permit specifically allows specimen collection, leave natural objects in place. Photograph instead of collecting.
5. **Minimize campfire impacts** — If your expedition does not require a fire, do not build one.
6. **Respect wildlife** — Observe from a distance. Do not feed, touch, or disturb animals. This is especially important if you are conducting a wildlife survey.
7. **Be considerate of other visitors** — Keep noise levels down, yield the trail, and respect shared spaces.

### Tread Lightly! Principles

If your expedition involves motorized access (driving to a remote trailhead, for example), Tread Lightly! principles also apply:

- **T**ravel responsibly on designated roads and trails
- **R**espect the environment and the rights of others
- **E**ducate yourself about rules and regulations
- **A**void sensitive areas (wetlands, nesting sites, archaeological sites)
- **D**o your part by minimizing your impact

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
In 1909, Robert Peary claimed to reach the North Pole — and left behind a trail of supply caches, discarded equipment, and debris across the Arctic ice. Today, exploration organizations require expeditions to remove all waste from their routes. The ethos has completely reversed: modern explorers are expected to leave *no trace* of their passage.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## 8c. Writing Your Post-Expedition Report

{{< drg/requirement number="8c" >}}
After you return, compile a report on the results of your expedition and how you accomplished your objective(s). Include a statement of the objectives, note your findings and observations, include photos, note any discoveries, report any problems or adverse events, and have a conclusion (whether you reached your objective or not). The post-expedition report must be at least one page and no more than three; one page can be photos, graphs, or figures.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Your post-expedition report is the final product of your exploration. It transforms your raw field data into a coherent narrative that answers the question: *What did you set out to learn, and what did you actually learn?*

### Report Structure

{{< drg/checklist title="Post-Expedition Report Outline" subtitle="Include each section in your 1–3 page report" >}}
- **Expedition title and date.**
- **Team members and their roles.**
- **Statement of objectives:** What were your primary and secondary goals?
- **Methods:** Briefly describe how you collected data (survey method, equipment used, sites visited).
- **Findings and observations:** What did you discover? Present your data clearly — use tables, lists, or descriptions.
- **Photos:** Include at least one page of photos with captions explaining what each shows.
- **Discoveries:** Did you find anything unexpected? Something not previously documented?
- **Problems or adverse events:** Be honest. What went wrong? Weather delays, equipment failure, a site that was inaccessible?
- **Conclusion:** Did you achieve your objective? What would you do differently? What questions remain unanswered?
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

### Writing Tips

- **Be specific.** "We saw many birds" is weak. "We identified 14 bird species across three habitat types, including an unexpected sighting of a Pileated Woodpecker in the riparian zone" is strong.
- **Be honest.** If you did not fully achieve your objective, say so — and explain why. Professional scientists report negative results routinely.
- **Let photos tell part of the story.** A well-captioned photo page is often the most compelling part of a report.
- **Stay within the page limit.** The requirement says 1–3 pages, with one page allowed for photos. Be concise.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="What If Your Expedition Did Not Go as Planned?" >}}
Your expedition was rained out. Your primary site was closed. You found fewer specimens than expected. Does that mean you failed?

Not at all. In real exploration, plans change and hypotheses are disproven all the time. Your report should honestly describe what happened, what you learned from the experience, and what you would change for a future expedition. A well-written report about a "failed" expedition is far more impressive than a fabricated success story.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}
