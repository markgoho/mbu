{{< drg/requirement number="9d" >}}
Meet with a Fire Prevention Specialist or a local fire official to learn about fire prevention efforts in your community. From what you learned, prepare a fire prevention message (skit, video, or billboard concept) and share with your counselor and troop (or other group approved by your counselor).
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Smokey Bear has been running since 1944 and is recognized by the overwhelming majority of American adults. That is what a prevention message can do when it works — and it is worth remembering that it started as a poster, not a budget.

This option has two halves that need to stay connected: **learn what prevention actually looks like in your community**, then **build a message from what you learned.** A message invented from general knowledge misses the point. A message built on the specific thing your local fire official told you is the assignment.

## Part One: Meet the Prevention Professional

Who to look for:

- A **Fire Prevention Specialist** or **Fire Prevention Officer** at a state forestry agency or federal unit — this is a real job title, and these are the people who run the local Smokey Bear program.
- A **fire marshal**, **fire inspector**, or **public education officer** at your local fire department.
- A **Firewise coordinator** or community risk reduction specialist.
- The **county fire warden**, who typically issues burn permits and knows exactly which behaviors cause fires locally.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Set this meeting up with your parent or guardian's permission and your counselor's knowledge, and follow Scouting America's Youth Protection policies — two-deep leadership and no one-on-one contact with an adult outside Scouting. Meet at the station or agency office with another adult present.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

What to ask, aimed squarely at building a message afterward:

- **What causes most of the preventable fires here?** This is the single most important question, and it determines your message's subject.
- **Who causes them?** Residents burning debris, visitors with campfires, contractors with equipment, drivers, teenagers? Your audience follows from the answer.
- **What prevention programs do you run, and which one works best?** Ask what they have tried that failed, too — that answer is usually the most useful one you get.
- **What message do you wish the public actually understood?** Prevention professionals almost always have a specific frustration.
- **When is your highest-risk period,** and how do you communicate restrictions?
- **What materials do you already have** that a Scout could use or adapt? Many agencies have free posters, handouts, and Smokey Bear materials.

## Part Two: Build the Message

You have three formats to choose from, and the requirement lets you pick.

### Skit

Best for a live troop meeting or campfire program, and the easiest to produce well with a patrol.

- Keep it to three to five minutes. Prevention skits die from length.
- Build it on one clear behavior — checking that the fire is cold, not just out; parking off the grass; getting the permit before burning.
- Use humor for the setup and land the point straight. A skit that is only funny gets remembered as funny; a skit that is only serious does not get remembered.
- Show the wrong way *and* the right way, in that order.
- End with one sentence the audience can repeat back.

### Video

Best for reaching people beyond the room, and for a Scout who wants something reusable.

- Aim for 60 to 90 seconds. Shorter is harder and better.
- Storyboard before you shoot. A shot list saves hours.
- Shoot in real locations — a campsite, a driveway, a gutter full of needles — because authenticity is your production budget.
- Record clean audio. Bad audio kills more student videos than bad video does.
- Get permission before filming people or property, and never film in a way that identifies a Scout without their family's consent.
- End with the specific action, on screen, in text.

### Billboard concept

Best for a Scout who thinks visually, and the format that forces the most discipline.

- **Seven words maximum.** A driver has about four seconds.
- One image that reads instantly at a distance.
- Extremely high contrast — dark text on light, or the reverse.
- One idea. Not three.
- Present it as a full mockup with a short written rationale: who the audience is, where the billboard would go, why the wording works, and what you want the driver to do differently.

## What Makes a Prevention Message Actually Work

{{< drg/checklist title="Test Your Message Against These" subtitle="Prevention campaigns that fail usually fail one of these" >}}

- **One specific behavior.** "Be safe with fire" changes nothing. "Drown it, stir it, feel it — then leave" changes something.
- **The right audience.** A message about debris burning aimed at Scouts is wasted; aim it at the adults who burn brush piles.
- **A reason, briefly.** People comply better when they know why, but the reason has to fit in a breath.
- **Something they can do today.** Immediate, concrete, and free beats important and vague.
- **Memorable, not preachy.** Lecturing makes people defensive. Humor, surprise, and a striking image do not.
- **Locally true.** If your area's problem is roadside equipment sparks, a campfire message is beautiful and useless.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Smokey Bear's slogan was changed in 2001 from "Only YOU can prevent forest fires" to "Only you can prevent wildfires" — a two-word edit made for two serious reasons. "Forest" left out the grass, brush, and rangeland where a huge share of fires burn, and the original phrasing implied all forest fire was bad, which undercut the case for the prescribed burning you studied in [Req 3](/merit-badges/wildland-fire-management/guide/req3/). Even the most successful public safety campaign in American history had to be revised when the science moved.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Part Three: Deliver It

The requirement says share it with your counselor **and** your troop or another approved group — so plan the delivery, not just the production.

- **Get on the agenda.** Ask your senior patrol leader for a slot at a troop meeting, or your Scoutmaster about a court of honor.
- **Consider a bigger audience.** A Cub Scout pack meeting, a school assembly, a community group, a camporee, or your council's summer camp opening campfire all work with your counselor's approval — and Cub Scouts are a genuinely excellent audience for fire safety.
- **Practice it.** Once, out loud, all the way through, before the real thing.
- **Ask for feedback afterward.** Did anyone repeat the message back correctly? That is your only real measure of success.
- **Offer it to the professional you interviewed.** Agencies are frequently short on materials, and a good Scout-produced video or poster sometimes gets used.

{{< drg/tip >}}
The strongest prevention messages Scouts produce almost always come from one concrete detail heard in the interview — the fire warden who says half their calls are backyard burn piles on windy days, or the ranger who says people bury campfires with dirt thinking that puts them out. Take good notes and listen for the sentence that makes you go "wait, really?" That is your message.
{{< /drg/tip >}}
