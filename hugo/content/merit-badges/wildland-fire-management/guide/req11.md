Careers in Wildland Fire Management. Explore careers related to this merit badge. Research one career to learn about the training and education needed, costs, job prospects, salary, job duties, and career advancement. Your research methods may include-with your parent or guardian's permission-an internet or library search, an interview with a professional in the field, or a visit to a location where people in this career work. Discuss with your counselor both your findings and what about this profession might make it an interesting career.

Wildland fire is unusual among career fields in that almost everyone in it, at every level, started in the same place: on a crew, holding a tool. The fire behavior analyst who forecasts a run, the district fire management officer who signs a burn plan, the Type 1 Incident Commander running a 3,000-person incident — most of them dug line for their first several seasons. That means the entry point is unusually clear, and the ceiling is unusually high.

It is also a field with a genuine, publicly acknowledged staffing problem, which is worth knowing when you evaluate job prospects.

## The Range of Careers

Do not assume "wildland fire career" means only firefighting. The field is much wider than that.

### On the fireline

- **Wildland firefighter** on an engine crew, a hand crew, or a fuels crew. The entry point for nearly everything else.
- **Interagency hotshot crew member.** Elite 20-person crews assigned to the most demanding line assignments, with a rigorous physical standard.
- **Smokejumper.** Parachutes to remote fires. Requires several seasons of experience before you can even apply.
- **Helitack crew member and rappeller.** Arrives by helicopter, often first on scene in the backcountry.
- **Engine captain, crew boss, superintendent.** Supervisory positions built on years of line experience and a stack of qualifications.
- **Wildland fire module member.** Specialized crews focused on prescribed fire and managed natural fire.

### Fire management and planning

- **Fire Management Officer (FMO)** at a district, forest, park, or refuge — responsible for the whole fire program.
- **Fuels specialist / prescribed fire burn boss.** Plans and executes the burns you studied in [Req 3](/merit-badges/wildland-fire-management/guide/req3/).
- **Fire prevention specialist / fire warden.** Public education, permits, inspections, and investigation of cause.
- **Fire investigator.** Determines origin and cause, and builds the case when a fire is criminal.
- **Incident management team positions** across all the sections you diagrammed in [Req 7b](/merit-badges/wildland-fire-management/guide/req7b/) — operations, planning, logistics, finance, information, and safety.
- **Dispatcher** at an interagency dispatch center or coordination center, moving resources across the country.

### Science and technical specialties

- **Fire behavior analyst (FBAN)** and **long-term analyst (LTAN).** Model and predict what the fire will do.
- **Incident Meteorologist (IMET).** An NWS forecaster who deploys to the fire and forecasts from the incident.
- **Fire ecologist.** Studies fire regimes, effects, and restoration.
- **GIS specialist and remote sensing analyst.** Maps fires, models risk, and produces the products everyone else plans from.
- **Hydrologist and soil scientist** on Burned Area Emergency Response teams.
- **Wildlife biologist and botanist** working on fire effects and post-fire recovery.
- **Fire researcher** at a Forest Service research station, the Missoula Fire Sciences Laboratory, or a university.

### Aviation

- **Air tanker pilot, helicopter pilot, lead plane pilot.** Extensive flight hours required.
- **Air Tactical Group Supervisor.** Coordinates aviation from the orbiting platform.
- **Helitack manager, aviation maintenance technician, air base manager.**

### Support and adjacent fields

- **Equipment operator** (dozer, tractor-plow, masticator), **mechanic**, **supply and logistics specialist**, **contracting officer**.
- **Public information officer** and community risk reduction specialist.
- **Insurance risk analyst, utility vegetation manager, wildfire mitigation consultant** — a fast-growing private-sector area.
- **Emergency manager** at county or state level.

## Researching Your One Career

The requirement asks you to go deep on **one**. Cover all six of the following.

### Training and education needed

- **Entry-level firefighting** generally requires being 18, a high school diploma or equivalent, and completion of the basic wildland fire training sequence — commonly S-130 (Firefighter Training), S-190 (Introduction to Wildland Fire Behavior), L-180 (Human Factors), and I-100 (Introduction to ICS) — plus passing the **work capacity test**. The Arduous level is a 3-mile hike carrying a 45-pound pack in 45 minutes or less.
- **Annual refresher** training (RT-130) and an annual fitness test are required to keep working.
- **Advancement** runs through the NWCG qualification system, where each position has a **position task book** that must be completed under evaluation on real incidents. This is the part most people do not know about: the profession's credentialing happens through documented performance, not just coursework.
- **Technical and science careers** typically require a bachelor's degree — forestry, fire science, natural resources, ecology, atmospheric science, or geography — and some require a master's or doctorate.
- **Pilots** need commercial certification and substantial flight hours, often thousands, with mountain and low-level experience.

### Costs

Look up actual numbers for the path you choose: tuition at a state university versus a community college fire science program, certificate program costs, and required personal equipment. Note that basic wildland training is frequently provided free by the hiring agency or offered inexpensively through state programs and community colleges — this field has a much lower barrier to entry than most.

### Job prospects

Be specific rather than optimistic. Look at the seasonal-versus-permanent split, which is the defining feature of the field. Many people work several seasons on temporary appointments before landing permanent work. Federal wildland fire hiring has undergone significant changes in recent years, including efforts to convert seasonal positions to permanent and to address recruitment and retention problems. State agencies, counties, tribal programs, and a growing private contracting sector all hire as well. Note also that longer fire seasons have increased demand while making the work harder to sustain.

### Salary

Use real sources: USAJOBS listings show federal pay grades and locality adjustments directly, and the Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes occupational data. Understand the structure — base pay plus overtime and hazard pay means fireline earnings during a busy season can be very different from base salary, while a quiet season means much less. Federal wildland firefighter pay has been the subject of significant legislative attention, so check current information rather than older figures.

### Job duties

Go past the obvious. What does a typical *day* look like in and out of fire season? What is the shift structure — 16-hour days, 14-day assignments, weeks away from home? What does the off-season involve: training, project work, equipment maintenance, layoff? What are the physical demands, and what are the health considerations, including smoke exposure, injury risk, and cumulative fatigue?

### Career advancement

Sketch the ladder: firefighter to squad boss to captain or crew boss to superintendent or FMO, or the parallel track into planning, prevention, aviation, or science. Note how mobility works — advancing in the federal system often means moving geographically — and how incident management team qualifications build a second career track on top of the day job.

{{< drg/checklist title="Research Sources Worth Using" subtitle="Go beyond a single web search" >}}

- **USAJOBS** — real federal wildland fire announcements with actual duties, qualifications, and pay.
- **Your state forestry agency's careers page** — state pathways are often easier to enter than federal ones.
- **Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook** — salary and outlook data for firefighting and forestry occupations.
- **NWCG** — the qualification system, position descriptions, and training course catalog.
- **An interview.** If you took [Req 9b](/merit-badges/wildland-fire-management/guide/req9b/), [9d](/merit-badges/wildland-fire-management/guide/req9d/), or [9e](/merit-badges/wildland-fire-management/guide/req9e/), you already met someone who can answer these questions — and their answers will be better than anything online.
- **Community college fire science programs** near you, which publish costs and course sequences.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The federal wildland fire workforce is dominated by seasonal employment in a way almost no other emergency service is. Many firefighters work six months on the line and spend the off-season in school, in a completely different job, or on unemployment — and a large share of the people who fight America's fires each summer are in their first three years of the work. Recent federal efforts to convert temporary positions to permanent are a direct response to how hard that pattern makes it to keep experienced people.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

{{< drg/be-prepared title="If You Want to Pursue This" >}}
There are real steps a Scout can take now, well before turning 18.

- **Get and stay fit.** The Arduous work capacity test is a real barrier. Hiking with a weighted pack is the most specific training there is, and you are already doing a version of it.
- **Earn related merit badges.** [Forestry](/merit-badges/forestry/), [Fire Safety](/merit-badges/fire-safety/), [Emergency Preparedness](/merit-badges/emergency-preparedness/), [Environmental Science](/merit-badges/environmental-science/), and [Weather](/merit-badges/weather/) all connect directly to this work.
- **Get first aid and CPR certified,** and consider EMT training once you are old enough. Medical qualifications make you significantly more employable.
- **Volunteer with a local fire department.** Many accept explorers, cadets, or junior members starting in the mid-teens.
- **Look for youth conservation programs.** Youth Conservation Corps, state conservation corps, and AmeriCorps crews do fuels and trail work and are common first steps into agency employment.
- **Take the online ICS courses.** FEMA's IS-100 and IS-700 are free, self-paced, and produce real certificates you can put on an application.
- **Talk to people already doing it.** Almost everyone in this field is willing to talk to a young person who is genuinely interested.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

{{< drg/tip >}}
Your counselor will ask what makes this profession interesting to *you*, so have an honest answer ready — and honest includes the hard parts. The people who stay in wildland fire talk about the crew, the country they get to work in, the immediacy of the work, and the fact that the job matters visibly. The people who leave talk about the pay, the time away from family, the injuries, and the smoke. A Scout who can discuss both sides shows they actually researched the career instead of the poster version of it.
