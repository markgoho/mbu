History of Wildland Fire. Research and discuss with your counselor the history of wildland fire, its suppression, prevention, and management in the United States.

On the morning of August 21, 1910, a Forest Service ranger named Ed Pulaski led 45 firefighters into a mine tunnel on the Coeur d'Alene River while a firestorm roared over the top of them. He held the men inside at gunpoint when panic set in, soaked blankets to hang across the entrance, and passed out from the smoke. Forty-one of them walked out alive. Pulaski later invented the combination axe-and-hoe tool that every wildland firefighter in America still carries — and still calls a **pulaski**.

That is how tangled this history is. The tools in your hands, the words on the radio, and the policies that decide whether a fire burns or gets attacked all trace back to specific fires, specific disasters, and specific arguments. Requirement 1 asks you to research that story and talk it through with your counselor, so this page gives you the spine of the narrative and the vocabulary to discuss it.

## Fire Before Fire Policy

Long before there were agencies, there was fire — and it was not rare. Lightning ignites tens of thousands of fires in North America every year, and Indigenous peoples across the continent added many more on purpose. They burned prairie to draw bison and elk into fresh grass, burned oak woodlands to improve acorn harvests and knock down insect pests, burned understory brush to make travel and hunting easier, and burned protective rings around villages. Anthropologists and fire scientists refer to this as **cultural burning**, and in many landscapes it shaped the vegetation European settlers later described as "natural."

Fire scars preserved in tree rings let scientists reconstruct these cycles precisely. A ponderosa pine forest in Arizona might show a light surface fire every 4 to 12 years for four hundred straight years — and then nothing after about 1880, when livestock grazing removed the grass that carried those fires and organized fire suppression began.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Some tree species cannot reproduce well without fire. Longleaf pine seedlings need bare mineral soil and full sunlight, which frequent low-intensity fire provides; without fire, hardwood competitors shade them out. Lodgepole pine and jack pine go further — many of their cones are sealed with resin and only open after being heated by flames.

## The Turning Points

Work through this timeline before your counselor conversation. You do not need to memorize dates, but you should be able to explain *why* each item changed what came after it.

| Year | Event | Why it mattered |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1871 | Peshtigo Fire, Wisconsin | Killed roughly 1,200–2,500 people, the deadliest wildfire in U.S. history, and revealed how fast fire moves through settled country |
| 1905 | U.S. Forest Service created | Gave the nation a professional agency responsible for fire on federal forests |
| 1910 | The Big Blowup | About 3 million acres and at least 85 deaths in two days; hardened the agency's commitment to total suppression |
| 1911 | Weeks Act | Let the federal government buy eastern forest land and fund cooperative state fire protection — the beginning of federal-state fire partnership |
| 1933–1942 | Civilian Conservation Corps | Built lookouts, roads, and trails and supplied a standing firefighting workforce of hundreds of thousands |
| 1935 | The 10 a.m. policy | Formalized the goal of controlling every fire by 10 a.m. the day after report |
| 1940 | First smokejumper fire jump | Made remote fires reachable within hours |
| 1944 | Smokey Bear debuts | Launched the longest-running public service advertising campaign in U.S. history |
| 1949 | Mann Gulch, Montana | Thirteen smokejumpers died; triggered serious study of fire behavior and firefighter safety |
| 1957 | 10 Standard Firefighting Orders written | A task force reviewed 16 tragedy fires and produced the safety rules still taught today |
| 1963 | The Leopold Report | Argued national parks should restore natural processes, including fire |
| 1968 | National Park Service policy change | First federal agency to allow some natural fires to burn as **prescribed natural fire** |
| 1978 | Forest Service replaces the 10 a.m. policy | Shifted the mission from fire *control* to fire *management* |
| 1988 | Yellowstone fires | About 1.2 million acres burned inside and around the park; forced a national reckoning over letting fires burn |
| 1994 | South Canyon Fire, Colorado | Fourteen firefighters died on Storm King Mountain; produced major changes in safety doctrine and the adoption of LCES |
| 1995 | Federal Wildland Fire Management Policy | First policy shared by all federal land agencies; recognized fire as an essential ecological process |
| 2000 | National Fire Plan | Followed the escaped Cerro Grande prescribed burn at Los Alamos; funded fuels reduction and community protection at scale |
| 2003 | Healthy Forests Restoration Act | Streamlined hazardous fuels projects, especially near communities |
| 2009 | FLAME Act | Created a dedicated reserve fund so suppression spending would stop draining other agency budgets |
| 2014 | National Cohesive Wildland Fire Management Strategy | Organized national effort around three goals: resilient landscapes, fire-adapted communities, and safe, effective response |
| 2018 | Camp Fire, California | Destroyed the town of Paradise and killed 85 people; made the wildland-urban interface the central problem in American fire |
| 2022 | Wildfire Crisis Strategy | Committed to treating tens of millions of acres of high-risk fuels across the West over a decade |
| 2023 | Lahaina Fire, Hawaii | Over 100 deaths in a wind-driven fire in non-forest fuels, showing wildfire risk is not limited to western forests |

## The Three Threads to Untangle

The requirement names four things — fire, suppression, prevention, and management — and your discussion will go much better if you can keep the last three separate in your head.

### Suppression: Getting Better at Stopping Fire

Suppression history is a story of speed and reach. Lookout towers and telephone lines in the 1910s meant a fire could be reported in an hour instead of a day. The CCC put crews and roads everywhere in the 1930s. Smokejumpers arrived in 1940, aerial retardant drops in the 1950s, helicopters in the 1960s, and infrared mapping and satellite detection later still. Along the way, suppression got safer and more disciplined, largely because of tragedies: Mann Gulch produced the study of fire behavior, the 1957 review produced the 10 Standard Firefighting Orders, and South Canyon produced the LCES framework you will study in [Req 6b](/merit-badges/wildland-fire-management/guide/req6b/).

### Prevention: Changing Human Behavior

Prevention history is mostly a story of communication. The wartime worry that saboteurs or careless citizens might destroy needed timber produced slogans, posters, and eventually a bear in a ranger hat. Smokey Bear's original line, "Only YOU can prevent forest fires," was updated in 2001 to "Only you can prevent wildfires" — partly because the old wording implied that all forest fire was bad and ignored the value of prescribed fire. Modern prevention adds burn permits, equipment spark-arrestor rules, campfire restrictions during high fire danger, roadside patrols during red flag conditions, and community programs.

### Management: Deciding What Fire Should Do

Management history is the most interesting thread, because it is a story of the country changing its mind. Between 1935 and 1978 the answer to "what should fire do?" was "nothing." Then ecologists, foresters in the Southeast who had never stopped burning, and park scientists pushed the idea that the goal is the right fire in the right place at the right intensity. The 1988 Yellowstone fires nearly killed that idea in public opinion; the enormous fires of the 2000s and 2010s revived it, because it became clear that a century of stored-up fuel had made the alternative worse.

## Bringing Your Own Region Into It

National history is only half of a good discussion. Fire history is intensely local, and your counselor will be far more impressed if you can connect the timeline above to the ground you actually camp on.

{{< drg/checklist title="Research Your Local Fire History" subtitle="Three questions to answer before your counselor conversation" >}}

- **What is the fire history of your area?** Search your state forestry agency, state historical society, or a nearby national forest or park for the largest recorded fires near you and what caused them.
- **Who has jurisdiction where you camp?** Federal, state, county, tribal, and local fire departments all have responsibility somewhere. Knowing which agency covers your Scout camp is genuinely useful information.
- **Has fire policy in your state changed recently?** Many states have revised burn permit rules, defensible space laws, or utility line requirements in the last few years, usually after a specific fire.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/tip >}}
Pick one fire from the timeline and go deep on it rather than skimming all twenty. A Scout who can explain exactly what the 1994 South Canyon Fire changed about firefighter safety has a much better conversation than one who can recite dates. If you already know which historic fire you want to use for [Req 10](/merit-badges/wildland-fire-management/guide/req10/), reading up on it now does double duty.
