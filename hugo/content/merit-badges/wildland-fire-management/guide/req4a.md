Requirement 4 splits the "keep it from being a disaster" half of fire management into two jobs that sound similar and are not. **Prevention** stops ignitions — it works on the match, the spark, the chain saw, the tailpipe. **Mitigation**, which you will study on the [next page](/merit-badges/wildland-fire-management/guide/req4b/), assumes a fire will happen anyway and reduces what it can destroy.

This page covers prevention, in two parts:

- **4a1** — the main causes of wildfire in the United States
- **4a2** — what *you* can do about human-caused fire, at home and on outings

Prevention is the least glamorous part of wildland fire management and by far the best deal. A campfire that never escapes costs nothing. The same fire, escaped and grown, can cost tens of millions of dollars and put hundreds of firefighters in harm's way.

## Requirement 4a1: The Main Causes of Wildfire in the United States

{{< drg/inherited-requirement number="4a1" req_path="4.a.1" topic="The main causes of wildfire in the United States" />}}

Start with the single most important fact in this entire requirement: **the large majority of wildfires in the United States are started by people.** Depending on the year and how the count is done, humans cause roughly 85 to 90 percent of ignitions. Lightning causes the rest — but because lightning fires often start in remote, rugged, unroaded country and arrive in clusters during dry thunderstorm outbreaks, they account for a disproportionate share of total acres burned.

Fire investigators sort causes into standard categories, and knowing them is what lets you have a real conversation about prevention.

### Human causes

- **Debris and open burning.** Burning leaves, brush piles, trash, or fields is consistently one of the largest single causes nationally. Fires escape when wind picks up, when nobody stays with the pile, when the pile is too close to vegetation, or when material thought to be out reignites hours later.
- **Equipment use and machinery.** Chain saws, brush cutters, mowers hitting rocks, welding and grinding, off-road vehicle exhaust, and dragging trailer safety chains all throw sparks. In cured grass in the afternoon, one spark is enough.
- **Campfires.** Fires that were not fully extinguished, were built in windy conditions, were too close to overhanging branches or dry duff, or were built during a fire restriction. This is the category Scouts control directly.
- **Arson and intentional ignition.** A significant share of ignitions in some regions, and a criminal offense with serious penalties.
- **Vehicles.** Catalytic converters and exhaust systems parked or driving over dry grass, brake and wheel-bearing failures, and dragging metal on pavement.
- **Power lines and utility equipment.** Lines contacting vegetation or each other, or failing hardware during wind events. Several of the most destructive fires in recent U.S. history began this way, which is why utilities now run public safety power shutoffs during extreme conditions.
- **Railroads.** Sparks from wheels and brakes, and hot material dropped along the right-of-way.
- **Smoking.** Discarded cigarettes, especially thrown from vehicles into roadside grass.
- **Fireworks.** Concentrated around holidays, and the reason so many communities ban them outright during high fire danger.
- **Children and unattended youth ignitions.** Tracked as its own category and addressed largely through education.
- **Escaped prescribed fire.** Rare relative to the number of burns conducted, but it happens, and honesty about it is part of understanding the field.

### Natural causes

- **Lightning.** By far the dominant natural cause. Especially important are **dry thunderstorms**, where rain evaporates before reaching the ground and leaves only the strike. Some lightning fires also **hold over** — smoldering in a snag or in duff for days before conditions let them grow.
- **Other natural ignitions.** Rockfall sparks, spontaneous combustion in accumulated organic material, and volcanic activity all occur, but they are statistically minor.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Because lightning fires favor remote high country while human fires cluster along roads and near homes, the two cause groups create almost opposite problems. Human ignitions are more numerous and far more likely to threaten structures; lightning ignitions are harder to reach, more likely to arrive many at once, and more likely to grow large. A single dry lightning outbreak can start hundreds of fires across a region in one night and exhaust every available crew.

### Finding the numbers for your own state

National percentages are a starting point, but cause patterns are regional. Debris burning dominates in parts of the Southeast; lightning dominates in the Interior West and Alaska; equipment and vehicle starts spike along highways in the Great Plains. Your state forestry agency publishes cause breakdowns, and bringing your state's top three causes to your counselor is a much stronger answer than reciting national figures.

## Requirement 4a2: Preventing Human-Caused Wildfires at Home and on Outings

{{< drg/inherited-requirement number="4a2" req_path="4.a.2" topic="How you can prevent human caused wildfires in your community and on outings with your unit." />}}

Here is the part of the badge where you have actual authority. You will build fires, cook on stoves, use tools, and camp in dry country for the rest of your Scouting career, and the habits you set now decide whether one of those becomes an ignition.

### Before the outing

- **Check fire danger and restrictions for your exact destination.** Fire restrictions are issued by land management unit, not by state, and they change week to week. Look up the managing agency's current stage of restrictions before you leave, and check whether campfires, charcoal, or even stoves are allowed.
- **Get the permit if one is required.** Many national forests require a free campfire permit even for a stove.
- **Plan a no-fire option.** If restrictions land while you are en route, you should already own a plan that works without an open flame. Stoves, cold lunches, and headlamps instead of firelight are not a downgrade — they are just a different trip.
- **Inspect your equipment.** Check that stoves are in good condition and fuel bottles do not leak, that chain saws and generators have functioning spark arresters, and that trailer safety chains are secured off the pavement.

### Campfires done right

- **Use an existing fire ring** wherever one exists. Building new rings multiplies scars and puts fire where nobody planned for it.
- **Clear the area.** Bare mineral soil for several feet around the ring, no overhanging branches, no roots or duff underneath. Duff will carry fire sideways underground where you cannot see it.
- **Keep it small.** A cooking fire needs to be smaller than most Scouts build. Big flames throw embers.
- **Never leave it unattended, and stop if the wind comes up.** Wind is the single condition that turns a contained fire into an escape.
- **Have water and a tool at the ring** every time — a filled bucket and a shovel or trowel, staged before the fire is lit.
- **Drown, stir, and feel.** Pour water, stir the ashes and coals with a shovel, scrape the sticks, pour again, and then hold the back of your hand near the ash. If it is warm, it is not out. Repeat until it is cold. "Cold to the touch" is the standard, and it is the step most often skipped.
- **Pack out unburned trash.** Foil and food scraps do not burn away and invite wildlife.

### Everything else on the trip

- **Park on gravel, dirt, or pavement,** never in tall dry grass where a hot exhaust system sits inches from fuel.
- **Refuel stoves cold, away from any flame,** and never inside or beside a tent.
- **Keep fuel and lanterns out of tents,** and use battery lanterns inside them.
- **Be careful with anything that heats or sparks** — that includes charcoal, tiki torches, fireworks (which have no place at a Scout activity), and target shooting with steel targets or steel-core ammunition.
- **Report smoke immediately.** If you see a column, note the location as precisely as you can, note the time, and call 911 or the agency dispatch number. Early reporting is one of the highest-value things a hiker can do.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="You Find an Abandoned, Still-Warm Campfire" >}}
This happens often, and how you respond matters more than you might think.

- **Assess safety first.** If it is already spreading beyond the ring, do not fight it. Move to a safe location and call 911 with your location.
- **If it is contained but hot, put it out properly.** Douse with water, stir the coals and ash together, scrape any burning sticks, and douse again.
- **Do not simply bury it.** Covering coals with soil insulates them; they can smolder for days and reemerge.
- **Check it with the back of your hand** before you leave, and check the ground around it for hot spots in the duff.
- **Report it** to the campground host, ranger, or land management agency, including the location. Repeated abandoned fires at a site tell managers where to focus patrols.

### Taking it to your community

Prevention scales when you teach it. Real options a Scout can actually pull off include teaching campfire safety to a Cub Scout pack or a new-Scout patrol, presenting a fire danger briefing at a troop meeting before summer camp, helping a neighbor clear needles from gutters and roof valleys, posting your council camp's fire restriction stage where everyone can see it, or serving as your troop's designated "fire out" checker on every campout. If you choose [Req 9d](/merit-badges/wildland-fire-management/guide/req9d/), you will build a prevention message of your own and deliver it.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Smokey Bear's message was updated from "Only YOU can prevent forest fires" to "Only you can prevent wildfires" in 2001. The change was deliberate: the old wording implied every forest fire was bad, which undercut the case for prescribed burning, and it left out grass, brush, and rangeland fires. If your counselor asks why the slogan changed, that connection between prevention messaging and fire ecology is a strong answer.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

Prevention stops ignitions, but no prevention program stops all of them — lightning alone guarantees that. So the second half of Requirement 4 asks a different question: when fire does come, how do we make sure a community survives it?
