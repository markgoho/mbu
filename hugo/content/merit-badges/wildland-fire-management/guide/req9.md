{{< drg/requirement number="9" >}}
Taking Action. Do ONE of the following:
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Everything up to this point has been study. Requirement 9 is where you put it to work, and **you choose exactly one** of five very different paths. Two are planning projects, two are conversations with professionals, and one is hands-on work on a real building.

Read all five before you decide. The right choice depends on what is actually available where you live, how much time you have, and honestly, what you want out of this badge.

## Your Options

**[Req 9a — Build a Fire Readiness Plan](/merit-badges/wildland-fire-management/guide/req9a/):** Develop a complete readiness plan for a wildland area you know well — a mapped plan covering resources, water supplies, barriers, access, fuel loads, apparatus, structure protection, values at risk, medical and evacuation considerations, and likely tactics. This is the most demanding option and the one that uses the most of what you learned in Requirements 5, 6, and 7.

**[Req 9b — Talk With a Forestry Official](/merit-badges/wildland-fire-management/guide/req9b/):** Meet with a state or federal forestry official or your local fire warden. Discuss local fire causes and types, your area's fire season, and which agency is responsible for wildland fire suppression where you live. Then discuss what you learned with your counselor.

**[Req 9c — Firewise Assessment & Project](/merit-badges/wildland-fire-management/guide/req9c/):** Assess a home or building in the wildland-urban interface — a Scout camp building counts — identify its risks, complete an approved project that reduces those risks, and write a brief report. The only option that leaves physical work behind you.

**[Req 9d — Create a Prevention Message](/merit-badges/wildland-fire-management/guide/req9d/):** Meet with a fire prevention specialist or local fire official, then build and deliver a prevention message as a skit, video, or billboard concept to your troop or another approved group. The most creative option, and the one that reaches the most people.

**[Req 9e — Talk Fire Weather](/merit-badges/wildland-fire-management/guide/req9e/):** Meet with a meteorologist or someone who works with fire weather. Discuss local fire weather concerns and typical fire seasons, and dig into what triggers a Fire Weather Watch versus a Red Flag Warning. The most scientific option.

## How to Choose

{{< drg/checklist title="Choosing Your Option" subtitle="Weigh these before you commit" >}}

- **Time required:** 9b and 9e are a single well-prepared meeting plus a follow-up discussion — a few hours total. 9d needs a meeting plus creative production and a delivery date. 9a is a multi-session research and mapping project. 9c is the longest, since it needs an assessment, counselor approval, the actual work, and a written report.
- **Who you need to find:** 9a and 9d benefit from a professional but 9a can be done independently. 9b requires a forestry official or fire warden. 9d requires a prevention specialist or fire official. 9e requires a meteorologist or fire weather specialist — check your nearest National Weather Service office, a local TV meteorologist, or a state fire weather program. If you live somewhere with no wildland fire agency nearby, 9a or 9d will be easier to arrange.
- **Where you will work:** 9a is map and research work you can do at a table. 9c puts you outside at a real structure with tools. 9b, 9d, and 9e happen wherever the professional works, plus wherever you present.
- **What you already have:** If you did a thorough job on the diagram for [Req 5c](/merit-badges/wildland-fire-management/guide/req5/) and the tactics demonstration in [Req 7a](/merit-badges/wildland-fire-management/guide/req7a/), you have a substantial head start on 9a. If [Req 4b](/merit-badges/wildland-fire-management/guide/req4b/) grabbed your attention, 9c is the natural continuation. If [Req 5a](/merit-badges/wildland-fire-management/guide/req5/) was your favorite page, take 9e.
- **What you will gain:** 9a builds real planning and mapping skill and forces you to integrate the whole badge. 9b gives you a professional contact and a genuine understanding of who protects your area. 9c leaves a building measurably safer and gives you a service project you can point to. 9d builds public speaking and persuasion skills and multiplies your impact across a whole unit. 9e teaches you to read fire weather products you will use every fire season for the rest of your life.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/tip >}}
If you are unsure, here is a practical recommendation. Scouts who want the badge to feel like real fire management work should take **9a** — it is the deepest and most satisfying. Scouts who are short on time but want a meaningful experience should take **9b**, because a good conversation with a fire warden teaches things no website contains and often opens doors to job shadowing later. Scouts working toward Eagle should look hard at **9c**, since a Firewise assessment frequently reveals a larger need that can grow into a service project — though be clear that this requirement is not itself an Eagle project.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Setting Up a Meeting With a Professional" >}}
Options 9b, 9d, and 9e all require contacting an adult you do not know. Do it well and you will get a much better experience.

- **Get your parent or guardian and counselor involved first.** Scouting America's youth protection rules require that meetings with adults outside Scouting include another adult, and your parent or counselor should help arrange the contact.
- **Start local.** Your county fire warden, the nearest ranger district or state forestry field office, a local fire department, or the nearest National Weather Service forecast office are all reachable and used to public inquiries.
- **Ask in writing first.** A short email explaining who you are, which merit badge you are working on, exactly what you need to discuss, and how much of their time you are asking for (30 minutes is plenty) gets a much better response than a cold call.
- **Send your questions ahead.** Professionals give far better answers when they have had a day to think.
- **Bring a notebook and take real notes.** You will be discussing this with your counselor afterward, and details fade fast.
- **Send a thank-you note.** It costs you five minutes, and it is why the next Scout gets a yes.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

Start with the option that fits, and remember: you only need one. The remaining pages are here if you want to see what the other paths look like before you commit.
