{{< drg/requirement number="9b" >}}
Visit with a state or federal forestry official or your local fire warden. Discuss the causes of and types of wildland fire that occur in your area. Discuss the prime fire season(s) in your area. Identify the agency responsible for wildland fire suppression in your area. Discuss what you learned with your counselor.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Everything in this guide so far has been national. This option makes it local, and the difference is bigger than you would expect. Fire in coastal South Carolina, the Colorado Front Range, the Minnesota Northwoods, and central Texas are four genuinely different problems with different causes, different seasons, and different agencies in charge — and the person who works your county can explain yours in twenty minutes better than any website can.

## Finding the Right Person

Titles vary a lot by state, so aim for any of these:

- **State forestry agency personnel** — a district forester, fire management officer, or fire prevention specialist. Most states have field offices covering a few counties each.
- **Fire warden.** In many eastern and northeastern states, the fire warden is a specific official who issues burn permits and holds legal authority over open burning in a town or district. If your state uses the title, this is the person the requirement is pointing you toward.
- **Federal officials** at a Forest Service ranger district, BLM field office, National Park Service unit, Fish and Wildlife Service refuge, or Bureau of Indian Affairs agency.
- **Local fire department** wildland or brush unit officers, especially in the WUI.
- **County emergency management** offices, which coordinate evacuation and often know the whole landscape of responsibility.

Start by searching for your state forestry agency plus your county name, or call the nearest ranger district and ask who handles fire. Reach out by email first, explain the merit badge, and propose a specific 30-minute window.

## What the Requirement Asks You to Cover

Four specific things, so make sure you leave with all four.

### 1. Causes of wildland fire in your area

You covered national causes in [Req 4a](/merit-badges/wildland-fire-management/guide/req4a/); now find out how your area differs. Ask what the top three ignition causes are locally, whether the mix has shifted in recent years, whether human or lightning ignitions dominate, and which cause the agency spends the most prevention effort on and why.

### 2. Types of wildland fire that occur in your area

Ask what actually burns. Is it grass, brush, timber litter, or crown fire in your area? Do fires there tend to stay on the surface, or do they crown? Are ground fires in peat or deep duff a problem? What typical size and behavior does the agency plan around, and what has the largest fire in recent memory looked like?

### 3. Prime fire season(s)

This is the question that most often surprises Scouts, because many regions have **two** seasons rather than one. Much of the Southeast and parts of the East burn in late winter and early spring when last year's vegetation is cured and before green-up; the Interior West peaks in mid to late summer; parts of California peak in autumn with offshore wind events; the Great Plains have a spring season. Ask what drives your season — snowmelt timing, green-up, the arrival of monsoon moisture, seasonal wind patterns, drought cycles — and whether the season has been lengthening.

### 4. The agency responsible for suppression

This one has a precise answer and is worth getting exactly right. Ask who has **initial attack responsibility** for the specific area where you live and where you camp, since it may be different for each. Jurisdiction in wildland fire is genuinely complicated: state land, federal land of several kinds, tribal land, county, and local fire district responsibility can be interleaved within a few miles. Ask about **mutual aid agreements** and whether the state has a **cooperative agreement** with federal agencies, and ask how a fire in your county actually gets reported, dispatched, and resourced.

{{< drg/checklist title="Questions Worth Asking Beyond the Requirement" subtitle="These are the ones that make an interview memorable" >}}

- **What is the most common mistake the public makes here?** Prevention specialists always have an answer, and it is usually specific and surprising.
- **What was your most memorable fire, and what did you learn from it?**
- **Does your agency use prescribed fire here? What are the obstacles?** Local answers about smoke, staffing, and burn windows are far more concrete than anything in a textbook.
- **How has fire in this area changed over your career?**
- **What should my troop know before camping here in fire season?**
- **How did you get into this work, and what would you tell a Scout who wants to do it?** This overlaps directly with [Req 11](/merit-badges/wildland-fire-management/guide/req11/) and can save you a second interview.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/tip >}}
Ask whether you can see the equipment. Almost every fire office has engines, tools, a fire shelter, and often a weather station on site, and most officials are delighted to show them. Ten minutes in an engine bay will teach you more about [Req 8](/merit-badges/wildland-fire-management/guide/req8/) than an hour of reading — and if you have not yet demonstrated three hand tools, this is often the easiest place to do it.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Preparing and Following Up

**Before:** read this guide's pages on causes, behavior, and tactics so you can ask informed questions. Write your questions down and email them ahead. Bring a notebook, and ask permission before recording or photographing anything.

**During:** take real notes, especially names, agencies, and numbers. Ask follow-ups when something surprises you — the surprises are the best material for your counselor discussion.

**After:** write a one-page summary the same day, while it is fresh, organized around the four required topics. Send a thank-you note. Then meet with your counselor and discuss what you learned, focusing on how your local picture differs from the national one you studied in this guide. That comparison is what turns an interview into understanding.
