This requirement has two halves. First you research the national picture and come to your counselor with real numbers for **annual acres burned in each of the last three years.** Then you work through six specific kinds of damage:

- **2a — Commercial forest products:** timber, pulp, and the jobs attached to them
- **2b — Fish and wildlife habitat:** what happens to animals and streams
- **2c — Soil and water:** erosion, water repellency, debris flows, drinking water
- **2d — Recreation and public use:** closures, damaged trails, lost seasons
- **2e — Homes, communities, and human resources:** structures, people, firefighters, budgets
- **2f — Air quality and public health:** smoke, particulates, and who gets sick

An important framing note before you start. Fire itself is not the villain here — [Requirement 3](/merit-badges/wildland-fire-management/guide/req3/) is entirely about the good that fire does. What this requirement examines is the damage from fire that is **too intense, too large, or in the wrong place**, which is exactly what a century of fuel buildup and expanding development has produced.

### Finding the Acres-Burned Numbers

The authoritative source is the **National Interagency Fire Center (NIFC)** in Boise, Idaho, which compiles national statistics from every federal, state, and local agency. Its statistics pages publish yearly totals for both the *number of fires* and the *acres burned*, plus 10-year averages for comparison. Look up the three most recent complete years yourself — NIFC updates the tables as final numbers come in, so a printed figure goes stale fast.

When you report those numbers, be ready for two follow-up questions your counselor is likely to ask:

- **Why do fires and acres move independently?** A year can have a huge *count* of fires but modest acreage, or relatively few fires that burn enormous acreage. Weather, wind events, and where the fires start matter more than how many there are. Recent years have swung between roughly 2.5 million and nearly 9 million acres nationally, so a single three-year window can look very different from the decade around it.
- **Do acres burned measure harm?** Not by themselves. A hundred thousand acres of low-intensity surface fire in a fire-adapted longleaf pine forest may be beneficial. Ten thousand acres of high-severity crown fire above a town's reservoir can be a catastrophe. **Burn severity** — how much of the vegetation and soil organic matter was actually killed or consumed — tells you more than raw size.

Bring three things to your counselor: the acres burned for each of the last three years, the source you used, and the 10-year average for comparison. Context is what turns a number into an argument.

## Requirement 2a: Commercial Forest Products

{{< drg/inherited-requirement number="2a" req_path="2.a" topic="Commercial forest products" />}}

American forests are a working industry. They supply lumber for construction, pulp for paper and cardboard, plywood, utility poles, fence posts, firewood, and chips for energy. A severe wildfire hits that industry in several ways at once.

**Standing timber is destroyed or downgraded.** A tree does not have to be reduced to ash to lose its value. Heat that kills the cambium — the thin living layer just under the bark — kills the tree even if the trunk looks intact. Char, cracks, and insect entry after the fire can drop a log from sawtimber grade to pulp grade or to nothing at all. Landowners often try **salvage logging** to recover value, but they are racing decay and wood-boring beetles, and salvage prices are low because everyone is selling at once.

**Future harvests disappear for decades.** A stand of Douglas-fir might be 50 to 80 years from planting to harvest. When high-severity fire kills a young plantation, the owner loses not one year of income but the entire investment plus everything it would have produced for two generations. Replanting costs money immediately and returns nothing for a very long time.

**Infrastructure and access burn too.** Bridges, culverts, log decks already cut and waiting, fences, and sawmills themselves can be lost. Roads damaged by fire and by suppression equipment need repair before any harvesting can resume.

**Local economies feel it.** In timber-dependent counties, mill shifts get cut, logging contractors sit idle, and the tax revenue that funds local schools and roads drops.

## Requirement 2b: Fish and Wildlife Habitat

{{< drg/inherited-requirement number="2b" req_path="2.b" topic="Fish and wildlife habitat" />}}

Most wildlife survives the passage of fire itself — birds fly, large mammals walk away, small mammals and reptiles go underground, and mortality during low- and moderate-intensity fire is usually low. The bigger story is what happens to **habitat** afterward.

**Structure is lost.** Animals do not just need "forest," they need specific features: cavity trees for woodpeckers and flying squirrels, dense mid-story cover for songbird nesting, downed logs for salamanders and small mammals, shrubs of a certain height for deer browse. High-severity fire can erase a particular structural stage across thousands of contiguous acres, which is a serious problem for species that need it and cannot travel far to find more.

**Aquatic habitat takes a delayed hit.** Fish are affected less by flames than by what comes after. Loss of streamside shade raises water temperature, which matters enormously to cold-water species like trout and salmon that need water below roughly 68°F. Ash and fine sediment washing into streams clog **spawning gravels**, smothering eggs. Ash inputs can also cause short, sharp swings in stream chemistry. In small headwater streams, a single post-fire debris flow can wipe out an entire local fish population.

**Habitat gets fragmented and invaded.** Very large severe burns can isolate populations from each other. Disturbed ground is also an opening for invasive plants — cheatgrass in the Great Basin is the classic example, because it cures early, burns readily, and shortens the fire cycle so much that sagebrush and the greater sage-grouse that depend on it cannot recover.

## Requirement 2c: Soil and Water

{{< drg/inherited-requirement number="2c" req_path="2.c" topic="Soil and water" />}}

This is the impact Scouts most often underestimate, and it may be the most consequential of all.

**Soil loses its cover and its structure.** Intense heat consumes the litter and duff layer that normally intercepts raindrops, exposing bare mineral soil to direct impact. It also destroys the organic matter and fungal networks that hold soil particles together in crumbs, so what is left is loose and easily moved.

**Some burned soils repel water.** During hot fires, waxy organic compounds vaporize, move down into the soil, and condense into a **hydrophobic layer** — a water-repellent band an inch or two below the surface. Rain that would normally soak in instead runs across the top. Hydrologists measure post-fire runoff from severely burned slopes at many times pre-fire levels for the same rainfall.

**Erosion and debris flows follow.** The first intense thunderstorm over a fresh burn scar can strip topsoil, gully hillsides, and send **debris flows** — fast slurries of water, mud, rock, and charred wood — down drainages. These flows are one of the deadliest post-fire hazards in the country and can occur years after the fire, whenever a strong enough storm arrives before vegetation recovers.

**Drinking water is at risk.** Roughly two-thirds of the U.S. water supply originates on forested land, much of it federally managed. When a burn scar sits above a reservoir, the utility downstream inherits sediment that fills storage capacity, nutrients that fuel algae blooms, and organic carbon that reacts with chlorine during treatment. Cities have spent hundreds of millions of dollars dredging reservoirs and upgrading treatment after upstream fires — and some have concluded it is cheaper to pay for forest thinning in their watersheds than to clean up afterward.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Camping Near a Burn Scar" safety="true" >}}
Scouts hike and camp in recently burned areas more often than they realize, and a burn scar is a genuinely hazardous place.

- **Check for closures first.** Land managers close burned areas for real reasons, and those closures are enforceable.
- **Never camp in or below a drainage on a burn scar.** Debris flows travel fast and give almost no warning. Camp on high ground away from channels.
- **Watch the forecast, not the sky.** Rain falling miles upstream can send a flow down a dry wash under blue sky above you.
- **Assume every standing burned tree can fall.** Fire-killed trees, called snags or **hazard trees**, drop limbs and topple without wind. Do not pitch tents under them.
- **Expect the ground to move.** Ash pits over burned-out root systems can stay hot for weeks and stay soft for years.

## Requirement 2d: Recreation and Public Use

{{< drg/inherited-requirement number="2d" req_path="2.d" topic="Recreation and public use" />}}

Public lands exist in large part so people can use them, and fire shuts that down.

**Closures come first and last longest.** During a fire, agencies close roads, trails, campgrounds, boat ramps, and sometimes entire national forests. After the fire, closures often continue for months or years while crews remove hazard trees, rebuild trail structures, and wait for slopes to stabilize. A closure order over a holiday weekend can cost a gateway community its entire season.

**Facilities and trails are physically damaged.** Wooden bridges, boardwalks, signs, trail markers, privies, picnic shelters, fences, and interpretive displays burn. Trails get buried under fallen snags or washed out by post-fire runoff. Rebuilding a backcountry bridge can take years to fund and schedule.

**The experience changes.** Even when a trail reopens, a walk through a mile of blackened snags is not the walk people came for. Scenic viewpoints lose their view. Shade disappears, which makes summer hiking hotter and more dangerous. Fishing declines where streams were damaged. Hunting patterns shift as game animals relocate.

**Smoke closes things that never burned.** Regional smoke routinely cancels events, races, and camps hundreds of miles from any flame — including Scout activities.

**The economic ripple is large.** Outfitters, guides, campground concessionaires, motels, restaurants, and outdoor retailers in gateway towns depend on a short season. Recreation-dependent counties can lose more money to closures and smoke than to the burned timber itself.

## Requirement 2e: Homes, Communities, and Human Resources

{{< drg/inherited-requirement number="2e" req_path="2.e" topic="Homes, communities, and human resources" />}}

**Structures burn, mostly from embers.** Post-fire investigations consistently find that the majority of homes lost in wildfires are ignited not by a wall of flame but by **firebrands** — burning embers lofted ahead of the fire, sometimes more than a mile — that land in gutters, on decks, in vent openings, or in mulch beds against a wall. That is a hopeful finding, because it means the details of a house and its first five feet matter enormously. [Req 4b](/merit-badges/wildland-fire-management/guide/req4b/) covers what to do about it.

**People die, and firefighters die.** Civilian fatalities are usually connected to late evacuation, blocked or single-access roads, and fires moving faster than any warning system. Firefighter fatalities come from burnovers, vehicle and aircraft accidents, falling trees, and heart attacks. Beyond deaths, wildland firefighters face burns, heat illness, dehydration, injuries from tools and rolling rock, and long-term respiratory and cardiovascular risk from repeated smoke exposure.

**Human resources are strained in ways that do not show up in acre counts.** Crews work 16-hour shifts on 14-day assignments and then get extended. Fire seasons that once ran three months now run much longer in many regions, which means fatigue, cumulative injury, family strain, and post-traumatic stress. Retention and hiring problems in wildland fire are a serious operational risk: the country can have all the engines and aircraft it wants and still be short of qualified people.

**Communities lose more than buildings.** Schools, water and power systems, cell towers, clinics, and businesses go down together. Families displaced for months may never return, and towns lose population, tax base, and institutions permanently. Insurance markets tighten, and some homeowners in high-risk areas cannot get coverage at all.

**The dollar figures are enormous.** Federal suppression spending alone has run in the billions of dollars annually, and total losses in bad years — structures, business interruption, health costs, watershed damage — run far higher.

## Requirement 2f: Air Quality and Public Health

{{< drg/inherited-requirement number="2f" req_path="2.f" topic="Air quality and public health" />}}

Wildfire smoke is now the reason many Americans encounter fire at all, and it travels far beyond the burn.

**The dangerous ingredient is PM2.5.** Smoke is a mix of gases and particles; the particles that matter most for health are **fine particulate matter smaller than 2.5 micrometers** across, roughly 1/30th the width of a human hair. They are small enough to travel deep into the lungs and pass into the bloodstream. Smoke also contains carbon monoxide, ozone-forming compounds, and irritant gases.

**Short-term effects show up fast.** Burning eyes, scratchy throat, coughing, headache, and shortness of breath appear within hours of heavy smoke. People with asthma have attacks. Emergency room visits for respiratory problems rise measurably during smoke episodes, and so do visits for heart attacks and strokes, because fine particles trigger inflammation and affect blood clotting.

**Some people are much more vulnerable.** Children breathe more air per pound of body weight than adults and their lungs are still developing. Older adults, pregnant people, outdoor workers, and anyone with asthma, COPD, or heart disease face higher risk. So do people without air conditioning or filtration, which makes smoke an equity problem as well as a health one.

**Smoke crosses the continent.** Plumes from western and Canadian fires regularly push air quality in Midwestern and East Coast cities into unhealthy or hazardous ranges. In some recent summers, smoke has erased decades of measured air quality improvement in parts of the country for days at a time.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Know how to protect your patrol on a smoky day. Check the **Air Quality Index (AQI)** on AirNow before outdoor activity. Above 100, scale back strenuous exercise for sensitive Scouts; above 150, everyone should reduce exertion; above 200, move activity indoors or reschedule. Bandanas, surgical masks, and wet cloths do **not** filter PM2.5 — only a properly fitted N95 or P100 respirator does, and those are not appropriate for young children or anyone who has trouble breathing in one. The most effective step is usually the simplest: get indoors, close windows, and reduce how hard everyone is breathing.

## Pulling the Six Together

Notice what these six impacts have in common: almost none of them are limited to the burned area, and almost all of them get worse with **severity**, not just size. Water treatment costs land on a city fifty miles downstream. Smoke lands on a state a thousand miles away. Habitat loss lands on species for decades. That is why the modern strategy is not simply "fight harder" — it is to change the fuels, the buildings, and the timing so fires burn less severely in the first place.

Which brings up an idea that sounds backwards until you understand it: one of the best tools for reducing all six of these harms is deliberately setting the forest on fire.
