{{< drg/requirement number="1b" >}}
Discuss the prevention of and treatment for the following health concerns that could occur while fishing: cuts and scratches, puncture wounds, insect bites, hypothermia, dehydration, heat exhaustion, heatstroke, and sunburn.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Fishing can keep you outdoors for hours at a time — in sun, wind, and sometimes extreme temperatures. Your body is exposed to water, sharp equipment, insects, and environmental stresses. Understanding how to prevent and treat common fishing-related health issues is essential to staying healthy and enjoying your time on the water.

## Cuts and Scratches

**Prevention:**
- Wear long sleeves and pants when fishing to protect exposed skin
- Inspect your tackle box and equipment regularly for sharp edges, broken hooks, and exposed barbs
- Handle hooks and lures carefully, and always point them away from your body
- Avoid grabbing fish directly; use a net or wet hands if you must handle one
- Watch for sharp rocks, shells, and debris at your fishing location

**Treatment:**
1. **Wash the wound** with clean water and soap to remove dirt, sand, and debris
2. **Stop any bleeding** by applying direct pressure with a clean cloth
3. **Apply an antiseptic** (hydrogen peroxide or antibiotic ointment) to prevent infection
4. **Cover the wound** with a bandage if it is still bleeding or dirty
5. **Monitor for infection** — if the wound becomes red, warm, swollen, or produces pus, see a doctor

## Puncture Wounds

Puncture wounds are deep, narrow holes made by hooks, lures, or sharp objects. These wounds are particularly dangerous because they can introduce bacteria deep into your tissue, increasing infection risk.

**Prevention:**
- Keep hooks capped or secured when not in use
- Wear closed-toe, sturdy shoes to protect your feet
- Avoid stepping on unknown objects in the water
- Be careful when removing hooks from fish — the fish may thrash and create a puncture wound

**Treatment:**
1. **Encourage bleeding** — Puncture wounds can trap bacteria, so allowing them to bleed (if safe) helps flush out contaminants
2. **Soak the wound** in warm, clean water for 10–15 minutes
3. **Apply an antiseptic** and cover with a bandage
4. **Watch carefully for infection** — puncture wounds can become infected rapidly
5. **Seek medical attention** if the wound is deep, caused by a dirty or rusty object, or from a fish bite (animal bites need professional care)

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Never ignore a puncture wound from a rusty hook or unknown object. Rust does not cause tetanus — bacteria in the wound does — but rusty objects are often dirty. If you are not sure about your tetanus vaccination, see a doctor. Tetanus is preventable with a booster shot.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Insect Bites

Mosquitoes, flies, ticks, bees, wasps, and other insects are common at fishing locations. Most bites are harmless, but they can itch terribly and occasionally cause serious reactions.

**Prevention:**
- Wear long sleeves and long pants, especially in early morning and evening when mosquitoes are active
- Use insect repellent containing 20–30% DEET on exposed skin and clothing
- Avoid brightly colored clothing, which attracts some insects
- Cover exposed skin before you get to your fishing location
- Tuck pant legs into socks in areas where ticks are common
- Avoid swatting at bees or wasps — if you leave them alone, they will usually leave you alone

**Treatment:**
- For mosquito and fly bites: Wash the area, apply ice or a cold compress, and use hydrocortisone cream or calamine lotion to reduce itching
- For tick bites: Use tweezers to grasp the tick close to your skin and pull steadily until it releases. Do not twist or jerk. Wash the bite with soap and water.
- For bee and wasp stings: Remove the stinger if visible by scraping it off with a card or fingernail (do not squeeze). Wash the area, apply ice, and use hydrocortisone cream

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Ticks can carry Lyme disease and other serious infections. If you find a tick on your body, remove it promptly and watch the bite location for a expanding red rash (the "bull's-eye" rash of Lyme disease). If a rash appears, seek medical attention.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Hypothermia

Hypothermia occurs when your core body temperature drops dangerously low. You can develop hypothermia in any cold environment — even in mild conditions if you are wet. Hypothermia is serious because it impairs your thinking and judgment, so you may not realize you are in danger.

**Prevention:**
- Wear a wetsuit, dry suit, or insulating layers when fishing in cold water
- Change out of wet clothes immediately
- Bring an extra set of dry clothes in your tackle bag
- Eat regular meals and snacks to fuel your body's heat production
- Stay dry — moisture speeds heat loss
- Avoid alcohol, which impairs your body's heat-regulating mechanisms
- Never fish alone in cold conditions

**Treatment:**
1. **Move to a warm, sheltered location** as soon as you suspect hypothermia
2. **Remove wet clothing** and replace with dry clothes
3. **Warm the person gradually** — wrapping in blankets, drinking warm (not hot) drinks, and staying in a warm room
4. **Seek emergency help** if the person is shivering severely, slurring speech, acting confused, or unresponsive

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Hypothermia is a medical emergency. If you suspect someone has hypothermia, call 911 immediately. Do not delay. Do not give them alcohol or coffee (which can make hypothermia worse).
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Dehydration

Dehydration is when your body loses more water than it takes in. Fishing often means sitting in the sun for hours without thinking about drinking enough water. Dehydration reduces your ability to regulate body temperature and can quickly become serious.

**Prevention:**
- Drink water constantly throughout your fishing trip, even if you do not feel thirsty
- Avoid sugary drinks and alcohol, which increase dehydration
- Eat foods with high water content (fruits, vegetables)
- Drink more if you are sweating or it is hot outside
- Bring more water than you think you will need

**Treatment:**
- Drink water or an electrolyte drink (sports drink) gradually throughout the day
- Rest in a cool or shaded location
- If dehydration is severe (dizziness, confusion, dark urine, no urination), seek medical help

## Heat Exhaustion

Heat exhaustion occurs when your body cannot cool itself effectively. You might experience dizziness, nausea, heavy sweating, weakness, or a headache.

**Prevention:**
- Wear light-colored, loose-fitting clothing
- Wear a hat to shade your head
- Take frequent breaks in the shade
- Drink plenty of water
- Avoid fishing during the hottest part of the day (11 a.m.–3 p.m.) if possible
- Be extra careful if you are overweight, out of shape, or have heart problems

**Treatment:**
1. **Move to a cool location** immediately
2. **Lie down** and elevate your feet slightly
3. **Remove extra clothing**
4. **Cool your skin** by applying cool water or wet cloths to your face, neck, and wrists
5. **Drink cool water** slowly
6. **Rest until you feel better**

## Heatstroke

Heatstroke is more serious than heat exhaustion. Your body temperature soars above 103°F, and your cooling system fails. Heatstroke is a medical emergency.

**Prevention:**
- Follow the same steps as heat exhaustion prevention
- Never ignore early warning signs of heat exhaustion — catch it before it becomes heatstroke
- Check on others regularly — heatstroke can impair judgment, so a person in heatstroke may not realize how serious it is

**Treatment:**
1. **Call 911 immediately**
2. **Move to a cool location** (or create shade)
3. **Cool the person as quickly as possible** — use cold water, ice packs, fans, or cool water spray
4. **Do not give them anything to drink** — they may not be able to swallow safely
5. **Stay with them** until emergency responders arrive

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Heatstroke symptoms include hot, dry skin; confusion; loss of consciousness; and sometimes cessation of sweating (despite extreme heat). If you suspect heatstroke, treat it as a medical emergency. Heatstroke can cause permanent organ damage or death if not treated quickly.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Sunburn

Sunburn is common among anglers who spend long hours under the sun's reflection off the water. Repeated sunburns increase your risk of skin cancer.

**Prevention:**
- Apply broad-spectrum sunscreen (SPF 30+) 15 minutes before going outside
- Reapply sunscreen every 2 hours and after swimming or sweating
- Wear light-colored, long-sleeved clothing and a hat
- Seek shade during the middle of the day (11 a.m.–3 p.m.)
- Wear sunglasses to protect your eyes

**Treatment:**
- Take a cool bath or shower to soothe the burn
- Apply moisturizer or aloe vera to reduce itching
- Drink plenty of water to help your skin heal
- Avoid further sun exposure until the burn heals
- Over-the-counter pain relievers can help with discomfort

{{< drg/tip >}}
Water reflects ultraviolet (UV) rays, making sunburn on the water worse than on land. A sunny day on the water can burn you faster than the same day on shore. This means you need sunscreen even on cloudy days.
{{< /drg/tip >}}
