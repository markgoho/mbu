{{< drg/requirement number="1c" >}}
Explain how to remove a barbed hook that is lodged in someone's arm.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A fishing hook with a barb is one of the most common fishing injuries. The barb is designed to hold the hook in a fish's mouth — but it also makes the hook difficult to remove from human skin. Knowing the proper technique can make the difference between a simple removal and a trip to the emergency room.

## The Problem with Barbed Hooks

A barbed hook consists of a sharp point and a small backward-facing barb just behind the point. When you push the hook into flesh, the barb digs in and prevents the hook from slipping back out. This design is perfect for catching fish but terrible for removal from skin.

**Why you should not simply yank the hook out:**
- The barb will tear and damage tissue, causing severe pain and heavy bleeding
- The more you struggle, the deeper the hook may go
- Yanking can snap the hook, leaving the barb embedded in the skin
- Increased tissue damage increases infection risk

## Proper Hook Removal: Two Methods

There are two safe, effective ways to remove a barbed hook. Choose the method based on how deep the hook is and what resources you have available.

### Method 1: The String Method (for shallow hooks)

This method works best for hooks that are not deeply embedded. It requires only a piece of string or fishing line.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Removing a Barbed Hook with String" >}}
You have hooked your friend's arm while casting. The hook is embedded about half an inch deep in the skin.

**Step 1: Prepare.** Have your friend sit down and rest the injured arm on a table. Gather a piece of fishing line, string, or strong thread; a clean cloth; and antibiotic ointment.

**Step 2: Clean the area.** Wash the hook site and surrounding skin with soap and water. Let the area dry.

**Step 3: Pass the string.** Take a 12-inch piece of string and loop it under the hook, passing the string under the curve of the hook (the bend, not the barb). The string should form a loop that touches the skin along the hook shaft.

**Step 4: Hold steady.** Use one hand to press down on the eye of the hook (the top) to keep it in place and prevent it from diving deeper. Press gently but firmly.

**Step 5: Jerk the string.** Grasp both ends of the string and pull sharply and smoothly toward you in one quick motion. The loop will lift the hook point and barb clear of the skin in a single movement. Do not pull slowly or hesitantly — speed is important.

**Step 6: Inspect and treat.** Look at the wound to make sure the entire hook came out. Wash the puncture wound, apply antibiotic ointment, and cover with a bandage if needed. Monitor for signs of infection.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

### Method 2: The Advance and Dull Method (for deeply embedded hooks)

If the hook is deeply embedded or the barb has gone all the way through the skin, the string method may not work. This method requires advance and pushing the hook the rest of the way through, then cutting and removing it.

**When to use this method:**
- The hook has penetrated deep into the skin
- The barb has broken the surface on the opposite side (the hook point is visible underneath the skin)
- The string method failed
- You have appropriate tools (wire cutters or pliers)

**Steps:**
1. **Anesthetize the area** (if possible) with ice or a topical anesthetic to reduce pain
2. **Advance the hook** — Push the hook forward through the skin, allowing the barb and point to emerge on the other side
3. **Push past the barb** — Continue advancing until the barb clears the skin on both the entry and exit points
4. **Remove the barb** — If you have wire cutters or pliers, clip or bend the barb flat so it will not re-catch on the tissue
5. **Back the hook out** — Now that the barb is no longer a factor, you can back the hook out in reverse
6. **Clean and bandage** — Wash the puncture wounds thoroughly and apply antibiotic ointment

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
If a hook has penetrated the eye, face, or any sensitive area, or if the barb is deeply embedded and you cannot remove it after trying the string method, do not continue. Seek professional medical help immediately. Emergency room doctors have the training and tools to remove difficult hooks safely.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## When to Seek Medical Help

Remove the hook yourself only if:
- You have the proper tools and knowledge
- The wound is not in a sensitive location (eye, ear, mouth, joint)
- You are able to remain calm
- The hook was relatively clean

**Seek medical help immediately if:**
- The hook is in or near the eye, ear, face, or mouth
- The hook broke and part of it is still embedded
- You cannot remove the hook within a few minutes
- The wound is very deep or bleeding heavily
- You do not know the person's tetanus vaccination status
- Signs of infection develop (increasing pain, redness, warmth, pus, swelling)

## Prevention is Better Than Removal

The best hook removal is one you never have to perform. Here is how to prevent hook injuries:

{{< drg/checklist title="Hook Safety Rules" subtitle="Follow these practices every time you fish" >}}
- Always cap or sheath hooks when not actively fishing
- Never cast a lure or fly at someone, even in play
- Point your rod tip down and to the side when walking near other people
- Keep your fishing line under control — loose line can wrap and cause injury
- Secure your tackle box and lures when not in use
- Warn people nearby before you cast — say "Coming through" or "Heads up"
- Teach children never to pick up loose fishing line or touch hooks
- Store hooks in a tackle box or pouch, never loose in a pocket or backpack
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/tip >}}
If you frequently fish in situations where hook injuries are possible — especially if you fish with other people — consider using barbless hooks. Many fly fishermen and catch-and-release anglers use barbless hooks, which are just as effective at catching fish but infinitely easier to remove from skin (or to remove from fish with minimal injury).
{{< /drg/tip >}}
