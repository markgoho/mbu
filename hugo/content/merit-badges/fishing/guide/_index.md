There is something almost magical about the moment a fish bites. The line goes taut, the rod tip bends, and suddenly you are connected to a wild creature in its own world. Whether you are standing in a quiet pond at dawn, casting from a rocky shoreline, or wading through a cold mountain stream, fishing teaches patience, skill, and respect for nature in ways that few other activities can match.

The Fishing merit badge is not about catching the most fish or the biggest fish. It is about understanding how fish live, learning to handle tackle safely and skillfully, and practicing conservation so that future generations of Scouts can experience the same thrill you are about to discover.

## Then and Now

### Then — The Art of Angling

For thousands of years, people have caught fish using simple tools: a line, a hook, and bait. In the 1800s, fishing became a gentleman's art. British aristocrats perfected fly fishing on Scottish streams. American anglers developed their own techniques for bass and trout. Local knowledge passed down through families was your most valuable resource — which streams held fish, what they ate, and when they bit.

Early Scout handbooks emphasized practical skills: tying knots, understanding fish behavior, and handling gear safely. Fishing was part survival skill, part sport, part way to feed your family.

- **Purpose:** Sustenance and recreation with simple, handmade gear
- **Mindset:** Learn from local experts, respect the water, take only what you need

### Now — Science Meets Tradition

Today, fishing blends ancient wisdom with modern science. Fish biologists study how dams and pollution affect populations. Conservation efforts restore salmon runs and protect endangered species. Anglers use sonar to locate fish and high-tech tackle to cast with precision. Social media connects a global community of fishing enthusiasts.

Yet the core experience remains unchanged: standing at water's edge, reading the water, and using skill and patience to fool a fish into taking your bait. Modern Scouts learn the same fundamental skills as their predecessors — but with a deeper understanding of why they work and an explicit commitment to leave the water cleaner and healthier than they found it.

- **Purpose:** Recreation, conservation, and connection to wild places
- **Mindset:** Enjoy the sport responsibly, protect fish populations, be an ambassador for clean water

## Kinds of Fishing

Fishing comes in many forms. Each has its own character, challenges, and appeal. Here are the main types you should know about.

### Freshwater Fishing

Freshwater fishing happens in lakes, ponds, rivers, and streams. It is the most accessible type of fishing for most Scouts because freshwater habitats are often nearby. You might target species like bass, trout, catfish, panfish, or pike — each with its own preferences for bait, lures, and habitat. Freshwater fishing can be as simple as dropping a line off a dock or as technical as fly casting in a mountain stream.

### Saltwater Fishing

Saltwater fishing occurs in oceans, bays, and estuaries. The fish are often larger and more powerful, and the tackle must be tougher to resist corrosion and the force of heavy fish. Saltwater species range from small snappers and pompano to large tarpon and tuna. If you live near the coast, saltwater fishing opens up a whole new world of possibilities.

The largest fish ever caught on a rod and reel was a Great White Shark weighing 2,664 pounds — but you do not have to catch a monster to have an amazing fishing experience. Many anglers consider a beautiful five-inch trout the catch of a lifetime.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

### Fly Fishing

Fly fishing is one of the most elegant and effective fishing methods. Instead of casting a heavy sinker or lure, you cast an artificial fly — a tiny imitation of an insect — onto the water's surface or beneath it. Fly fishing demands precision, finesse, and a deep understanding of the river or stream. It is often called a meditation — a chance to quiet your mind and focus on reading the water.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Whether you fish in freshwater or saltwater, always wear a personal flotation device (PFD) when near deep water. Drowning happens quickly and silently. A PFD could save your life or the life of a friend.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Shore & Bank Fishing

Not all fishing requires a boat. Shoreline and bank fishing — from docks, jetties, river banks, and beaches — puts you right at the fish's door. This is often the most accessible type of fishing for young anglers and requires minimal equipment. You can start with a simple rod and reel, a bucket of bait, and patience.

### Lure Fishing & Artificial Baits

Lure fishing uses artificial imitations of baitfish, insects, frogs, or crawdads. Lures work by triggering a fish's instinct to chase or strike at something that looks alive. Successful lure fishing demands more technique than bait fishing — you have to make the lure move in a way that convinces the fish to bite.
