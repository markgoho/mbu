{{< drg/requirement number="1a" >}}
Explain to your counselor the most likely hazards you may encounter while participating in fishing activities, and what you should do to anticipate, help prevent, mitigate, and respond to these hazards.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Fishing is generally a safe activity — but like any time you spend near water or handling sharp equipment, hazards exist. The key is recognizing them before they become problems. A prepared angler anticipates trouble and takes simple steps to prevent it.

## Common Fishing Hazards

### Water-Related Hazards

**Drowning** is the number one hazard of fishing. Whether you slip off a dock, wade into deeper water than you realize, or get caught in a current, water can be dangerous. Drowning happens silently and quickly — a person can slip beneath the surface in seconds.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Always wear a personal flotation device (PFD) or life jacket when fishing near deep water, on boats, or near moving water. Even excellent swimmers wear PFDs — they save lives. There is no such thing as being too safe.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

**Cold water** can cause hypothermia — dangerous loss of body heat — even on days that feel warm. A sudden dunking in a cold lake or stream can cause your body temperature to drop fast. Hypothermia can impair your thinking and muscle control within minutes.

**Strong currents** in rivers can sweep you off your feet. Flowing water looks deceptively calm but can exert tremendous force. Even shallow-looking rivers can have deep holes and dangerous underwater obstacles.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Never fish alone, especially near deep water or moving water. Fish with a buddy. If something happens to you, someone is there to call for help or pull you out.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Hook and Line Hazards

**Fishing hooks** are designed to embed themselves in fish mouths — which means they can embed themselves in your skin, eye, or face just as easily. Barbed hooks are particularly dangerous because they are hard to remove. A hook in the eye can cause blindness. A hook in an artery can cause severe bleeding.

**Fishing line** — especially monofilament line — can wrap around your fingers, neck, or clothing. If a heavy fish pulls suddenly or a motor is running, the line can cut or strangle. Always keep loose line clear of your body.

**Lures and sinkers** are small projectiles. When you cast, a lure travels at high speed toward you and anyone nearby. A lure or sinker striking your eye, face, or head can cause serious injury.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="A Hook Embedded in Your Finger" >}}
You set the hook when a fish bites and feel a sharp pain in your finger. You have accidentally hooked yourself.

- **Stay calm.** Panicking makes the situation worse.
- **Do not yank the hook out.** This will cause more damage and increase bleeding.
- **Remove the hook carefully.** If the hook is shallow and not barbed, gently back it out. If it is barbed and deep, do not remove it yourself — go to urgent care or the emergency room.
- **Clean the wound.** Wash it thoroughly and monitor for infection (increasing pain, redness, warmth, or pus).
- **Seek help if needed.** A deep hook wound or one in a sensitive location (face, eye) needs medical attention.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

### Tackle and Equipment Hazards

**Fishing rods** can snap or break, sending splinters into your hands or face. A broken rod with a jagged end is a sharp weapon.

**Reels** have moving parts that can pinch or catch fingers. A spinning reel can catch loose clothing or hair.

**Tackle boxes** contain sharp hooks, lures with multiple barbs, and points. Reaching into a tackle box without looking can result in cuts and punctures.

### Environmental Hazards

**Slippery surfaces** are everywhere near water — wet rocks, algae-covered banks, wet docks, and boat decks. A slip can send you into the water or cause you to fall and hit your head.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Wear proper footwear with good grip when fishing. Wading boots with felt soles are designed for slippery riverbeds. Non-slip shoes or boots protect you on docks and boats.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

**Sharp rocks, shells, and debris** underwater can cut your feet. Broken glass, rusty metal, and other garbage in fishing spots can cause puncture wounds and infection.

**Weather hazards** include thunderstorms, strong winds, intense sun, and heat. Lightning is attracted to tall fishing rods held in the air. A storm can roll in quickly and create dangerous waves or currents.

**Insects, snakes, and other wildlife** are part of the fishing environment. Bees and wasps, mosquitoes, ticks, snakes, and alligators (depending on where you fish) all pose potential hazards.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Most snake encounters are harmless if you leave the snake alone. Snakes are far more afraid of you than you are of them. If you see a snake, simply move away and give it space.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Anticipate, Prevent, Mitigate, and Respond

### Anticipate
Before you go fishing, think about what could go wrong. Are you fishing in deep water? Is the weather forecast concerning? Will there be insects? Are you familiar with the location? What time of day will you fish? What is your worst-case scenario?

### Prevent
Take steps to avoid hazards before they happen. Wear a PFD. Bring a first-aid kit. Wear long sleeves and insect repellent. Check the weather. Never fish alone. Inspect your equipment for damage before you go.

### Mitigate
During your trip, reduce the impact of hazards that do occur. If you fall in, your PFD will keep you afloat. If you hook yourself, you know how to remove it safely. If the weather turns, you know when to head to shore.

### Respond
If a hazard becomes an emergency, know what to do. Can you call 911? Do you have a first-aid kit? Do you know how to perform CPR or treat severe bleeding? Is there a buddy to help?
