{{< drg/requirement number="10" >}}
If regulations and health concerns permit, clean and cook a fish you have caught. If you are unable to catch a fish for eating, acquire a fish, clean the fish you acquired, and cook the fish you acquired. Note: It is not required that you eat the fish.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Fishing is not just about the catch — it is about the full experience, including preparing your meal. Cleaning and cooking fish are skills that connect you to your food and honor the animal you caught. Even if you choose not to eat the fish, knowing how to process it properly is an essential angling skill.

## Preparing to Clean Your Fish

Before you start cleaning, prepare your workspace and gather the right tools.

### Tools You Need

**Essential tools:**
- **Fillet knife** — A thin, flexible knife designed for fish (6–7 inches long)
- **Cutting board** — Preferably one designated for fish (to avoid cross-contamination)
- **Paper towels** — For cleanup
- **Trash bag** — For scraps (never dispose in the water)
- **Fresh water** — For rinsing

**Optional but helpful:**
- **Fish scaler** — Makes removing scales easier (though you can use the back of a knife)
- **Pliers** — Helpful if pin bones need removal
- **Newspaper** — Protects surfaces during cleaning
- **Gloves** — Makes handling easier and keeps your hands clean

### Workspace Setup

{{< drg/checklist title="Cleaning Workspace Setup" subtitle="Prepare properly before you start" >}}
- Lay newspaper or use a designated fish-cleaning surface
- Have fresh running water available
- Keep your trash bag nearby
- Ensure your knife is sharp — dull knives are dangerous and produce poor cuts
- Wash your hands thoroughly before starting
- Wear an apron if you want to keep dry
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

## How to Clean a Fish

There are several methods for cleaning fish. The most common are filleting (removing meat from bones) and pan-dressing (gutting and leaving intact).

### Method 1: Filleting (Preferred for Most Fish)

Filleting produces boneless fillets of meat with no waste. This is the cleanest method and produces the best results for cooking.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Filleting a Fish" >}}
You have caught a largemouth bass or other whole fish and want to fillet it.

**Step 1: Position the fish.** Lay the fish on its side on your cutting board. It should be right-side up (eye and mouth visible).

**Step 2: Make the first cut.** Starting just behind the gills on the back of the fish, cut at a slight angle toward the head. Angle your knife so it passes just behind the gill plate.

**Step 3: Follow the backbone.** Turn the knife parallel to the cutting board and slice along the backbone from the head toward the tail. Keep the knife against the backbone — this removes meat while leaving bones behind. Use long, smooth strokes. Do not saw back and forth.

**Step 4: Separate the fillet.** As you reach the belly, carefully separate the fillet from the rib cage and backbone. You should end up with one complete fillet (side of meat).

**Step 5: Flip and repeat.** Flip the fish over and repeat on the other side. You now have two fillets.

**Step 6: Remove pin bones (optional).** Some fish have small bones running through the middle of the fillet. You can feel them with your finger. Using pliers or a knife, remove them by lifting and pulling at a slight angle. Or, leave them — they are edible and add texture.

**Step 7: Trim and clean.** Trim away any dark meat along the sides (this has a strong flavor) if desired. Rinse the fillets under cool running water.

**Step 8: Inspect.** Check for any remaining scales or bones. Your fillets should be clean and ready for cooking.

**Step 9: Store or cook immediately.** Refrigerate fillets on ice if you are not cooking immediately. Use within 1–2 days.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

### Method 2: Pan-Dressing (for Whole Fish Cooking)

Some people prefer cooking whole fish (pan-dressed, meaning gutted and cleaned but intact). This method is used for small fish like trout or panfish.

**Steps:**
1. **Gut the fish:** Make a cut from the anus toward the gills (do not cut through the backbone). Remove the innards into a trash bag.
2. **Clean thoroughly:** Rinse out the inside cavity under running water until clean.
3. **Remove gills (optional):** Lift the gill cover and remove the gills (some people skip this step).
4. **Rinse outside:** Rinse the entire fish under running water.
5. **Cook with scales on** or **quickly scale** if desired (they can be left on and the flesh separates from the scaled skin during cooking).

## Cooking Your Fish

There are infinite ways to cook fish. Here are the most accessible methods for a Scout to prepare a healthy, delicious meal.

### Method 1: Pan-Frying (Simplest and Best for Beginners)

Pan-frying is quick, simple, and produces delicious results. It works best for thin fillets.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Pan-Frying Fish Fillets" >}}
You have two clean fillets and want to cook them quickly for dinner.

**Step 1: Heat the pan.** Place a skillet or frying pan over medium-high heat. Add 2 tablespoons of butter or oil.

**Step 2: Prepare the fillets.** Pat fillets dry with paper towels (this promotes browning). Season with salt and pepper, and optionally with lemon juice or herbs.

**Step 3: Wait for the pan to heat.** The butter should sizzle when a drop of water hits it — the pan is ready.

**Step 4: Cook the fillets.** Carefully place fillets skin-side-up in the hot pan. Listen for a sizzle — if there is no sizzle, the pan is not hot enough.

**Step 5: Do not move them.** Let the fillets cook undisturbed for 3–4 minutes. This allows them to brown and cook through.

**Step 6: Flip carefully.** Gently flip each fillet using a spatula. Try to keep the fillet intact (do not break it).

**Step 7: Cook the other side.** Cook for another 2–3 minutes until the fish is cooked through. The flesh should flake when pressed with a fork.

**Step 8: Remove from heat.** Carefully transfer fillets to a plate. Discard the cooking fat.

**Step 9: Serve immediately.** Fish is best eaten hot. Serve with lemon, vegetables, rice, or potatoes.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

### Method 2: Baking

Baking is healthy, forgiving, and great if you are cooking for multiple people.

**Basic steps:**
1. **Preheat oven** to 375°F
2. **Prepare fillets** — place on a greased baking sheet
3. **Season** — salt, pepper, lemon juice, and optional herbs
4. **Bake** — 10–12 minutes until fish flakes easily with a fork
5. **Serve** — remove from oven and plate immediately

### Method 3: Foil Packet (Best for Campfire Cooking)

If you are camping and want to cook your catch over a campfire, foil packets work great.

**Steps:**
1. **Prepare** — clean fillets, season with salt, pepper, and lemon juice
2. **Build packet** — place fillet on a large piece of heavy-duty foil
3. **Add vegetables** — add diced vegetables (onions, peppers, potatoes) if desired
4. **Seal** — fold foil over and seal edges
5. **Cook** — place on campfire grate over coals for 10–15 minutes until cooked through
6. **Serve** — carefully open (steam is hot!) and eat from the foil packet

## Food Safety and Preparation

### Safe Handling Practices

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Fish spoils quickly, especially in warm weather. Keep your catch cold from the moment you catch it. Do not leave fish sitting in the sun. Spoiled fish can cause serious food poisoning.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

**Temperature safety:**
- Cook fish to an internal temperature of 145°F (63°C)
- Use a meat thermometer to check if unsure
- Fish is done when it flakes easily with a fork

**Cross-contamination prevention:**
- Use a separate cutting board for fish (do not use the same board for raw chicken, meat, or vegetables without washing first)
- Wash all surfaces, utensils, and hands after handling raw fish
- Do not reuse marinades that have touched raw fish

**Storage:**
- Keep fish cold from the moment you catch it (ice in a cooler)
- If not cooking the same day, freeze in an airtight container
- Thaw frozen fish in the refrigerator, never at room temperature
- Cooked fish keeps 3–4 days in the refrigerator

### Taste and Preferences

Different fish have different flavors:

**Mild, white fish (trout, bass, walleye):**
- Subtle, delicate flavor
- Pairs well with lemon, butter, simple seasonings
- Flakes into tender, white meat

**Oily fish (catfish, pike):**
- Stronger, more "fishy" flavor
- Benefits from seasonings, sauces, bolder flavors
- Richer meat

**Panfish (bluegill, sunfish):**
- Sweet, mild flavor
- Perfect for beginners
- Small fillets make for cute, whole-fish presentations

## Respecting Your Catch

Cleaning and cooking fish is not just a skill — it is an act of respect. The fish gave its life so you could eat. Honor that gift:

- **Waste nothing.** Use every edible part if possible (some people make stock from heads and bones)
- **Cook properly.** Poor cooking wastes good food
- **Eat and enjoy.** Savor your meal and appreciate where it came from
- **Share.** Bring cooked fish to family or friends who helped you
- **Learn.** Each fish you prepare teaches you something

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Many cultures around the world have traditional fish recipes passed down for generations. Learning to cook fish connects you to these traditions and teaches respect for the resource. Indigenous peoples have sustainably fished and eaten fish for thousands of years.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## If You Cannot Catch a Fish

The requirement allows you to acquire a fish if you cannot catch one. This might be because:
- You live in an area without accessible fishing
- You fish but do not catch anything (this happens to all anglers)
- Regulations prevent harvest in your area

**Options for acquiring a fish:**
- **Purchase from a grocery store** — Buy whole fish if possible (this teaches the same skills)
- **Ask a local angler or fisheries agency** — Some organizations provide fish for educational purposes
- **Fish farm or hatchery** — Some facilities sell or donate fish
- **Farmers market** — Look for whole fish for sale

The important thing is that you learn the skills of cleaning and cooking fish. The source does not matter — the learning does.

## Demonstration to Your Counselor

When you show your counselor that you have cleaned and cooked a fish:

1. **Describe the process.** Walk them through how you cleaned the fish (what method, how long it took)
2. **Show the result.** If possible, show the cooked fish (or have leftover portions to show)
3. **Explain what you learned.** Talk about what surprised you, what was challenging, what went well
4. **Discuss food safety.** Demonstrate that you understand proper handling and storage
5. **Share the experience.** Tell them about eating your catch — what did it taste like? Was it worth the effort?

{{< drg/tip >}}
If you cook for your counselor or at a Scout camp, you might inspire other Scouts to try it. Cooking your own catch is one of the most rewarding parts of fishing — sharing that experience with others spreads the passion for fishing and outdoor skills.
{{< /drg/tip >}}
