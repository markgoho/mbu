{{< drg/requirement number="1d" >}}
Name and explain five safety practices you should always follow while fishing.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Safety practices form the foundation of responsible fishing. These are not optional suggestions — they are habits that should become automatic, ingrained in every fishing trip you take. Here are five critical safety practices that every angler must follow.

## Safety Practice 1: Always Wear a Personal Flotation Device Near Water

A personal flotation device (PFD) or life jacket saves lives. Drowning is silent and fast — a person can slip beneath the surface in seconds without anyone noticing. Even the strongest swimmers drown.

**Why it matters:**
- Water accidents are unpredictable (you slip on a wet rock, a sudden current pulls you in, a boat rocks)
- Panic takes over in cold water, making swimming difficult
- A PFD keeps you afloat even if you are unconscious
- Wearing a PFD is the single most effective way to prevent drowning

**When to wear it:**
- Anytime you are fishing near deep water
- When fishing on a boat or dock
- When wading in rivers or streams
- In any situation where you could fall in over your head

**How to choose:**
- Select a Coast Guard-approved PFD that fits snugly and is appropriate for your activity
- Inflatable PFDs are less bulky but require proper maintenance
- Worn PFDs (the padded kind you wear like a vest) are always ready and never fail
- Choose a bright color so rescuers can spot you easily

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Do not assume that being a good swimmer makes a PFD unnecessary. Professional rescue divers, Coast Guard personnel, and experienced water sports athletes all wear PFDs every time they are near water. Weather changes rapidly, and accidents happen to anyone.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Safety Practice 2: Never Fish Alone

Fishing with a buddy is one of the most important safety practices. A friend can:
- Call for emergency help if you have an accident
- Perform CPR or first aid if you need it
- Help rescue you if you fall in
- Provide warmth and shelter in an emergency
- Witness what happened so rescuers know what to do

**Buddy responsibilities:**
- Keep an eye on each other, especially around water
- Know each other's swimming ability and limitations
- Check on each other regularly — especially if one person goes quiet or disappears
- Agree on a meeting point if you separate
- Know how to call 911 and have a cell phone with you (even if there is no service, 911 calls often go through)

{{< drg/tip >}}
If you do fish alone — though it is not recommended — let someone know where you are going, what time you expect to return, and what to do if you do not check in by a certain time. This way, if something happens, someone will look for you.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Safety Practice 3: Check the Weather Before You Go and Heed Warning Signs

Weather changes rapidly near water. Calm morning conditions can deteriorate into thunderstorms or strong winds within minutes. Weather hazards include:
- **Lightning** — Tall fishing rods in open water attract lightning
- **Strong winds** — Winds can blow over boats or make wading dangerous
- **Sudden cold** — A temperature drop can lead to hypothermia
- **Fog** — Fog can disorient you and make it hard to find your way back
- **Flash floods** — Rivers and streams can rise suddenly after heavy rain upstream

**Before you go:**
- Check the weather forecast for your fishing location
- Watch for warning signs during your trip (darkening sky, distant thunder, sudden wind increase, temperature drop, fog rolling in)
- Have an exit plan — know how to get back to shore or safety quickly if weather deteriorates

**If weather turns bad:**
- Head back to shore or shelter immediately — do not wait
- Reel in your line and secure your equipment before moving
- Stay low to avoid lightning attraction
- Stay together with your buddy
- Seek shelter in a car or building if lightning threatens

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
If you hear thunder, lightning is close enough to strike you. If you see lightning, take shelter immediately. Do not wait for rain. Do not assume that because it is not raining where you are, you are safe — lightning can strike from storms miles away.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Safety Practice 4: Inspect Your Equipment Before Each Trip

Equipment failure can cause accidents. A broken reel can jam, a damaged rod can snap and splinter, and worn line can break, sending a lure flying. Taking five minutes to inspect your tackle can prevent injury.

**What to inspect:**
- **Rod and reel condition** — Look for cracks, splintering, loose parts, and damage
- **Line integrity** — Check for fraying, weak spots, discoloration, or excessive wear. Test by pulling hard
- **Hooks and lures** — Make sure barbs are intact and hooks are not rusted or dull
- **Reel function** — Engage and disengage the bail, check that the reel turns smoothly
- **Knots** — Examine any knots for slippage or damage
- **PFD condition** — Make sure straps work and there are no tears or damage

**Equipment to replace:**
- Line that is more than a year old or showing wear
- Rods or reels with visible damage
- Hooks that are rusty, dull, or damaged
- Any equipment that does not function smoothly

{{< drg/checklist title="Equipment Inspection Checklist" subtitle="Perform before every fishing trip" >}}
- Rod and reel are intact with no cracks or damage
- Fishing line is strong and shows no weak spots or fraying
- Hooks are sharp and barbs are intact
- Reel functions smoothly (spool turns, bail engages and disengages)
- Knots are tied correctly and not slipping
- PFD is intact and fits properly
- First-aid kit is stocked and accessible
- Lures are organized and hooks are capped
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

## Safety Practice 5: Wear Appropriate Clothing and Protection

Proper clothing protects you from sun, insects, cold, and injury. Fishing often means sitting still for hours, so your body temperature can drop even on moderately warm days. You are also exposed to UV rays reflected off the water.

**Year-round protection:**
- **Sunscreen** — Apply SPF 30+ sunscreen 15 minutes before going out and reapply every 2 hours
- **Hat** — A wide-brimmed hat shades your face and neck
- **Sunglasses** — UV-protective sunglasses protect your eyes from sun and glare off the water
- **Long sleeves and pants** — Loose-fitting, light-colored clothing protects your skin and provides insect protection

**Cold-weather protection:**
- **Layering** — Wear moisture-wicking layers under an insulating jacket and waterproof outer layer
- **Gloves** — Neoprene or wool gloves keep your hands warm while allowing you to fish
- **Warm hat** — A significant amount of body heat escapes from your head
- **Extra dry clothes** — Pack a complete change of clothes in a waterproof bag

**Warm-weather protection:**
- **Light colors** — White and light colors reflect heat; dark colors absorb it
- **Loose fit** — Loose clothing allows air circulation and cooling
- **Insect repellent** — DEET repellent protects against mosquitoes and ticks
- **Footwear** — Wear sturdy, closed-toe shoes or wading boots to protect against sharp rocks, shells, and aquatic hazards

{{< drg/tip >}}
Wading boots with felt soles are designed specifically for fishing in rivers and streams. They provide excellent grip on slippery rocks and keep your feet warm in cold water. They are a worthwhile investment if you plan to do much wading.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Making Safety Automatic

These five practices should become habits, so automatic that you follow them without thinking. Start small:

**This trip:**
- Wear your PFD
- Fish with a buddy
- Check the weather before you go

**Next trip:**
- Inspect all your equipment before leaving home
- Check the weather and watch for warning signs

**After that:**
- Make sure you are wearing appropriate clothing and protection
- Review all five practices and commit to them becoming habit
