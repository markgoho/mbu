{{< drg/requirement number="5" >}}
Name and identify five basic artificial lures and five natural baits and explain how to fish with them. Explain why baitfish are not to be released.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Fish eat. Understanding what they eat — and how to imitate it or provide it — is the fundamental skill of fishing. Whether you use artificial lures or natural baits, your success depends on understanding why fish bite and how to present your offering in a way that triggers that response.

## Five Basic Artificial Lures

Artificial lures imitate the natural prey of fish. They work by triggering a fish's instinct to chase or strike at something that looks alive and vulnerable.

### 1. Crankbait

**What it is:** A hard-bodied lure shaped like a small fish, usually with a lip that makes it dive to a specific depth when retrieved.

**How it works:** The crankbait's shape and diving lip make it move through the water with a wobbling action. This wobble mimics an injured or fleeing baitfish.

**Best for:** Bass, pike, and walleye in lakes and rivers

**How to fish it:**
- Cast it out and retrieve steadily with a smooth, consistent reel speed
- The diving lip creates the wobbling action automatically
- Vary your retrieve speed — slow retrieves for cold water, faster retrieves for warm water
- Experiment with pauses to make it flutter

**Depth:** Typically dives 4–15 feet depending on the lip design

### 2. Spinner

**What it is:** A simple lure with a spinning blade or blades on a shaft with a hook. The blade rotates as you retrieve.

**How it works:** The spinning blade creates vibration and flash that imitates small baitfish. The vibration helps fish locate the lure, especially in murky water.

**Best for:** Trout, bass, and pike in streams and lakes

**How to fish it:**
- Cast and retrieve steadily — the blade spins as you retrieve
- The faster you retrieve, the faster the blade spins
- Works well in moving water like rivers and streams
- Also works as a vertical jigging lure if you hop it up and down

**Advantage:** Works well in all light conditions and murky water

### 3. Soft Plastic Worm or Creature Bait

**What it is:** A flexible, rubber-like lure made to imitate a worm, crawfish, or other creature. It has a simple hook through the center or head.

**How it works:** The soft plastic looks and feels like real prey. Many soft plastics are "scent-infused" with attractants that appeal to fish.

**Best for:** Bass, catfish, and bottom-feeding species

**How to fish it:**
- Cast near structure (weeds, rocks, sunken trees) where fish hide
- Retrieve slowly with a dragging or hopping motion across the bottom
- Let it sit periodically — sometimes a slow, stationary lure is most effective
- Texas rig (hook through the head with the weight sliding on the line) is a popular setup

**Presentation:** Often fished slowly and deliberately near the bottom

### 4. Topwater Lure (Popper or Walker)

**What it is:** A floating lure that sits on the water's surface. When you twitch it, it creates splashes and commotion.

**How it works:** The splashing and popping sounds imitate prey on the surface. This triggers aggressive strikes from fish that hunt by sight and sound.

**Best for:** Bass and pike, especially in low-light conditions

**How to fish it:**
- Cast it out and let it sit for a moment
- Use quick, sharp twitches of the rod to create splashes
- Pause between twitches to let the water settle
- The commotion brings fish from a distance to investigate

**Best time:** Early morning, late evening, or overcast days when fish are more active on the surface

### 5. Jig

**What it is:** A weighted hook with a fuzzy or rubber skirt. The weight is built into the lure head, giving it a distinctive shape.

**How it works:** The jig sinks through the water. When you retrieve it with an up-and-down hopping motion, the skirt flutters, imitating a wounded baitfish or crawfish.

**Best for:** Bass, catfish, and walleye in lakes and rivers

**How to fish it:**
- Cast and allow it to sink (count as it drops to control depth)
- Retrieve with a hopping motion — reel, lift the rod tip, lower the rod, repeat
- Pause on the bottom periodically — many strikes happen when the jig sits still
- Use different retrieves — some fish prefer steady hops, others prefer pauses

**Versatility:** Works at any depth and is highly effective year-round

## Five Natural Baits

Natural baits appeal to a fish's natural feeding instincts. Fish have evolved to recognize and chase live prey, making natural baits highly effective.

### 1. Earthworms (Worms)

**What they are:** Common garden earthworms or specialized fishing worms (like nightcrawlers).

**How to use them:**
- Thread the worm onto the hook — hook it once near the head so it remains alive and wiggles
- Use a small to medium hook (sizes 6–2)
- Fish near the bottom or suspend on a bobber
- The worm's natural movement attracts fish

**Best for:** Panfish, trout, catfish, and most freshwater species

**Why they work:** Fish instinctively recognize worms as food. The movement triggers feeding behavior.

**Advantage:** Inexpensive, widely available, and work in almost any situation

### 2. Minnows

**What they are:** Small live fish (like shiners, shad, or herring) purchased from bait and tackle shops.

**How to use them:**
- Hook them through the back or nose to keep them alive and active
- Fish them near structure where larger predator fish hunt
- Use a larger hook (sizes 2–2/0) for bigger minnows
- Can be fished on a bobber, weighted, or free-lined

**Best for:** Bass, pike, trout, and other predatory fish

**Why they work:** Predatory fish hunt other fish naturally. A live, struggling minnow triggers predatory strikes.

**Advantage:** Highly effective for large fish; natural presentation in their world

### 3. Crickets and Grasshoppers

**What they are:** Live insects purchased from bait shops or caught in the field.

**How to use them:**
- Hook them gently through the back to keep them alive
- Use a small hook (sizes 8–4)
- Fish them on the surface or subsurface
- The hopping and movement on the water attract strikes

**Best for:** Trout, panfish, and smallmouth bass

**Why they work:** Fish eat insects naturally. Crickets and grasshoppers look and move like real food.

**Advantage:** Work especially well in streams and rivers with rocky bottoms

### 4. Crayfish (Crawdads)

**What they are:** Small freshwater crustaceans that live on the bottom of streams and lakes.

**How to use them:**
- Hook them through the tail or back
- Fish them on the bottom where crayfish naturally live
- Keep them moving slightly to appear alive
- Use a medium hook (sizes 4–1)

**Best for:** Bass, walleye, catfish, and trout

**Why they work:** Crayfish are a primary food source for many fish species. The natural color and movement trigger strikes.

**Advantage:** Highly effective, especially in their native habitats (rocky, flowing water)

### 5. Cut Bait

**What it is:** Fresh or frozen fish cut into chunks and used as bait. Common cut baits include herring, shad, mackerel, or catfish.

**How to use it:**
- Cut fresh fish into chunks (2–3 inches) with the skin on
- Hook through the skin or body
- Fish it on the bottom in deeper water
- The scent disperses through the water, attracting fish

**Best for:** Catfish, pike, and large bottom-feeding species

**Why it works:** The scent trail leads fish to the bait. Cut bait is especially effective in murky water.

**Advantage:** Highly scent-effective, inexpensive, and works for large catfish and pike

## Why Baitfish Cannot Be Released

The requirement asks you to explain why baitfish are not to be released. This is an important conservation principle.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Never release live baitfish into waters where you are fishing or into local waterways. Baitfish species often come from other regions or even different continents. Releasing them can destroy native fish populations and the entire ecosystem.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

**Reasons baitfish should not be released:**

**Invasive species problem:** Baitfish that escape or are released often become invasive species in local waterways. For example, grass carp, imported as aquatic vegetation control, have become major pests in many states.

**Competition with native species:** Non-native baitfish compete with native fish for food and habitat. They can outcompete locals because they have no natural predators in their new environment.

**Disease transmission:** Imported baitfish can carry parasites or diseases that native fish have never encountered. An outbreak can devastate local populations.

**Genetic pollution:** If the baitfish species is native but from a distant region, escaped fish can breed with local populations and genetically alter them.

**Ecosystem disruption:** The introduction of a new species can have unpredictable effects on an entire aquatic ecosystem — affecting plants, invertebrates, and all the fish that depend on them.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The zebra mussel, originally from Eastern Europe, was likely introduced to North American waters in ballast water from ships. It has since spread through waterways across the continent, damaging dams, pipes, and ecosystems. It is now illegal to transport live zebra mussels across state lines.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

**What to do with unused baitfish:**
- **Use them all.** Buy only the baitfish you will use during your trip.
- **Dispose of them responsibly.** Check local regulations, but typically you should bury dead baitfish away from the water or dispose of them in a trash can.
- **Never release them.** Even if they are the same species as native fish, releasing baitfish can spread disease or disrupt local populations.

## Bait vs. Lure Selection

| Factor | Natural Bait | Artificial Lure |
|--------|--------------|-----------------|
| **Effectiveness** | Higher in unfamiliar water | Requires more skill |
| **Skill needed** | Less — bait attracts on its own | More — presentation matters |
| **Cost** | Ongoing (must replace bait) | One-time (reusable) |
| **Environment** | Some damage to ecosystem | Minimal impact |
| **Learning** | Start with bait to learn basics | Progress to lures as skills develop |
| **Situation** | Better in muddy or murky water | Better in clear water with visibility |
