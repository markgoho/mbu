## Req 8a — Reading the Meter

{{< drg/requirement number="8a" >}}
Read a meter associated with an electric bill. Determine the total power used since the bill, and the cost of that power.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Your electric meter is mounted on the outside of your home (or sometimes in a basement or utility room). It measures how much electrical energy your home consumes, and the utility company reads it to calculate your bill.

### Types of Meters

**Digital meters** display the reading directly as a number — easy to read. Most modern meters are digital and may transmit readings wirelessly to the utility (called "smart meters").

**Analog meters** have four or five dials with numbers 0–9. Each dial spins in the opposite direction from the one next to it. To read an analog meter, write down the number each dial has most recently passed (if the pointer is between two numbers, use the lower one).

### Calculating Power Used

Your meter reads in **kilowatt-hours (kWh)** — the standard unit for measuring electrical energy consumption. To calculate how much power you have used since your last bill:

**kWh used = Current meter reading − Previous meter reading**

Your previous meter reading is printed on your last electric bill.

### Calculating the Cost

Multiply the kWh used by your utility's rate (also on your bill, usually listed as "price per kWh"):

**Cost = kWh used x Price per kWh**

For example: If you used 900 kWh and the rate is $0.12/kWh:
900 x $0.12 = **$108.00** for electricity usage.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Read your meter on the same day each week for a month and track the daily average usage. You might be surprised by how much usage varies — running the AC in summer or the heater in winter can double or triple your daily consumption.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Req 8b — Taxes and Fees

{{< drg/requirement number="8b" >}}
Explain other charges on the bill that were taxes or fees.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Your electric bill includes more than just the cost of the electricity you consumed. Look for these additional charges:

- **Delivery charges / Distribution fees** — The cost of maintaining the power lines, poles, transformers, and other infrastructure that delivers electricity from the power plant to your home.
- **Generation charges** — Sometimes separated from delivery, this is the cost of actually producing the electricity at the power plant.
- **Demand charges** — Some plans charge extra for peak usage (the highest amount of power drawn at any one moment during the billing period).
- **Fuel adjustment charges** — A variable charge that rises or falls depending on the cost of fuel (natural gas, coal, oil) used by the power plant.
- **Renewable energy surcharge** — A small fee that funds your state's renewable energy programs.
- **State and local taxes** — Sales tax, utility tax, or franchise fees charged by your state, county, or city.
- **Regulatory fees** — Small charges that fund the state public utility commission, which regulates your utility company.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
In many states, you can choose your electricity supplier (the company that generates the electricity) separately from your utility (the company that delivers it). This is called "energy deregulation," and it means you can shop around for the best rate — but the delivery charges remain the same regardless of which supplier you choose.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Req 8c — Energy Conservation

{{< drg/requirement number="8c" >}}
Discuss with your counselor five ways your family can conserve energy.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Conserving electricity saves money, reduces pollution from power plants, and extends the life of the electrical grid. Here are practical strategies you can discuss with your counselor — and implement at home.

### 1. Switch to LED Lighting

LED bulbs use about 75% less energy than incandescent bulbs and last 25 times longer. Replacing the five most-used light fixtures in your home with LEDs can save about $75 per year.

### 2. Manage Heating and Cooling

Heating and cooling account for about 50% of a typical home's energy use. Set the thermostat to 68°F in winter and 78°F in summer. A programmable or smart thermostat that adjusts automatically when you are asleep or away can save 10–15% on heating and cooling costs.

### 3. Eliminate Phantom Loads

Many electronics draw power even when turned off — TVs, game consoles, chargers, and cable boxes. This "phantom" or "vampire" power can account for 5–10% of your electric bill. Unplug devices when not in use, or plug them into a power strip you can switch off.

### 4. Use Appliances Efficiently

- Run the dishwasher and washing machine only with full loads.
- Wash clothes in cold water — heating the water accounts for about 90% of a washing machine's energy use.
- Clean the dryer lint filter before every load — a clogged filter makes the dryer work harder and longer.
- Keep your refrigerator at 37°F and your freezer at 0°F.

### 5. Seal Air Leaks

Drafts around windows, doors, and electrical outlets let conditioned air escape. Weatherstripping and caulk are inexpensive and can reduce heating and cooling costs by 10–20%. On a cold day, hold your hand near window frames and outlet plates — if you feel cold air, that is a leak worth sealing.

{{< drg/checklist title="Energy Conservation Actions" subtitle="Five practical ways to reduce electricity use" >}}
- Replace incandescent bulbs with LED bulbs in your most-used fixtures.
- Set thermostats to energy-saving temperatures and use a programmable schedule.
- Unplug electronics or use switchable power strips to eliminate phantom loads.
- Run dishwashers and washers only when full; use cold water for laundry.
- Seal air leaks around windows, doors, and outlets with weatherstripping and caulk.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}
