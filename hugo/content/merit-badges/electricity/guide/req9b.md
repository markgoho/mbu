Units of measure - Ampere (amps), ohms, volts, watts, and watt-hours
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

In [Req 9a](/merit-badges/electricity/guide/req9a/), you learned the concepts — current, resistance, voltage, power, and energy. Now let's look at the specific units we use to measure them. Each unit is named after a pioneer who helped unlock the science of electricity.

## Ampere (Amps, A)

The **ampere** measures electric current — the rate of electron flow through a conductor. One ampere equals approximately 6.24 x 10^18 electrons passing a point in one second. That is 6.24 quintillion electrons — an incomprehensibly large number, flowing past in a single second.

**Named after:** Andre-Marie Ampere (1775–1836), a French physicist who developed the mathematical framework for electromagnetism.

**Everyday examples:**
- Phone charger: 1–2 A
- Table lamp: 0.5 A
- Microwave: 8–10 A
- Electric dryer: 24 A (on a 240V circuit)

## Ohm (Ohms, Ω)

The **ohm** measures electrical resistance — how much a material opposes the flow of current. A conductor with 1 ohm of resistance allows 1 ampere of current to flow when 1 volt is applied.

**Named after:** Georg Simon Ohm (1789–1854), the German physicist who discovered the relationship between voltage, current, and resistance (Ohm's Law).

**Everyday examples:**
- Copper wire (1 foot): about 0.003 Ω
- Toaster heating element: 8–15 Ω
- Human skin (dry): 1,000–100,000 Ω
- Rubber insulation: millions of Ω

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Wet skin has dramatically lower resistance than dry skin — as low as 500 ohms compared to 100,000 ohms when dry. This is why electrical accidents near water are so much more deadly. The same voltage that would give you a mild tingle with dry hands can deliver a fatal shock if your hands are wet.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Volt (Volts, V)

The **volt** measures voltage — the electrical pressure or potential difference that pushes electrons through a circuit. One volt is the pressure needed to push one ampere of current through one ohm of resistance.

**Named after:** Alessandro Volta (1745–1827), the Italian physicist who invented the first true battery (the voltaic pile).

**Everyday examples:**
- AA battery: 1.5 V
- Car battery: 12 V
- U.S. wall outlet: 120 V
- Electric dryer outlet: 240 V
- Residential power line: 7,200 V
- High-voltage transmission line: 115,000–765,000 V

## Watt (Watts, W)

The **watt** measures power — the rate at which electrical energy is consumed or produced. One watt equals one volt times one ampere (P = V x I). It tells you how fast a device is using energy.

**Named after:** James Watt (1736–1819), the Scottish inventor who improved the steam engine. Although Watt worked with steam, not electricity, his work on measuring power was so fundamental that the unit of electrical power bears his name.

**Everyday examples:**
- LED bulb: 8–12 W
- Laptop: 30–65 W
- Desktop computer: 200–500 W
- Microwave: 700–1,200 W
- Space heater: 1,500 W
- Electric oven: 2,000–5,000 W

## Watt-Hour (Wh) and Kilowatt-Hour (kWh)

The **watt-hour** measures energy — the total amount of electricity consumed over time. One watt-hour is the energy used by a one-watt device running for one hour. Since watt-hours are very small, your electric bill uses **kilowatt-hours** (1 kWh = 1,000 Wh).

**Everyday examples:**
- Charging a phone: about 0.01 kWh
- Running a laptop for 8 hours: about 0.3 kWh
- Running a window AC for 1 hour: about 1.2 kWh
- Average U.S. home per month: about 886 kWh

## Quick Reference Table

| Unit | Measures | Symbol | Named After |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ampere | Current (electron flow) | A | Andre-Marie Ampere |
| Ohm | Resistance | Ω | Georg Simon Ohm |
| Volt | Voltage (pressure) | V | Alessandro Volta |
| Watt | Power (rate of energy use) | W | James Watt |
| Watt-hour | Energy (total use over time) | Wh | James Watt |

{{< drg/tip >}}
Here is a quick way to remember the relationship: **Volts push, Amps flow, Ohms resist, Watts work.** Voltage is the pressure, current is what flows, resistance is what fights the flow, and power is the work that gets done as a result.
{{< /drg/tip >}}
