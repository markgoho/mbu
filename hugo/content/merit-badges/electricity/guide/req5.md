For this requirement, you will create two drawings — one showing how a battery works and another showing how an electric bell works. Before you put pencil to paper, let's make sure you understand what each component does and why it matters.

## Inside a Battery

A battery is a self-contained chemical power source. The simplest version — a single electrochemical cell — has just four essential components.

### Components of a Battery

**1. Negative Electrode (Anode)**
This is the electrode where the chemical reaction releases electrons. In a standard alkaline battery, the anode is made of zinc. As the zinc reacts with the electrolyte, it gives up electrons that want to flow to the other side.

**2. Positive Electrode (Cathode)**
This is the electrode where electrons are consumed by a chemical reaction. In an alkaline battery, the cathode is manganese dioxide. It accepts the electrons that flow through the external circuit.

**3. Electrolyte**
The electrolyte is a chemical paste or solution that allows ions (charged atoms) to flow between the two electrodes inside the battery. It completes the internal circuit. In an alkaline battery, the electrolyte is potassium hydroxide. Without the electrolyte, the chemical reactions at the electrodes would stop because the internal circuit would be broken.

**4. Separator**
A thin barrier between the anode and cathode prevents them from touching each other directly (which would short-circuit the battery and waste all the energy as heat). The separator allows ions to pass through but keeps the electrodes physically apart.

**How it works:** The chemical reaction at the anode produces electrons. These electrons cannot travel through the electrolyte (the separator blocks them), so they are forced to travel through the external circuit — the wire connecting the two terminals — to reach the cathode. That flow of electrons through the wire is the electric current that powers your device.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The very first battery was built by Alessandro Volta in 1800. He stacked alternating discs of copper and zinc separated by cardboard soaked in saltwater. This "voltaic pile" produced a steady electric current for the first time in history — and it is why we call the unit of electrical potential a "volt."
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Inside an Electric Bell

An electric bell is a clever mechanical device that uses electromagnetism to create a repeating hammering motion. It is essentially a self-interrupting circuit.

### Components of an Electric Bell

**1. Electromagnet**
A coil of wire wrapped around an iron core. When current flows through the coil, it becomes magnetized and pulls the armature toward it. This is the same principle you explored in [Req 3](/merit-badges/electricity/guide/req3/).

**2. Armature (Striker Arm)**
A thin, flexible strip of iron attached to a hammer (the part that hits the bell). The armature is positioned near the electromagnet. When the electromagnet energizes, the armature is pulled toward it.

**3. Contact Screw (Contact Point)**
A small metal screw that touches the armature when the armature is in its resting position. This contact completes the circuit, allowing current to flow to the electromagnet. When the armature moves toward the electromagnet, it pulls away from the contact screw and breaks the circuit.

**4. Bell (Gong)**
The metal dome that the hammer strikes to produce the ringing sound.

**5. Spring (Return Mechanism)**
A spring or the natural springiness of the armature itself pushes the armature back to its resting position after the electromagnet releases it.

### The Cycle

Here is where it gets clever:

1. You press the button, completing the circuit. Current flows through the electromagnet.
2. The electromagnet pulls the armature toward it. The hammer strikes the bell — *ding!*
3. But when the armature moves, it separates from the contact screw, breaking the circuit.
4. With no current, the electromagnet loses its magnetism and releases the armature.
5. The spring pushes the armature back to its resting position, where it touches the contact screw again.
6. The circuit is complete again, and the cycle repeats — *ding! ding! ding!*

This happens many times per second, creating the continuous ringing sound you hear when someone presses a doorbell.

{{< drg/tip >}}
When you make your drawings, label every component clearly and use arrows to show the direction of current flow. Color-code the circuit path if you can — it makes the drawing easier to follow. Your counselor will want you to explain what each part does, so practice your explanation before the meeting.
