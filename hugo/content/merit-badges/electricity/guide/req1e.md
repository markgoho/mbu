An electrical burn is not like a thermal burn from touching a hot stove. When current passes through the body, it heats tissue along its entire path — from the entry point through muscles, blood vessels, and nerves to the exit point. The surface burn you see may be a small, charred spot, but underneath, the damage can extend deep into the body. This is why electrical burns always require professional medical evaluation, even when they look minor.

## Types of Electrical Burns

### Contact Burns

These occur where the current enters and exits the body. The entry point (usually the hand) often looks like a small, whitish-gray or charred wound. The exit point (often the foot or knee) may look similar. Between these two points, internal tissue may be severely damaged.

### Arc Burns

When a high-voltage electrical arc jumps through the air to a person, it can reach temperatures above 5,000 degrees Fahrenheit — hotter than the surface of the sun. Arc burns produce deep thermal injuries to the skin and can ignite clothing, causing additional flame burns.

### Flash Burns

These are surface burns caused by the intense heat and light of an electrical arc, without the current actually passing through the body. They resemble sunburns and can affect the eyes (arc eye) as well as exposed skin.

## First Aid for Electrical Burns

### Step 1: Ensure Safety

Before treating any burn, confirm the victim is no longer connected to the electrical source. Review the separation techniques from [Req 1b](/merit-badges/electricity/guide/req1b/).

### Step 2: Call 911

All electrical burns require emergency medical care. Even a small-looking contact burn can mask serious internal injuries. Call 911 or have someone else call immediately.

### Step 3: Treat the Burn

- **Do not** apply ice, cold water, butter, ointment, or any home remedy to an electrical burn. Unlike thermal burns, cooling with water does not help because the damage is internal.
- **Do not** remove clothing that is stuck to the burn — you may tear away skin.
- **Cover** the burn loosely with a clean, dry, sterile dressing or a clean cloth. Do not wrap it tightly — the tissue may swell.
- **Elevate** burned arms or legs above the level of the heart, if possible, to reduce swelling.

### Step 4: Monitor the Victim

While waiting for emergency responders:

- Watch for signs of shock (pale skin, rapid pulse, confusion) — treat as described in [Req 1d](/merit-badges/electricity/guide/req1d/).
- Keep the person calm and still.
- Do not give food or drink.
- If the person stops breathing, begin CPR.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Never underestimate an electrical burn. A tiny entry wound on a finger can mean the current traveled through the arm, across the chest, and out through a foot — damaging muscle, nerves, and blood vessels the entire way. The victim needs a hospital, even if they say they feel fine.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Electrical burns can cause a condition called rhabdomyolysis, where damaged muscle tissue releases a protein called myoglobin into the bloodstream. Myoglobin can clog the kidneys and cause kidney failure. This is one reason hospitals monitor electrical burn patients for several days — the worst effects may not appear until 24 to 48 hours after the injury.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

{{< drg/checklist title="Electrical Burn Treatment" subtitle="What to do and what to avoid" >}}
- Confirm the victim is separated from the electrical source.
- Call 911 immediately — all electrical burns need medical evaluation.
- Cover burns loosely with a dry, sterile dressing.
- Do not apply ice, water, butter, or ointments.
- Do not remove clothing stuck to the burn.
- Elevate burned extremities above heart level.
- Monitor for signs of shock and treat accordingly.
- Watch for breathing problems and be ready to perform CPR.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}
