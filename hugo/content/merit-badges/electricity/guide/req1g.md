{{< drg/requirement number="1g" >}}
Explain what to do if caught out in the open during an electrical storm.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A lightning bolt carries up to 300 million volts and heats the air around it to 30,000 degrees Fahrenheit — five times hotter than the surface of the sun. Lightning kills about 20 people per year in the United States and injures hundreds more. As a Scout who spends time outdoors, you need to know exactly what to do when a thunderstorm catches you in the open.

## The 30-30 Rule

Before the storm arrives, use the **30-30 Rule** to decide when to seek shelter:

- **First 30:** When you see lightning, count the seconds until you hear thunder. If the count is 30 seconds or less, the storm is within 6 miles and you should take shelter immediately.
- **Second 30:** After the last flash of lightning, wait 30 minutes before leaving shelter. Lightning can strike from a storm that appears to be moving away.

## If You Are Caught in the Open

Sometimes there is no building or vehicle to shelter in. If a thunderstorm catches you exposed, follow these guidelines:

### Move to Lower Ground

Lightning tends to strike the highest point in an area. If you are on a ridge, hilltop, or open field, move to a lower area. A valley, a ditch, or a depression in the terrain is better than being the tallest thing around.

### Avoid Tall, Isolated Objects

Stay away from:

- Lone trees (lightning strikes the tallest object)
- Metal fences, poles, and towers
- Bodies of water (lakes, rivers, puddles)
- Open fields where you are the tallest object

### Avoid Groups

If you are with other Scouts, spread out at least 50 feet apart. If lightning strikes near the group, spacing out reduces the chance that multiple people are injured.

### Assume the Lightning Crouch

If you feel your hair stand on end, hear a crackling sound, or see a blue glow (St. Elmo's fire) on metal objects — a lightning strike may be imminent. Drop into the **lightning crouch**:

1. Squat low with your feet together.
2. Tuck your head down.
3. Cover your ears with your hands.
4. Make yourself as small a target as possible.
5. Do NOT lie flat — the more of your body that touches the ground, the more area is exposed to ground current.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
**Never shelter under a lone tree.** A tree is often the tallest object in an area and is likely to be struck. When lightning hits a tree, the current can jump from the tree to anyone standing nearby. If you are in a forest, seek shelter among shorter trees in a dense stand — not under the tallest one.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Best Shelters

The safest place during a thunderstorm is inside a substantial building — one with plumbing and wiring that can conduct lightning safely to the ground. The next best option is a hard-topped vehicle (car, truck, van) with the windows closed. The metal body of the vehicle acts as a Faraday cage, directing the current around the outside and into the ground through the tires.

**These are NOT safe shelters:**
- Tents, lean-tos, or open pavilions
- Convertible cars
- Golf carts
- Picnic shelters with no walls

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Lightning does not need to strike you directly to hurt you. About 50% of lightning fatalities are caused by ground current — electricity spreading through the ground from a nearby strike. Side flashes (current jumping from a struck object to a person) and contact voltage (current flowing through a metal object you are touching) account for most of the rest.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Storm Rolls In During a Hike" >}}
Your patrol is hiking a ridge trail when you hear thunder in the distance. You count 25 seconds between the flash and the boom.

- **The storm is about 5 miles away** — within striking range. Act now.
- **Descend immediately.** Get off the ridge and move to lower ground.
- **Spread out** at least 50 feet apart to reduce group risk.
- **Ditch metal.** Set down trekking poles, metal-frame packs, and any other metal objects. Move at least 50 feet away from them.
- **If you cannot reach a building or car**, crouch low in a depression or valley with your feet together.
- **Wait 30 minutes** after the last flash of lightning before resuming your hike.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}
