{{< drg/requirement number="10" >}}
Do TWO of the following:
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This is where your electricity knowledge becomes physical. You will choose **two** of the six projects below, build them, and demonstrate that they work. Each project reinforces different concepts you have learned throughout this badge.

Read through all six options and pick the two that interest you most. If you are unsure, talk with your counselor — they may have materials available or recommendations based on what works well.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
All of these projects use low-voltage DC power (batteries). They are safe for Scouts to build without supervision, but always follow basic precautions: do not short-circuit batteries (they can overheat), do not connect homemade devices to wall outlets, and wear safety glasses when cutting wire.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Option A: Buzzer, Bell, or Light Circuit

{{< drg/requirement number="10a" >}}
Connect a buzzer, bell, or light with a battery. Have a key or switch in the line.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This is the most straightforward project — build a complete working circuit with a power source, a load, and a switch. It demonstrates the fundamental principle that a circuit must be closed (complete) for current to flow.

**Materials:** A D-cell battery (or battery holder), insulated wire, a small buzzer or flashlight bulb with holder, and a simple switch (you can buy one or make one from a clothespin and two thumbtacks).

**Key concepts:** Closed vs. open circuit, the role of each component (source, conductor, load, switch).

## Option B: Simple Electric Motor

{{< drg/requirement number="10b" >}}
Make and run a simple electric motor (from a kit is acceptable, if approved by your counselor ahead of time).
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

An electric motor converts electrical energy into mechanical (spinning) motion using the interaction between current-carrying wire and a magnetic field. Building one shows how the electromagnet you made in [Req 3](/merit-badges/electricity/guide/req3/) becomes the basis for machines that drive the modern world.

**Materials:** Magnet wire (enameled copper wire), a D-cell battery, two large paper clips (as supports), a small neodymium or ceramic magnet, and tape.

**The basic design:** Coil the magnet wire into a tight loop, leaving two straight ends as the axle. Rest the axle in paper clip supports attached to the battery. Sand the enamel off one side of each axle end (this acts as a commutator, allowing current only during half the rotation). Place a magnet under the coil — the coil should spin.

**Key concepts:** Electromagnetic force, commutation, conversion of electrical to mechanical energy.

{{< drg/tip >}}
The trickiest part of a homemade motor is sanding the enamel correctly. Sand all the enamel off one axle end, but only sand the top half of the other end. This creates a half-commutator — the coil gets a push from the magnetic field during half the rotation and coasts through the other half, keeping it spinning.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Option C: Simple Rheostat

{{< drg/requirement number="10c" >}}
Build a simple rheostat. Show that it works.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A rheostat is a variable resistor — by adjusting it, you control how much current flows in a circuit. Building one demonstrates the relationship between resistance and current described by Ohm's Law.

**Materials:** A wooden board, a pencil lead (graphite rod from a mechanical pencil or graphite from an art supply store), wire, a D-cell battery, a small light bulb with holder, and an alligator clip.

**The basic design:** Graphite has moderate resistance. Lay the graphite rod on the board and connect one end to the circuit. Use an alligator clip on a wire to contact the graphite at different points along its length. Moving the clip closer to the battery end shortens the graphite path (less resistance, brighter light). Moving it farther lengthens the path (more resistance, dimmer light).

**Key concepts:** Variable resistance, Ohm's Law in action, how dimmers work.

## Option D: Single-Pole, Double-Throw Switch

{{< drg/requirement number="10d" >}}
Build a single-pole, double-throw switch. Show that it works.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A **single-pole, double-throw (SPDT)** switch connects one input to one of two possible outputs. It is used in circuits where you want to select between two paths — like switching between two lights or two circuits.

**Materials:** A wooden base, three small screws or nails, a metal strip or heavy wire (the toggle), wire, a battery, and two small light bulbs.

**The basic design:** Mount three contacts on the base. The center contact is the "pole" (input). The two outer contacts are the "throws" (outputs). The metal strip pivots on the center contact and swings to touch either the left or the right output, completing that circuit and lighting the corresponding bulb.

**Key concepts:** Circuit switching, single-pole vs. double-pole, how selector switches work.

## Option E: 3-Way Switch Wiring

{{< drg/requirement number="10e" >}}
Explain how 3-way switch wiring works in a lighting circuit.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Have you ever noticed that some lights in your home can be controlled from two different switches — like the light at the top and bottom of a stairway? That is a 3-way switch circuit. Despite the name, a "3-way switch" has only three terminals (not three positions).

**How it works:** Two 3-way switches are connected by "traveler" wires. Each switch can connect the common terminal to one of two traveler wires. The light turns on when both switches connect to the same traveler wire (completing the circuit) and turns off when they connect to different traveler wires (breaking the circuit). Flipping either switch changes the state — on becomes off, off becomes on — regardless of the other switch's position.

**Key concepts:** Traveler wires, common terminal, how the light's state can be toggled from either location.

## Option F: Series vs. Parallel Circuits

{{< drg/requirement number="10f" >}}
Connect two lights together in a series circuit along with a battery and a switch. Then connect the same circuit in parallel. Discuss the differences in the two circuits.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This project makes the difference between series and parallel circuits visually obvious.

**Series circuit:** The two bulbs are connected end-to-end in a single loop. All the current flows through both bulbs. The bulbs will be **dimmer** than normal because they share the voltage, and if one bulb burns out, the other goes dark too (the circuit is broken).

**Parallel circuit:** Each bulb is connected directly to the battery in its own branch. Each bulb gets the full voltage. The bulbs will be **brighter** (normal brightness), and if one burns out, the other stays lit because its branch is still complete.

**Key concepts:** Series vs. parallel, voltage division vs. current division, why homes use parallel wiring (so that one blown bulb does not kill every light in the house).

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Every outlet in your home is wired in parallel. This is why unplugging the toaster does not turn off the kitchen lights. The original Christmas tree lights, invented in the 1880s, were wired in series — when one bulb burned out, the entire string went dark, and you had to test each bulb to find the dead one.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}
