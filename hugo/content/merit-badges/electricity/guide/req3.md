Make a simple electromagnet and use it to show magnetic attraction and repulsion.

In 1820, Danish physicist Hans Christian Oersted noticed something strange during a lecture: a compass needle moved when he turned on an electric current nearby. He had accidentally discovered that electricity creates magnetism. This discovery — that electric current produces a magnetic field — is the foundation of electric motors, generators, speakers, MRI machines, and the hard drives that store your data.

## How an Electromagnet Works

A permanent magnet (like a refrigerator magnet) is always magnetic. An electromagnet is magnetic only when current flows through it — turn off the current and the magnetism disappears. This on-off control is what makes electromagnets so useful.

Here is the basic principle: when electric current flows through a wire, it creates a weak magnetic field around the wire. If you coil the wire into a tight spiral, the magnetic fields from each loop add together, creating a stronger field. Wrap that coil around an iron core (like a nail), and the iron amplifies the field dramatically because iron is easily magnetized.

The strength of your electromagnet depends on three factors:

1. **Number of coil turns** — More turns of wire = stronger magnet
2. **Amount of current** — More current (amps) = stronger magnet
3. **Core material** — Iron or steel cores amplify the field much more than air or wood

## Building Your Electromagnet

### Materials

- One large iron nail (3–4 inches long)
- About 3 feet of insulated copper wire (18–22 gauge magnet wire works well)
- One D-cell battery (1.5V)
- Small metal objects to test: paper clips, staples, pins, small nails

### Steps

1. **Wind the wire** — Start about 6 inches from one end of the wire. Wrap the wire tightly around the nail in a single layer, making as many turns as you can. Keep the coils close together and going in the same direction. Leave about 6 inches of wire free at the other end.
2. **Strip the ends** — Use wire strippers or sandpaper to remove about an inch of insulation from each end of the wire, exposing the bare copper.
3. **Connect to the battery** — Touch one bare wire end to the positive terminal (+) of the battery and the other to the negative terminal (−). Hold them in place with tape or your fingers.
4. **Test it** — Hold the nail near a pile of paper clips. They should jump up and cling to the nail. Disconnect the wire from the battery, and the paper clips should fall off.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
The wire and nail will get warm during use because the battery is essentially short-circuiting through the coil's low resistance. Do not leave the circuit connected for more than 30 seconds at a time. Let the nail and wire cool between tests. The battery will also drain quickly.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Showing Attraction and Repulsion

**Magnetic attraction** is easy to demonstrate — your electromagnet picks up paper clips and other iron/steel objects. That is attraction: the magnetic field pulls ferromagnetic materials toward the electromagnet.

**Magnetic repulsion** requires a second magnet. Here is how to show it:

1. Bring a permanent bar magnet or refrigerator magnet near your energized electromagnet.
2. One end of the permanent magnet will be attracted to the electromagnet (the opposite poles attract).
3. Flip the permanent magnet around, and the same end of the electromagnet will now push it away (like poles repel).
4. You can also reverse the polarity of your electromagnet by swapping the battery connections (switch which wire goes to + and which goes to −). The poles of the electromagnet will reverse, and what attracted before will now repel.

{{< drg/tip >}}
To make a stronger electromagnet, try adding more turns of wire (use a longer piece). You can also try using two D-cell batteries in series (end to end in a battery holder) to double the voltage and increase current. More current means a stronger magnetic field. Just be careful — higher current means more heat.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The most powerful electromagnets in the world are used in MRI (Magnetic Resonance Imaging) machines in hospitals. A typical MRI magnet produces a field about 30,000 times stronger than Earth's magnetic field — strong enough to attract a metal chair from across the room at dangerous speed. Hospital staff must remove all metal objects before entering the MRI room.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Real-World Electromagnets

Electromagnets are everywhere, working invisibly:

- **Electric motors** — Inside every fan, washing machine, and electric car, spinning coils create rotating magnetic fields that turn a shaft
- **Speakers and headphones** — A coil vibrates back and forth against a permanent magnet, pushing air to create sound waves
- **Junkyard cranes** — Giant electromagnets pick up crushed cars and drop them by cutting the current
- **Maglev trains** — Electromagnets both levitate the train above the track and propel it forward at speeds over 300 mph
- **Hard drives** — Tiny electromagnets write data by magnetizing microscopic regions on a spinning disk
