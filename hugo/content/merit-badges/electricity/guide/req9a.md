{{< drg/requirement number="9a" >}}
Electrical terms - Current, energy, power, resistance, and voltage
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

These five terms are the building blocks of understanding electricity. They describe what electricity is, how it behaves, and how we measure it. Think of them as the vocabulary you need before you can "speak electricity."

## Current

**Current** is the flow of electric charge (electrons) through a conductor. It is the movement itself — the actual electrons traveling through a wire. Without current, nothing happens. No lights, no motors, no charging.

Think of it like water flowing through a garden hose. The water moving through the hose is like current flowing through a wire. More water flowing per second means a stronger stream. More electrons flowing per second means a higher current.

Current is measured in **amperes** (amps). You will learn more about this unit in [Req 9b](/merit-badges/electricity/guide/req9b/).

## Voltage

**Voltage** is the electrical pressure — the force that pushes electrons through a conductor. Without voltage, electrons sit still. Voltage is what makes current flow, just as water pressure makes water flow through a hose.

A battery's voltage is the "push" it provides. A 1.5V AA battery provides a gentle push. A 120V wall outlet provides a much stronger push. A 765,000V transmission line provides an enormous push — enough to move current hundreds of miles.

Voltage is measured in **volts** (V).

{{< drg/tip >}}
The water analogy is the best way to remember these terms: voltage is the water pressure, current is the flow of water, and resistance is the size of the pipe. A narrow pipe (high resistance) lets less water through (low current), even if the pressure (voltage) is high.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Resistance

**Resistance** is how much a material opposes the flow of current. Every material has some resistance — even copper wire, which is an excellent conductor, has a small amount. Materials with very high resistance (like rubber, glass, and plastic) are called **insulators**. Materials with very low resistance (like copper, aluminum, and silver) are called **conductors**.

Resistance converts electrical energy into heat. This is useful when you want heat (a toaster's heating element has high resistance by design) and dangerous when you do not want it (a loose wire connection has resistance that generates unwanted heat, potentially starting a fire).

Resistance is measured in **ohms**.

## Power

**Power** is the rate at which electrical energy is used or produced. It tells you how much energy a device consumes per second. A 100-watt light bulb converts 100 joules of electrical energy into light and heat every second. A 1,500-watt space heater converts 1,500 joules per second.

Power is calculated with the equation you used in [Req 6b](/merit-badges/electricity/guide/req6b/):

**Power (Watts) = Voltage (Volts) x Current (Amps)**

Power is measured in **watts** (W).

## Energy

**Energy** is the total amount of work electricity does over time. Power tells you the rate; energy tells you the total. If a 100-watt bulb runs for 10 hours, it uses:

100 W x 10 hours = **1,000 watt-hours = 1 kilowatt-hour (kWh)**

This is the unit your utility company charges you for, as you learned in [Req 8](/merit-badges/electricity/guide/req8/). Energy is measured in **watt-hours** or **kilowatt-hours**.

## How They All Connect

These five terms are deeply interconnected through two fundamental laws:

**Ohm's Law:** Voltage = Current x Resistance (V = I x R)
- If you increase voltage, current increases (assuming resistance stays the same).
- If you increase resistance, current decreases (assuming voltage stays the same).

**Power Equation:** Power = Voltage x Current (P = V x I)
- A device drawing more current at the same voltage uses more power.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Georg Simon Ohm published his law relating voltage, current, and resistance in 1827, but the scientific community rejected it for years. Fellow physicists called his work "a tissue of naked fantasy." It took over a decade before Ohm's Law was recognized as one of the most fundamental principles in electrical science. Today, the unit of resistance — the ohm — is named in his honor.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}
