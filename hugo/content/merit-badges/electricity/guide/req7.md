{{< drg/requirement number="7" >}}
Make a floor plan wiring diagram of the lights, switches, and outlets for a room in your home. Show which fuse or circuit breaker protects each one.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Electricians, architects, and building inspectors use wiring diagrams every day. A wiring diagram is a map that shows where every electrical device is located in a room and how they connect to the circuit panel. For this requirement, you will create one for a room in your own home.

## Choosing a Room

Pick a room that has a good variety of electrical devices — a kitchen, living room, or bedroom works well. Avoid bathrooms (usually simple) unless that is all you have. The more outlets, switches, and lights you can include, the more useful the exercise.

## Standard Electrical Symbols

Professional wiring diagrams use standardized symbols so that anyone trained can read them. Here are the ones you need:

| Symbol | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Circle with lines radiating outward | Ceiling light fixture |
| Circle with an F inside | Ceiling fan with light |
| Two parallel lines (=) | Duplex outlet (standard wall outlet) |
| Two parallel lines with GFCI | GFCI-protected outlet |
| S | Single-pole switch (standard light switch) |
| S3 | Three-way switch |
| SD | Dimmer switch |
| A circle with crosshairs | Junction box |

Solid lines between symbols show the wiring path. Dashed lines indicate wires running through the ceiling or floor.

## Step-by-Step Process

### 1. Draw the Floor Plan

On a large piece of paper (graph paper works best), draw the outline of the room to scale. Include:

- Walls, doors, and windows
- Any fixed cabinets or counters (especially in kitchens)
- The approximate dimensions

### 2. Walk the Room

Walk around the room with your drawing and mark the exact location of every:

- Wall outlet
- Light switch
- Ceiling light or fan
- Hardwired appliance (like a dishwasher, garbage disposal, or range hood)

Mark each one on your drawing using the standard symbols.

### 3. Identify the Circuits

This is where your breaker-mapping work from [Req 1a](/merit-badges/electricity/guide/req1a/) and [Req 2](/merit-badges/electricity/guide/req2/) pays off. Turn off breakers one at a time and determine which outlets, lights, and switches lose power. Use a lamp or a plug-in night light to test outlets quickly.

On your diagram, use different colors or labels to show which breaker protects each device:

- Red lines or "CB-1" for breaker #1
- Blue lines or "CB-3" for breaker #3
- And so on

You may find that a single room has outlets on two or three different circuits — especially in kitchens, where the National Electrical Code requires at least two separate 20-amp circuits for countertop outlets.

### 4. Draw the Wiring Paths

Connect the devices on each circuit with lines showing the wiring path. In most homes, wires run through the walls and across the ceiling. Use:

- Solid lines for wires in the walls
- Dashed lines for wires in the ceiling or floor

{{< drg/tip >}}
Use colored pencils for different circuits — it makes the diagram much easier to read. Include a legend in the corner that shows which color represents which breaker number. Your counselor will appreciate a clean, color-coded diagram.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### 5. Add Labels

For each circuit shown, note the breaker number and rating at the panel. Example: "Circuit 7 — 15A" or "Circuit 12 — 20A."

## Sample Layout

A typical bedroom might show:

- 4 duplex outlets (on two walls)
- 1 ceiling light
- 1 single-pole switch (by the door)
- All on Circuit #5 (15A breaker)

A kitchen might show:

- 6 duplex outlets (2 GFCI-protected on the counter)
- 1 ceiling light
- 2 switches (one for the ceiling light, one for the garbage disposal)
- Counter outlets on Circuit #8 (20A)
- Overhead light and remaining outlets on Circuit #14 (15A)
- Dishwasher on Circuit #10 (20A, dedicated)

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The National Electrical Code requires that no point along a wall in a habitable room be more than 6 feet from an outlet. This rule, designed to prevent the overuse of extension cords, is why most bedrooms have outlets on every wall and kitchens have them every 4 feet along the countertop.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}
