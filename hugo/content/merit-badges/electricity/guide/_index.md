Flip a switch and a dark room fills with light. Plug in a charger and your dead phone comes back to life. Electricity is so deeply woven into modern life that most people never think about it — until the power goes out. Then, suddenly, you realize just how much depends on the invisible flow of electrons through the wires in your walls.

The Electricity merit badge pulls back the curtain on the force that powers your world. You will learn how electricity is generated, how it travels from power plants to your home, how the circuits in your house work, and — critically — how to stay safe around a force that can injure or kill in a fraction of a second. By the end, you will be able to build simple electrical devices, read your family's electric bill, and wire a circuit with confidence.

## Then and Now

### Then

Humans have been aware of electricity for thousands of years — ancient Greeks discovered that rubbing amber with fur created a static charge that attracted feathers. But harnessing electricity for practical use is a surprisingly recent achievement. Benjamin Franklin's famous kite experiment in 1752 proved that lightning was electrical in nature. In the 1800s, pioneers like Alessandro Volta (who invented the first true battery), Michael Faraday (who discovered electromagnetic induction), and Thomas Edison and Nikola Tesla (whose rivalry over DC and AC power shaped the modern grid) transformed electricity from a laboratory curiosity into the backbone of civilization.

By the early 1900s, only about 8% of American homes had electricity. Rural electrification did not reach most farms until the 1930s and 1940s. Your great-grandparents may have grown up reading by kerosene lamp.

### Now

Today, the average American home uses about 10,500 kilowatt-hours of electricity per year — enough to power a 100-watt light bulb continuously for nearly 12 years. The electrical grid is one of the largest and most complex machines ever built, stretching more than 160,000 miles of high-voltage transmission lines across the United States. And the grid is evolving fast: solar panels, wind turbines, battery storage, and smart meters are transforming how electricity is generated, distributed, and used. Understanding electricity is no longer just useful — it is essential for navigating the energy future you will inherit.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The "War of the Currents" between Thomas Edison (DC) and Nikola Tesla/George Westinghouse (AC) in the late 1880s was one of the fiercest technology battles in history. Edison even publicly electrocuted animals to "prove" AC was dangerous. Tesla's AC system won — it could travel long distances more efficiently — and it still powers your home today.

## Kinds of Electricity

### Static Electricity

Rub your feet on carpet, then touch a doorknob — that tiny zap is static electricity. It happens when electrons build up on the surface of an object and then discharge suddenly. Lightning is static electricity on a massive scale: charge builds between clouds and the ground until the voltage is high enough to jump the gap, creating a bolt that can reach 30,000 degrees Fahrenheit.

### Current Electricity

This is the steady, controlled flow of electrons through a conductor — and it is what powers everything in your home. Current electricity comes in two forms: **direct current (DC)**, where electrons flow in one direction, and **alternating current (AC)**, where electrons rapidly reverse direction. You will learn the differences in Requirement 4.

### Electromagnetism

When electric current flows through a wire, it creates a magnetic field around that wire. Wrap the wire into a coil, add an iron core, and you have an electromagnet — a magnet you can turn on and off. Electromagnetism is the principle behind electric motors, generators, transformers, and speakers. You will build your own electromagnet in Requirement 3.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Electricity can kill. Even household current at 120 volts can cause fatal cardiac arrest. Throughout this badge, you will learn specific safety rules for every situation — from inspecting wiring to responding to downed power lines. Take every safety instruction seriously. There is no room for shortcuts when working with electricity.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}
