You walk into a room and see someone frozen in place, gripping an appliance cord — muscles locked, unable to let go. Electrical current is flowing through their body. Your instinct screams to grab them and pull them free. **Do not do it.** If you touch them with your bare hands, the current will flow through you too, and now there are two victims instead of one.

## Why You Cannot Just Grab Them

When current passes through the human body, it causes muscles to contract involuntarily. This is why a person gripping a live wire often cannot let go — their hand muscles are clenched shut by the current itself. If you touch the victim's skin or wet clothing while they are still energized, you become part of the circuit. The current will pass through your body just as it is passing through theirs.

## Step-by-Step Rescue

### 1. Cut the Power

If you can reach the circuit breaker or unplug the appliance without touching the victim or the energized wire, do that first. As you learned in [Req 1a](/merit-badges/electricity/guide/req1a/), flip the breaker or pull the plug. If the cord is damaged and sparking, use the breaker — do not touch the cord.

### 2. Separate the Victim from the Source

If you cannot cut the power quickly, you need to push or pull the victim away from the electrical source using a **non-conducting object**. Good options include:

- A dry wooden broom handle or mop handle
- A dry wooden chair
- A dry, thick rope or belt (not wet, not made of wire)
- A rolled-up dry newspaper or magazine
- A rubber doormat

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
**Never use anything metal, wet, or damp.** Water and metal conduct electricity. A wet towel, a metal pipe, or even a slightly damp wooden stick can transmit lethal current to you. If nothing suitable is available, it is better to wait for the power to be cut or for emergency responders than to risk electrocuting yourself.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### 3. Use the Proper Technique

- Stand on a dry surface — a rubber mat, dry wood, or a thick stack of dry newspapers.
- Use one hand if possible, keeping the other behind your back.
- Push or hook the victim away from the wire or the appliance. Aim to move their body away from the source in one firm motion.
- Do not make contact with the victim's bare skin until they are separated from the source.

### 4. After Separation

Once the victim is free from the electrical source:

1. Call 911 immediately (or have someone else call).
2. Check for breathing and pulse.
3. Begin CPR if needed and you are trained to do so.
4. Treat for shock — lay the person down, elevate their legs, and keep them warm.
5. Look for burns at the entry and exit points (often the hands and feet).

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Live Wire in the Kitchen" >}}
You walk into the kitchen and see your sibling frozen with their hand on a sparking toaster cord. Here is how you respond:

- **Do NOT grab them.** You will become part of the circuit.
- **Pull the plug** if you can reach it without touching them or the wet counter.
- **If you cannot unplug safely**, grab a dry wooden broom handle and push their arm away from the cord.
- **Once they are free**, ease them to the floor, call 911, and check for breathing.
- **Look for burns** on their hand (entry point) and possibly their feet (exit point).
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
It takes as little as 0.1 amperes (100 milliamps) of current flowing through the heart to cause fatal ventricular fibrillation. For comparison, a standard household circuit can deliver more than 100 times that amount. The danger is not the voltage alone — it is the current flowing through vital organs.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}
