{{< drg/requirement number="6a" >}}
Define what overloading an electric circuit means. Tell what you have done to make sure your home circuits are not overloaded.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Every wire in your home has a limit to how much current it can safely carry. Push too much current through a wire, and it heats up — sometimes enough to melt the insulation and start a fire. Overloading a circuit means drawing more current than the wiring and the circuit breaker are rated to handle safely.

## What Overloading Means

A typical home branch circuit uses 14-gauge wire protected by a 15-amp circuit breaker. This means the circuit can safely carry up to 15 amps of current. If the total current draw from all the devices plugged into that circuit exceeds 15 amps, the circuit is overloaded.

In practice, the safe working limit is 80% of the breaker rating — so a 15-amp circuit should carry no more than 12 amps of continuous load. The 20% margin accounts for startup surges when motors kick in (like a vacuum cleaner or window AC unit) and for fluctuations in the power supply.

### What Happens When a Circuit Overloads

1. The wires begin to heat up because they are carrying more current than designed for.
2. The insulation on the wires may soften, melt, or degrade.
3. The circuit breaker detects the excess current and trips (opens the circuit), cutting power.
4. If the breaker fails to trip — which can happen with old or faulty breakers — the overheated wires can ignite surrounding materials inside the wall.

This is exactly why circuit breakers exist: they are the last line of defense between an overloaded circuit and a house fire. You will learn more about how breakers work in [Req 6c](/merit-badges/electricity/guide/req6c/).

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Never replace a tripped breaker with a higher-rated one to "fix" the tripping. If a 15-amp breaker keeps tripping, the solution is to reduce the load on that circuit — not to install a 20-amp breaker. A 20-amp breaker on a 15-amp wire will let dangerous current flow without tripping, and the wire itself becomes the fire hazard.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Common Causes of Overloading

- **Too many devices on one circuit** — Plugging a space heater (12.5 amps), a hair dryer (12 amps), and a curling iron (1 amp) into outlets on the same circuit easily exceeds 15 amps.
- **Daisy-chaining power strips** — Plugging one power strip into another does not create more capacity. The circuit behind the outlet still has the same limit.
- **Extension cord misuse** — Long, thin extension cords add resistance and generate heat. Using a 16-gauge cord for a 15-amp appliance is a fire risk.
- **Old wiring** — Homes built before the 1960s may have fewer circuits, meaning more outlets share the same wire. Modern electronics demands have far exceeded what those circuits were designed for.

## What You Can Do at Home

During your home inspection in [Req 2](/merit-badges/electricity/guide/req2/), you looked at many of these issues. Here are specific actions to prevent overloading:

{{< drg/checklist title="Preventing Circuit Overload" subtitle="Practical steps for your home" >}}
- Know which outlets share a circuit by testing breakers (turn one off and see what loses power).
- Avoid running high-draw appliances on the same circuit simultaneously.
- Use power strips with built-in circuit breakers instead of cheap multi-outlet adapters.
- Never daisy-chain power strips or extension cords.
- Replace old, undersized extension cords with appropriately rated ones.
- Move portable heaters to a dedicated circuit when possible.
- If breakers trip frequently, consult a licensed electrician about adding circuits.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
A single portable space heater can draw 12.5 amps on a 15-amp circuit — that is 83% of the circuit's capacity from just one device. Plug a lamp and a phone charger into another outlet on the same circuit and you are approaching the limit. This is why space heaters are the number one cause of home heating fires.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}
