{{< drg/requirement number="4b" >}}
Explain three ways that electricity is produced.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Every time you flip a switch, the electricity that flows to your light was produced somewhere — often hundreds of miles away. But how does spinning a turbine or exposing silicon to sunlight actually create the flow of electrons we call electricity? Let's look at three major methods.

## 1. Electromagnetic Induction (Generators)

This is by far the most common method of producing electricity. It works on the same principle you explored when building your electromagnet in [Req 3](/merit-badges/electricity/guide/req3/) — but in reverse. Instead of using electricity to create magnetism, electromagnetic induction uses magnetism to create electricity.

When a coil of wire rotates inside a magnetic field (or a magnet rotates inside a coil of wire), the changing magnetic field pushes electrons through the wire, creating an electric current. This is exactly what Michael Faraday discovered in 1831, and every power plant that uses a spinning turbine relies on this principle.

The turbine can be spun by many different energy sources:

- **Coal or natural gas** — Burning fuel heats water to steam, which spins a steam turbine
- **Nuclear** — Nuclear fission heats water to steam, which spins a turbine
- **Hydroelectric** — Falling water spins a water turbine directly
- **Wind** — Wind spins the blades of a wind turbine directly
- **Geothermal** — Underground heat creates steam, which spins a turbine

In every case, the spinning turbine turns the shaft of a generator, which rotates coils of wire through magnetic fields to produce AC electricity.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The Hoover Dam's generators produce about 4 billion kilowatt-hours of electricity per year — enough to serve 1.3 million people. The water falls over 500 feet to reach the turbines, and the force of that drop spins generators that weigh over 4 million pounds each.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## 2. Photovoltaic Effect (Solar Panels)

Solar panels convert sunlight directly into electricity — no moving parts, no steam, no turbine. This works through the **photovoltaic effect**, discovered by French physicist Edmond Becquerel in 1839.

A solar cell is made of two layers of silicon (a semiconductor). The top layer has extra electrons (negative charge) and the bottom layer has spaces where electrons are missing (positive charge). When photons (particles of light) hit the cell, they knock electrons loose from their atoms. The built-in electric field between the two layers pushes these free electrons in one direction, creating a flow of direct current.

A single solar cell produces only about 0.5 volts — not enough to power much. So cells are wired together into panels, and panels are wired together into arrays. A typical rooftop solar array of 20–30 panels can produce enough electricity to power an entire home.

Solar panels produce DC, which must be converted to AC by an **inverter** before it can be used in your home or sent to the grid. You will learn more about inverters in [Req 9d](/merit-badges/electricity/guide/req9d/).

{{< drg/tip >}}
If your family is considering solar panels, look up your area's "solar insolation" — the average amount of sunlight per day. The southwestern United States receives about 6 hours of peak sunlight per day, while the Pacific Northwest gets about 3.5. This directly affects how much electricity a solar array will produce.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## 3. Chemical Reaction (Batteries)

Batteries convert chemical energy directly into electrical energy through controlled chemical reactions. Inside a battery, two different materials (called electrodes) sit in a chemical solution (the electrolyte). A chemical reaction at one electrode releases electrons, while a reaction at the other electrode absorbs them. Connect a wire between the two electrodes, and the electrons flow through the wire — that is your electric current.

Different battery types use different chemical reactions:

| Battery Type | Electrodes | Typical Use |
|---|---|---|
| Alkaline (AA, D-cell) | Zinc + manganese dioxide | Flashlights, remotes, toys |
| Lithium-ion | Lithium compound + graphite | Phones, laptops, electric cars |
| Lead-acid | Lead + lead dioxide | Car starter batteries |
| Nickel-metal hydride | Nickel oxide + metal alloy | Rechargeable household batteries |

Rechargeable batteries can reverse the chemical reaction by pushing current back through the battery (charging), restoring the electrode materials to their original state. Non-rechargeable batteries undergo irreversible reactions — once the chemicals are used up, the battery is dead.

You will explore battery construction in more detail in [Req 5](/merit-badges/electricity/guide/req5/).

## Other Ways to Generate Electricity

While the requirement asks for three methods, it is worth knowing that electricity can also be produced by:

- **Thermoelectric effect** — Temperature differences across certain materials produce voltage (used in spacecraft power)
- **Piezoelectric effect** — Mechanical pressure on certain crystals produces voltage (used in lighters and sensors)
- **Fuel cells** — Hydrogen and oxygen combine electrochemically to produce electricity and water (used in some buses and NASA spacecraft)
