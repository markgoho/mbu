{{< drg/requirement number="9d" >}}
Equipment and their use - circuit, conductor, Ground Fault Circuit Interrupter (GFCI), insulator, inverter, rectifier, rheostat, substation, surge protection, solar panel, transformer, transmission and distribution systems, and wind turbine.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This is the longest vocabulary list in the badge — thirteen items that cover everything from the wire in your wall to the power plant on the horizon. These are the physical components that make the electrical grid work.

## Circuit

A **circuit** is a complete, closed loop through which electric current can flow. A circuit must include a source of energy (battery, generator), a conductor (wire), and usually a load (light, motor, appliance). If any part of the loop is broken, the circuit is "open" and current stops flowing.

## Conductor

A **conductor** is a material that allows electricity to flow through it easily because it has very low resistance. Metals are the best conductors — copper is the most commonly used in home wiring because it combines excellent conductivity with reasonable cost. Aluminum, silver, and gold are also good conductors.

Water (especially saltwater) is a conductor as well, which is why electricity and water are such a dangerous combination.

## Ground Fault Circuit Interrupter (GFCI)

A **GFCI** is a fast-acting safety device that monitors the current flowing through a circuit. It compares the current going out on the hot wire to the current returning on the neutral wire. If the GFCI detects a difference of just 5 milliamps (0.005 A) — meaning some current is leaking through an unintended path, possibly through a person — it shuts off the circuit in about 1/30th of a second.

GFCIs are required by the National Electrical Code in bathrooms, kitchens, garages, outdoor outlets, and anywhere else that water might be present. You can identify them by the "Test" and "Reset" buttons on the outlet face.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
A GFCI can save your life. It reacts fast enough to cut power before a lethal shock can occur. Test your GFCI outlets monthly by pressing the "Test" button — a plugged-in device should lose power. Press "Reset" to restore it. If a GFCI does not trip when tested, replace it immediately.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Insulator

An **insulator** is a material that strongly resists the flow of electricity. Rubber, plastic, glass, ceramic, and dry wood are all good insulators. The plastic coating on electrical wires is an insulator — it prevents the current from escaping the wire and keeps you from being shocked when you touch the cord.

Insulators are equally as important as conductors. Without insulation, every wire would be a shock and fire hazard.

## Inverter

An **inverter** converts direct current (DC) into alternating current (AC). This is essential for solar panel systems: solar panels produce DC, but your home and the electrical grid run on AC. The inverter performs the conversion so your solar energy can power your appliances or be fed back to the grid.

Inverters are also found in uninterruptible power supplies (UPS) — the battery backup units used to protect computers and servers. When the power goes out, the UPS inverter converts its battery's DC into AC to keep devices running.

## Rectifier

A **rectifier** is the opposite of an inverter — it converts AC into DC. Nearly every electronic device you own contains a rectifier. Your phone charger takes 120V AC from the wall outlet and uses a rectifier (combined with a transformer and voltage regulator) to produce the 5V DC your phone needs.

## Rheostat

A **rheostat** is a variable resistor used to control current flow. By adjusting the rheostat, you can increase or decrease the resistance in a circuit, which changes how much current flows. Old-fashioned dimmer switches used rheostats — turning the knob increased resistance, reducing the current to the light bulb and dimming it.

Modern dimmers use electronic components instead of rheostats because rheostats waste energy as heat. You will have the option to build a rheostat in [Req 10c](/merit-badges/electricity/guide/req10/).

## Substation

A **substation** is a facility where voltage is transformed between levels as electricity moves through the grid. Step-up substations near power plants raise the voltage for efficient long-distance transmission. Step-down substations near neighborhoods reduce the voltage for residential use. A typical substation transforms 69,000–345,000 volts down to 7,200–14,400 volts for local distribution.

## Surge Protection

A **surge protector** defends equipment against overvoltage events (voltage spikes). Inside a surge protector, components called metal oxide varistors (MOVs) detect when voltage exceeds the safe level. When a spike occurs, the MOV diverts the excess energy to the ground wire, protecting whatever is plugged in.

Surge protectors come in two types:
- **Point-of-use** — The familiar power strip with surge protection (look for a joule rating — higher is better)
- **Whole-house** — Installed at the electrical panel to protect the entire home

{{< drg/tip >}}
Not all power strips are surge protectors. Look for a joule rating on the packaging — a quality surge protector should be rated at 1,000 joules or more. A power strip without a joule rating is just a multi-outlet adapter with no surge protection.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Solar Panel

A **solar panel** (photovoltaic panel) converts sunlight directly into DC electricity through the photovoltaic effect. Each panel contains dozens of individual solar cells made from silicon semiconductors. You learned how solar cells work in [Req 4b](/merit-badges/electricity/guide/req4b/).

## Transformer

A **transformer** changes AC voltage from one level to another. It works through electromagnetic induction — two coils of wire wrapped around the same iron core. AC current in the primary coil creates a changing magnetic field that induces voltage in the secondary coil. By changing the ratio of turns between the coils, the transformer steps voltage up or down.

Transformers are the reason AC won the War of the Currents. They are cheap, efficient, and have no moving parts. You can see them in your neighborhood as the cylindrical cans mounted on utility poles or the green boxes on the ground near your house.

## Transmission and Distribution Systems

The **transmission system** carries electricity from power plants to population centers using high-voltage lines (115,000–765,000 volts). Higher voltage means lower current, which reduces energy loss as heat in the wires.

The **distribution system** carries electricity from local substations to homes and businesses at lower voltages (7,200–14,400 volts for neighborhoods, then 120/240 volts at your house). The transformer on the pole near your home or the pad-mounted transformer on the ground performs the final step-down.

The journey: **Power plant** → Step-up transformer → **Transmission lines** → Step-down substation → **Distribution lines** → Neighborhood transformer → **Your home**

## Wind Turbine

A **wind turbine** converts the kinetic energy of wind into electricity through electromagnetic induction. Wind spins the blades, which turn a shaft connected to a generator. Modern utility-scale wind turbines stand 300+ feet tall with blades over 150 feet long and can generate 2–5 megawatts each — enough to power 500–1,500 homes.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The largest wind turbines in the world have blades over 350 feet long — longer than a football field. A single rotation of these massive blades can generate enough electricity to power an average home for two days.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}
