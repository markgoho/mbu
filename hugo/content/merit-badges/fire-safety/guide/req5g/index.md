{{< drg/requirement number="5g" >}}
Develop a home fire-escape plan and a fire-drill schedule with your family, draw a floor plan of your home with exits marked and a map showing your family meeting point, and conduct a home fire drill.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

In a fire, panicking and indecision kill people. A family that has planned and practiced knows exactly where to go and what to do. This requirement asks you to create a plan, practice it, and make sure your family is prepared. It's not just a document—it's preparation that saves lives.

## Why Fire Escape Plans Matter

**Research shows:**
- Families with practiced fire plans escape 40% faster
- Panic and indecision cause people to re-enter burning buildings
- Children freeze without knowing where to go
- Elderly/disabled may not know what help is available
- Planning prevents firefighters from risking lives searching for people

**The goal:** When an alarm sounds, every family member knows exactly where to go without thinking.

## Step 1: Create Your Floor Plan

### What Your Floor Plan Should Show

**Essential elements:**

1. **All rooms and exits**
   - Windows (marked as potential exits)
   - Doors (all interior and exterior doors)
   - Hallways connecting rooms
   - Stairs (up and down)
   - Balconies/decks

2. **Alarm locations**
   - Mark where smoke alarms are installed
   - Mark CO alarms if present
   - Show which alarm covers which area

3. **Hazards** (optional but helpful)
   - Mark space heaters
   - Mark fireplaces
   - Mark electrical panels
   - Helps family avoid these areas if fire is there

4. **Evacuation routes**
   - Primary route (best/fastest exit)
   - Secondary route (if primary is blocked by fire/smoke)
   - Every room should have at least one exit route marked

5. **Family meeting point**
   - Mark location outside home where family gathers
   - Should be visible from all exits
   - Should be safe from falling debris (away from building)

### How to Draw Your Floor Plan

**Materials:**
- Paper (large sheet if possible, 8.5x11" minimum)
- Pencil or pen
- Ruler (optional, for straight lines)
- Colored pencils/markers (helpful to distinguish routes)

**Process:**

1. **Start with outline** — Draw rough outline of your home as seen from above
   - Living areas on one level
   - Bedrooms on another level (if multi-story)
   - Include walls, doorways, windows

2. **Label rooms** — Mark each room clearly
   - Kitchen, Living Room, Bedrooms (1, 2, 3), Bathrooms, Hallways
   - Keep labels readable

3. **Mark exits** — Draw circles or arrows at all exits
   - Doors to outside
   - Windows (if accessible for escape)
   - Mark different colors if primary vs. secondary

4. **Draw evacuation routes** — Use arrows to show escape path
   - Primary route in one color (e.g., blue)
   - Secondary route in different color (e.g., red)
   - Routes should go to outside exit

5. **Mark alarm locations** — Small circles or X's where alarms are
   - Label if smoke or CO
   - Helps family know where danger is detected first

6. **Show meeting point** — Mark with star or special symbol outside home
   - Include street name if needed
   - Show distance ("50 feet from front door")
   - Explain why this location (open space, away from building)

7. **Add legend** — Create a key explaining symbols
   - Blue arrow = Primary route
   - Red arrow = Secondary route
   - Circle = Smoke alarm
   - etc.

**Example floor plan structure:**

```
        [Bedroom 1]--[Bathroom]--[Bedroom 2]
             |                         |
        [Hallway] ← Smoke Alarm
             |
        [Living Room]
             |
        [Kitchen]--[Exit door]

Outside meeting point:
Front yard, 50 feet from house, under oak tree
```

## Step 2: Plan Evacuation Routes

### Identifying Routes from Each Room

**For each room, identify:**

1. **Primary route**
   - Fastest, easiest way out
   - Usually main door
   - Example: Bedroom → Hallway → Front Door → Outside

2. **Secondary route** (what if primary is blocked?)
   - Alternative exit
   - May involve window escape
   - Example: Bedroom → Window escape

### Special Considerations

**Bedrooms:**
- Window escape is often secondary route
- Ensure windows open (not painted shut)
- Check if window screen removes easily
- Measure distance to ground (can family jump safely? Or use ladder?)
- Mark window as exit on plan

**Upper floors:**
- Window exits may require ladders
- Rope ladder or chain ladder available?
- Escape ladder should be accessible

**Bathrooms:**
- Usually small rooms with limited exits
- May only have window for escape
- Plan accordingly

**Basement:**
- Multiple exits important (can fill with smoke quickly)
- Window wells for outside egress
- Mark clearly on plan

**Children's rooms:**
- Ensure child knows both routes
- Practice window escape with child
- Consider if child can open window alone

### Kitchen Fire Scenario

**Special case:** If fire starts in kitchen:
- Kitchen is main exit route for some family members
- Need secondary route through other parts of house
- Don't use kitchen exit if fire is there
- Mark plan to show alternate route if kitchen is unavailable

## Step 3: Establish Meeting Point

### Choosing Your Meeting Point

**Criteria for good meeting point:**

1. **Outside the home** — Far enough that you're not in danger
2. **Easy to reach** — From all exits without re-entering home
3. **Safe location** — Away from building (falling debris, extreme heat)
4. **Visible** — Family can see it from different exits
5. **Recognizable** — Landmark that everyone knows
6. **Safe for children** — Not on busy road, not in dangerous location

**Good meeting points:**
- End of driveway (but not in street)
- Front yard, far from house
- Tree in yard
- Neighbor's mailbox or driveway
- Front corner of property
- Nearby park

**Bad meeting points:**
- In the street
- Directly in front of house (fallen structure hazard)
- Neighbor's garage or enclosed structure
- Back of property (too far, hard to see)
- Inside a car (tempts leaving area)

### Marking Your Meeting Point

**Make it obvious:**
- Paint landmark (mailbox, tree, rock)
- Place reflector or sign
- Mark on your floor plan very clearly
- Tell all family members exact location

**Measure distance:**
- "50 feet from front door"
- "At the large oak tree on corner"
- "Right side of driveway near street"

## Step 4: Create a Fire Drill Schedule

### Planning Regular Drills

Fire drills should be practiced regularly so reactions become automatic:

**Recommended schedule:**
- **Quarterly drills** (every 3 months) — Minimum
- **Twice yearly** — Once in summer, once in winter (conditions different)
- **After changes** — If family member added or exits blocked, practice again
- **After age changes** — Young children learn to escape faster with practice

### How to Conduct a Fire Drill

**Materials:**
- Everyone home (include all family members)
- Close time before starting
- Someone to time the drill
- Notebook to record observations

**Safety first:**
- Make clear this is a DRILL (not a real fire)
- Ensure no one goes in dangerous areas
- Use actual exits (don't "pretend" exits)
- Anyone with mobility issues should practice their specific route

**The drill sequence:**

1. **Designate fire location** — "Fire in kitchen"
2. **Sound alarm** — Or have someone yell "FIRE!"
3. **Evacuate following plan**
   - Each person follows their planned route
   - Go to designated exit
   - Exit home quickly (don't take possessions)
   - Go to meeting point
4. **Account for everyone** — At meeting point, count who arrived
5. **Time the process** — How long did everyone exit?
6. **Review observations:**
   - Did everyone know their route?
   - Did anyone hesitate or get confused?
   - Were any exits blocked?
   - Could any part be faster?
   - Did anyone try to take belongings (mistake!)?

### Making Drills Realistic

**Common drill problems:**
- Kids treat it as game (not serious)
- Parents "walk" instead of moving quickly
- Drills always use front door (what if blocked?)
- Drills happen during day (practice nighttime evacuation too)
- Everyone knows the drill location (surprise people occasionally)

**Better drills:**
- Vary time of day (include nighttime)
- Vary fire location ("Fire in bedroom!")
- Require people to use secondary routes
- Do practice with lights off (simulates smoke conditions)
- Make it realistic (move quickly, don't stop to discuss)

### Kids and Fire Drills

**Young children (5-10):**
- May not understand "drill" vs. "real"
- Need repeated practice
- May freeze or hide (special practice for this)
- Need reassurance it's practice
- Practice 2-3 times yearly

**Teens (11+):**
- Understand plan more easily
- May be overconfident
- Can help younger siblings
- Practice 1-2 times yearly

**Elderly family members:**
- May have mobility issues
- Practice their specific route
- Ensure they know it's safe
- Practice during day AND night

## Step 5: Special Situations

### What If Someone Can't Evacuate?

If family member has mobility limitations or other barriers:

**Plan for them:**
- Specific route they can safely use
- Assistance plan (who will help?)
- If cannot exit (locked in room), go to window and signal rescuers
- Doors that lock should have key accessible

**For elderly in upstairs bedroom:**
- Practice getting to window
- Ensure window opens
- Consider rope ladder or chair to climb down
- Know firefighters will search and find them
- Stay in room (room is safer than hallway with smoke)

### Pets

**Include in plan:**
- Where pet crate/carriers are
- Who grabs pet during evacuation
- Pet meeting location (with family at outside meeting point)
- Don't waste time searching for pets—human safety first, but have plan for quick grab

**During fire:**
- Put pet carriers by your exit so you can grab quickly
- If pet missing, tell firefighters (they can search)
- Never go back in for pet

### People with Disabilities

**Hearing impaired:**
- Visual alarm signal in bedroom
- Visual signal for drill starting
- Assign person to alert them to fire

**Mobility impaired:**
- Accessible exit plan
- Assistance person identified
- Evacuation chair if on upper floor
- Communication plan with firefighters

## Creating Your Written Plan

**Write down key information:**

1. **Family meeting point**
   - Address if applicable
   - Description ("large oak tree at end of driveway")
   - Distance from house

2. **Evacuation routes by room**
   - Bedroom 1: Primary (door), Secondary (window)
   - Bedroom 2: Primary (hallway), Secondary (window)
   - etc.

3. **Important numbers**
   - 911
   - Out-of-state contact person (call them after evacuation)
   - Non-emergency fire department number

4. **Special instructions**
   - Children's procedures
   - Disabled person procedures
   - Pet procedures
   - "Don't go back in for anything"

5. **Drill schedule**
   - Quarterly drills
   - Next scheduled drill date

**Post this plan:**
- On fridge
- In each bedroom
- Near exits
- Anywhere family will see it regularly

## Demonstrating Your Drill to Your Counselor

Your counselor will want to see:

1. **Floor plan** — With exits, routes, alarm locations, meeting point marked
2. **Written evacuation plan** — Key information documented
3. **Evidence of drill** — Photos, notes from actual fire drill
4. **Family understanding** — Can your family members explain their route?
5. **Timing** — How long does evacuation take?
6. **Meeting point** — Can you show/describe where family meets?

### Discussion Points

Be prepared to discuss:
- Why did you choose the meeting point?
- What challenges did you find during drill?
- How will you practice regularly?
- What would you do if fire blocked your primary route?
- How will you handle family members with special needs?

## Key Principles for Fire Escape

{{< drg/be-prepared title="If Fire Occurs in Your Home" >}}
When your alarm sounds or you discover fire:

1. **Alert everyone** — Yell "FIRE!" if alarm doesn't wake everyone
2. **Get out immediately** — Don't investigate, don't look back
3. **Use your planned route** — Go to your designated exit
4. **Leave everything** — No time for possessions, pets, or people who won't leave
5. **Go to meeting point** — Every family member goes to the same location
6. **Account for everyone** — When all are there, designate someone to call 911
7. **Never go back in** — Not for people, pets, or anything

Remember: **Leave belongings, leave people who won't listen, and get out fast.**

{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

## The Reality of Fire Escape

**Important to know:**
- Fires spread much faster than you expect
- Smoke arrives before flames
- Heat is lethal before you see flames
- Every second counts
- Your plan removes decision-making (decisions take time)
- Families with plans escape in half the time

{{< drg/tip >}}
The most important part of your fire plan is not the map—it's the practice. Families that drill regularly escape fastest when fire happens. A plan nobody practices is just paper.
{{< /drg/tip >}}
