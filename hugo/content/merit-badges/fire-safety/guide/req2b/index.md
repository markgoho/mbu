## Fire Extinguisher Types and Agents

Fire extinguishers use different chemical agents to extinguish different types of fires. Each agent works through one or more of the fire tetrahedron principles: removing heat, fuel, oxygen, or interrupting the chemical reaction.

## Type ABC (Multi-Purpose) Extinguishers

**Agent:** Dry chemical powder (usually ammonium polyphosphate)

**What It Does:** The powder coats fuel, interrupts the chemical reaction, and provides some cooling. It's effective on multiple fire types.

**Works On:** Class A, B, and C fires

**Where to Find It:** Homes, offices, kitchens, garages, cars

**Advantages:**
- Works on multiple fire types
- Inexpensive ($20–$50)
- Good for general household use
- Quick knockdown of flames

**Disadvantages:**
- Leaves a mess (thick powder residue)
- Messy to clean up
- Clouds visibility with powder
- Not ideal for cooking oil fires (Class K)

{{< drg/tip >}}
ABC extinguishers are the most common household extinguisher. Many homes should have at least one in the kitchen and one in the garage. They're inexpensive insurance.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Type B (Foam) Extinguishers

**Agent:** Aqueous film-forming foam (AFFF) or protein-based foam

**What It Does:** Creates a foam blanket that smothers fuel vapors and cools the surface

**Works On:** Class B fires (flammable liquids)

**Where to Find It:** Workshops, fuel storage, marinas, airports

**Advantages:**
- Excellent for Class B fires
- Creates cooling and smothering effect
- Better visibility than dry chemical powder
- More effective on gasoline and oil than ABC extinguishers

**Disadvantages:**
- Only works on Class B (not A or C)
- More expensive than ABC
- Requires water supply in some models (heavy)
- Environmental concerns with some formulations

## Type C (CO₂) Extinguishers

**Agent:** Carbon dioxide gas (CO₂)

**What It Does:** Displaces oxygen and cools the fire. Leaves no residue.

**Works On:** Class B and C fires (electrical fires)

**Where to Find It:** Electrical rooms, data centers, server rooms, laboratories

**Advantages:**
- Safe for electrical equipment
- Leaves no residue (won't damage electronics)
- Non-toxic
- Good penetration into crevices

**Disadvantages:**
- Less effective on Class B than foam
- Limited range (short stream)
- Horn becomes extremely cold during use (can cause frostbite)
- Requires re-pressurization after use
- Doesn't work on Class A (materials reignite)

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
CO₂ extinguishers get so cold (-78°F at the nozzle) that you can get frostbite from holding the horn. Wear insulated gloves if you ever use one.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Type D (Metal) Extinguishers

**Agent:** Dry powder specific to the metal burning (sodium chloride, graphite, copper powder, etc.)

**What It Does:** Smothers and cools specific metal fires

**Works On:** Class D fires (combustible metals)

**Where to Find It:** Machine shops, laboratories, aerospace facilities, anywhere combustible metals are stored

**Advantages:**
- Only effective option for metal fires
- Specialized agents for different metals

**Disadvantages:**
- Expensive
- Not available in most locations
- Requires knowledge of which powder for which metal
- Can be ineffective on large metal fires

## Type K (Wet Chemical) Extinguishers

**Agent:** Potassium carbonate-based wet chemical

**What It Does:** Cools the oil and creates a foam blanket that smothers vapors

**Works On:** Class K fires (cooking oil fires) and also effective on Class B

**Where to Find It:** Commercial kitchens, restaurants, some home kitchens

**Advantages:**
- Only effective option for Class K
- Also works on Class B
- Creates saponification (converts oil to soapy substance)
- Excellent cooling effect

**Disadvantages:**
- Expensive ($60–$150 per extinguisher)
- Heavy
- Not common in residential settings
- Requires specialized maintenance

## Water Extinguishers

**Agent:** Plain water or water mist

**What It Does:** Cools fuel below ignition temperature; absorbs heat

**Works On:** Class A fires only

**Where to Find It:** Older buildings, some residential settings

**Advantages:**
- Inexpensive
- Safe
- Excellent cooling capability
- No residue

**Disadvantages:**
- Only works on Class A
- Heavy and bulky
- Freezes in cold climates
- Ineffective on oil fires and electrical fires

## Extinguisher Selection Chart

| Fire Class | Best Extinguisher | Second Choice | Never Use |
|-----------|------------------|---------------|-----------|
| **A** (Wood, paper, cloth) | Water or ABC | Wet Chemical | CO₂, Foam alone |
| **B** (Gasoline, oil) | Foam or ABC | CO₂ or Wet Chem | Water |
| **C** (Electrical) | CO₂ or ABC | Dry Chem powder | Water, Foam, Wet Chem |
| **D** (Metals) | Class D powder | Only option | Everything else |
| **K** (Cooking oil) | Wet Chemical or K | ABC (less ideal) | Water, standard foam |

## How to Read an Extinguisher Label

Every extinguisher has a colored label showing which fire classes it works on:

- **Red square with A** = Class A
- **Blue square with B** = Class B
- **Yellow square with C** = Class C
- **White square with D** = Class D
- **Black square with K** = Class K

An ABC extinguisher shows all three squares. A fire extinguisher's label also shows:
- The agent type (dry chemical, CO₂, foam, etc.)
- Pressure gauge (green = OK, red = needs servicing)
- Usage instructions (pull pin, aim, squeeze, sweep)
- Expiration date or service tag
- Recharge instructions

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Choosing the Right Extinguisher for Your Home" >}}
For a typical home, you should have:
- **ABC extinguisher** in the kitchen (most accessible location)
- **ABC extinguisher** in the garage or workshop
- Consider a **Class K extinguisher** if you cook frequently with oil
- Check monthly that pressure gauges show green (OK zone)
- Replace or recharge immediately after use
- Replace every 5–15 years depending on type (check manufacturer specs)
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}
