{{< drg/requirement number="3c" >}}
Identify the most frequent causes of burn injuries and how to prevent them.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Burns happen in seconds, but understanding which situations create the highest risk lets you avoid them. The most common burn injuries are preventable—they don't happen by accident, they happen because of predictable, avoidable mistakes.

## The Most Common Burn Injuries

### Kitchen Burns (Highest Risk Category)

**Why kitchens are dangerous:**
The kitchen is where most serious burns occur in homes. Hot liquids, hot surfaces, and steam create perfect conditions for injury in a crowded, fast-paced space.

**Most frequent kitchen burns:**

**Pot/Pan Contact (Largest Single Cause)**
- Touching a hot stove top, oven door, or pot handle
- Reaching across a burner
- Knocking a pot off the stove
- Prevention: Use back burners when possible; keep handles turned inward; keep children away from stove; establish a "cooking zone" that no one enters unless helping

**Boiling Liquid Splash**
- Boiling water from pasta, soup, or vegetables
- Hot oil splashing during frying
- Steam from a lid or pot
- Prevention: Never fill pots more than 2/3 full; avoid reaching over pots; keep a child-free zone around the stove; wear fitted sleeves; use a splatter screen for frying; let liquids cool slightly before pouring

**Microwave Superheating**
- Water or liquids heated in microwave become hotter than boiling (up to 300°F) without visibly steaming
- Moving the container can cause sudden boiling/splashing
- Prevention: Stir liquids carefully; place a wooden stick in microwave cups; let liquids cool after heating

**Grease/Oil Burns (Severe)**
- Hot cooking oil causes deeper, more severe burns than water
- Can ignite if too hot (grease fires)
- Prevention: Keep oil below smoking point (350-400°F); never leave hot oil unattended; keep water away from hot oil; cover pans if oil spatters; wear long sleeves

### Tap Water Burns (Bathroom/Bathing)

**Scalding from hot water taps:**
- Children under 5 and elderly people are most vulnerable
- Water hotter than 140°F can cause third-degree burns in seconds
- Tub/shower mixing valve failure can suddenly deliver scalding water
- Prevention: Test water with your hand or elbow before entering; set water heater to 120°F maximum; install anti-scald devices on shower/tub valves; teach children never to adjust water temperature alone

### Fire-Related Burns (Secondary)

**Contact with flames or fire:**
- Candles, fireplaces, campfires
- Clothing catching fire
- Flash burns from rapid fire spread (ignited from something burning nearby)
- Prevention: Keep flammable items away from flames; never leave candles unattended; use fireplace screens; maintain proper clearance from woodstoves; follow proper campfire safety

### Chemical Burns

**Cleaners, batteries, paint thinner:**
- Often cause burns deeper than thermal burns of same size
- Can continue burning if chemical isn't removed
- Prevention: Wear gloves when using harsh chemicals; follow label instructions; know what's toxic in your home; keep chemicals away from children

### Electrical Burns

**Electric shock or arc flash:**
- Can cause both surface and internal damage
- May not be immediately obvious (like internal organ damage)
- Prevention: Use GFCI outlets in bathrooms/kitchens; never use appliances near water; keep cords in good repair; never insert metal into electrical outlets

### Contact with Hot Surfaces

**Hot appliances and objects:**
- Curling irons, space heaters, ovens, stovetop burners
- Often leave distinctive grid-pattern or circular burns
- Prevention: Use appliance covers; turn handles away from edges; maintain distance from hot surfaces; unplug before leaving room

## Age-Related Risk Factors

**Children under 5:**
- Highest burn rate of any age group
- Don't understand heat danger
- Can't prevent or escape burns quickly
- Most vulnerable in kitchen and bathroom
- Prevention: Constant supervision; barriers around hazards

**Teenagers (14-19):**
- Second highest burn rate
- Often caused by experimentation with fire, cooking, or chemicals
- Overconfidence in their abilities
- Prevention: Fire safety education; supervised cooking and campfire use; chemical safety training

**Elderly adults:**
- Slower reaction time to heat
- Thinner skin burns more easily
- May have mobility limitations
- Often live alone (delayed medical help)
- Prevention: Anti-scald devices; careful appliance use; regular safety checks

## Burn Depth and Severity Correlation

**The depth determines how serious:**

**First-degree burns** (superficial, like sunburn)
- Only affect outer layer of skin
- Red, no blistering
- Usually minor (except large area)
- Treatment: Cool water, aloe, pain relief

**Second-degree burns** (partial thickness)
- Damage extends into deeper skin layers
- Blistered, wet appearance
- Very painful
- Require medical attention if larger than 3 inches
- Most thermal burns from kitchens (boiling water, oil)

**Third-degree burns** (full thickness)
- Destroy all skin layers
- May appear white, charred, or waxy
- Often painless (nerve damage)
- Require immediate hospital care
- Often from flames or prolonged contact with heat

**Fourth-degree burns**
- Damage extends to muscle and bone
- Medical emergency
- Rare from household sources (mostly industrial/severe fire)

## High-Risk Situations That You Can Control

{{< drg/tip >}}
The majority of serious burns (70%+) involve children under 5 or kitchen-related incidents. These are the two areas where prevention efforts save the most lives.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

**Cooking while distracted:**
When you're talking on the phone, multitasking, or rushing, you're less aware of hot surfaces and spilling risks. If you're going to cook, give it your full attention.

**Loose clothing near open flames:**
Flowing fabric catches fire faster than fitted clothing. Wear appropriate clothing when cooking or around campfires.

**Cooking with children underfoot:**
Kids naturally gravitate toward the kitchen where you are. Establish a safety zone behind you where children stay while cooking, not at arm's reach.

**Using running water to put out oil fires:**
This is perhaps the most dangerous mistake. Water and hot oil create explosions. Never throw water on an oil fire.

**Trying to rescue items from fires:**
Burns on hands, arms, and face are common when people try to grab things from fire. Let items burn—your safety matters more.

**Unknown appliance temperatures:**
Space heaters, ovens, and curling irons get extremely hot. If you don't know what temperature something reaches, assume it will burn you.

## Prevention Checklist

{{< drg/checklist title="Burn Prevention" subtitle="Check each area of your home" >}}
- **Kitchen:** Back burners used, handles turned in, pot/pan placement safe, children managed, grease fire prevention understood
- **Bathroom:** Water heater set to 120°F or lower, anti-scald devices installed, children supervised during bathing
- **Bedrooms:** Space heaters at safe distance from bedding and walls (at least 3 feet), electrical cords in good repair
- **Living areas:** Fireplace/woodstove screens in place, candles unattended none, furniture arranged to prevent falls into heat sources
- **Chemical storage:** Cleaners and chemicals locked or out of reach, labels visible, knowledge of what's harmful
- **Electrical:** GFCI outlets in wet areas, appliances in good repair, no overloaded circuits
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

## Quick Prevention Summary by Location

**In the Kitchen:**
- Never leave cooking unattended
- Use back burners; turn handles inward
- Keep kids behind a barrier
- Test water temperature before use
- Know how to respond to grease fires (cover, don't spray)

**In the Bathroom:**
- Test water before entering
- Supervise children bathing
- Set water heater to 120°F
- Install anti-scald valves

**At Camp or Outdoors:**
- Keep loose clothing away from flames
- Use proper fire lays
- Never leave fire unattended
- Ensure fire is fully extinguished before leaving

**With Appliances:**
- Know what gets hot and how hot
- Keep flammable items at safe distance
- Never use appliances near water
- Unplug before leaving the room

## The Statistics Behind Prevention

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Most burn injuries happen in the home, and most happen during normal activities (cooking, bathing) rather than emergencies. This means nearly all serious burns are preventable through awareness and basic precautions.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

Prevention works because most burns share common causes: not paying full attention, not understanding how hot something is, or putting children in high-risk situations. By identifying these patterns in your own home and routines, you can prevent the burns before they happen.
