## The Four Stages of Fire

Like all living things, fires have a life cycle. Understanding each stage helps you prevent fires, evacuate when necessary, and understand firefighting tactics.

## Stage 1: Ignition (The Birth)

Ignition is the moment when heat, fuel, and oxygen come together and the chemical reaction begins. This happens at the **ignition temperature** (also called the *flash point*) — the minimum temperature at which a material will ignite.

Different materials have different ignition temperatures:
- **Paper**: ~450°F
- **Wood**: ~575°F
- **Gasoline**: ~495°F
- **Cotton fabric**: ~600°F

Ignition sources include sparks, open flames, hot surfaces, friction, and chemical reactions. Often, ignition starts with a tiny flame you could cup in your hand—but that's about to change.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Ignition is the prevention sweet spot. If you can remove heat, fuel, or oxygen *before* ignition temperature is reached, you prevent the fire entirely. This is why safe cooking, electrical maintenance, and fire-safe storage of flammable materials matter so much.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Stage 2: Growth (The Teen Years)

Once ignition occurs, fire enters its growth phase if adequate fuel and oxygen are available. During growth, the fire:
- Spreads to adjacent materials
- Releases more heat
- Creates smoke and toxic gases
- Increases in temperature

The growth phase can be slow (a smoldering cigarette in furniture) or explosive (spilled gasoline ignited by a spark). A small campfire grows as you add more wood; a raging house fire grows as it spreads from room to room.

**Critical fact:** During the growth phase, smoke and heat rise toward the ceiling, eventually filling the upper portion of the room. This is why firefighters tell you to crawl low under smoke—the coolest, clearest air is near the floor.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Why Crawling Under Smoke Saves Your Life" >}}
In a room filling with smoke, there's a critical difference between the smoke layer at the ceiling (toxic, hot) and the air near the floor (cooler, less smoky).
- **Drop and crawl** when you see smoke. Keep your head low (within 12 inches of the floor) as you move toward the exit.
- **Hold your breath** if smoke is very thick—you only need to breathe long enough to reach fresh air.
- **Get out completely** before standing up. Don't try to fight the fire or save belongings—smoke can incapacitate you in seconds.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

## Stage 3: Flashover (The Dangerous Peak)

Flashover is the moment when a fire transitions from growing in one area to suddenly igniting *everything* in the room at once. It's a critical threshold that firefighters fear.

During the growth phase, heat and smoke fill the upper portion of a room. Combustible materials (furniture, curtains, walls) absorb this heat. When the temperature of all these materials reaches their ignition temperature simultaneously, they all ignite at once—**flashover**.

Before flashover: One area is burning, smoke fills the room, but you might still escape.
After flashover: The entire room is an inferno. Escape is nearly impossible.

Flashover typically occurs at room temperatures around 1,000°F (well above survival temperature for humans). It can happen in as little as 5-10 minutes from initial ignition.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Flashover is why "get out and stay out" is the golden rule of fire survival. Don't assume you have time to search for people, grab belongings, or hide and wait for help. If there's smoke or flame, evacuate immediately. Flashover can trap and kill firefighters even with equipment—it will certainly kill you.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Stage 4: Decay (The End)

Decay begins when the fire consumes available fuel or when oxygen becomes depleted. Fire intensity drops, flames shrink, and the fire eventually dies unless new fuel or oxygen is supplied.

In decay, the fire transitions to **smoldering**—slow, low-temperature burning that produces copious amounts of smoke and carbon monoxide. Smoldering fires can reignite suddenly if fresh oxygen is introduced (like opening a door or breaking a window).

This is why firefighters ventilate buildings carefully and why opening windows or doors during a fire can actually make things worse—introducing fresh oxygen can cause a smoldering fire to erupt into flames.

## The Window for Escape

Understanding the fire life cycle reveals a critical truth: **you have a narrow window to escape safely.**

- **Ignition to growth**: Perhaps 3-5 minutes
- **Growth to flashover**: Perhaps 5-15 minutes (varies based on the room, fuel load, and ventilation)
- **Total survival window**: Roughly 10-20 minutes before conditions become unsurvivable

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
In a typical house fire, survival time is often less than 15 minutes from ignition to uninhabitable conditions. This is why sleeping with bedroom doors closed and practicing fire drills with your family literally can mean the difference between life and death. A few minutes' head start is everything.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Fire Growth Variables

Not all fires follow the same timeline. Growth speed depends on:

**Fuel load** — How much combustible material is in the fire's path? A room packed with furniture will grow faster than an empty room.

**Ventilation** — How much oxygen is available? A fire in a sealed room will transition to decay faster; a fire with good ventilation will grow faster.

**Thermal properties of materials** — How quickly do walls and furniture absorb and release heat? Different materials affect flashover timing.

**Configuration** — Room size, ceiling height, and material arrangement all affect fire behavior.

This is why firefighters arrive with uncertainty about fire conditions—every building is different, every fire behaves slightly differently. They're trained to recognize signs of flashover and evacuate before it happens.
