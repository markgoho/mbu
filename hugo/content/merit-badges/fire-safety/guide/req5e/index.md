{{< drg/requirement number="5e" >}}
Determine if, and what types of, smoke and CO alarms are required in homes in your community. List what types of smoke and CO alarms your home has, their locations, and their expiration dates.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Smoke and CO alarms are the difference between life and death in many fires. This requirement asks you to research local regulations, then inventory what your home has. Understanding alarm types and requirements is critical fire safety knowledge.

## Why Alarms Matter

Alarms give you the most critical gift in a fire: **time to escape**. In a structure fire, you typically have 2-3 minutes before the house becomes uninhabitable due to heat and smoke. Without an alarm, that time vanishes while the fire spreads undetected. With an alarm, you wake up or are alerted within seconds of fire detection.

**The statistics:**
- Homes without alarms have 2x the death rate of homes with alarms
- Alarms wake people at night (when most fire deaths occur)
- Alarms allow faster escape
- Alarms catch early-stage fires before they spread

## Types of Smoke Alarms

Smoke alarms detect visible smoke particles. There are two main detection technologies:

### Ionization Alarms

**How it works:**
- Contains radioactive material (Americium-241) that ionizes air molecules
- When smoke enters, it disrupts the ion flow
- Circuit detects the disruption and triggers alarm
- Detects fast-flaming fires quickly

**Best for:**
- Flaming fires (kitchen, fast-spreading fires)
- Early warning on fires with visible smoke
- Places where people are active

**Not best for:**
- Smoldering fires (slow-burning, lots of smoke but less flame)
- Bedrooms (slower response)

**Cost:** Usually cheaper ($10-25)

### Photoelectric Alarms

**How it works:**
- Contains light source and light sensor
- Light normally doesn't reach sensor
- When smoke enters, it scatters light onto sensor
- Scattered light triggers alarm
- Detects smoldering fires quickly

**Best for:**
- Smoldering fires (slow-burning materials)
- Bedrooms (catches slow fires before sleep is interrupted)
- Areas with paper/wood/textile fires

**Not best for:**
- Fast flaming fires (slower response)

**Cost:** Similar to ionization ($10-25)

### Dual-Sensor Alarms

**How it works:**
- Combines both ionization and photoelectric technologies
- Most expensive but most comprehensive
- Detects both fast-flaming and slow-smoldering fires

**Best for:**
- Any location (most versatile)
- Homes wanting maximum protection
- Critical areas like bedrooms

**Cost:** More expensive ($20-40)

### Smart/Connected Alarms

**How it works:**
- WiFi-enabled alarms that send alerts to your phone
- Can be silenced via app
- May show battery status remotely
- Can have lights to show smoke detection

**Advantages:**
- Know immediately if alarm goes off when you're away
- Can silence nuisance alarms remotely
- Better monitoring of maintenance needs

**Disadvantages:**
- More expensive ($30-100+)
- Require WiFi
- More complex troubleshooting

## Types of CO Alarms

Carbon monoxide alarms detect the gas using a chemical sensor that changes color or triggers electronic detection when exposed to CO. Unlike smoke, CO is invisible and odorless—you can't detect it without an alarm.

### Electrochemical Sensors

**How it works:**
- Chemical gel reacts to CO exposure
- Electrical current changes when CO is detected
- Alarm triggers at 70 ppm (parts per million) over 60 minutes, or 400 ppm over 15 minutes
- Most common type in homes

**Advantages:**
- Relatively affordable ($20-50)
- Reliable for residential use
- Good false-alarm record (less prone to false alarms than some types)

**Disadvantages:**
- Sensor may degrade over time
- May not work in very cold conditions

### Metal Oxide Sensor

**How it works:**
- Semiconductor reacts to CO
- Changes electrical resistance in presence of CO
- Triggers when CO detected

**Advantages:**
- Very sensitive (can detect very low CO levels)
- Works in cold
- Faster response than electrochemical in some cases

**Disadvantages:**
- More expensive
- Occasionally false alarms from cooking/combustion byproducts

## Dual-Function Alarms

Some alarms detect both smoke AND carbon monoxide in one unit. These are convenient:
- **Advantage:** One alarm handles two hazards
- **Advantage:** Less equipment to maintain
- **Disadvantage:** More expensive ($30-60)
- **Disadvantage:** If one sensor fails, you may not know which one

## Local Requirements: Research Task

Your community likely has specific requirements. Find out by:

### Check Your Local Fire Code

**Options:**
1. **Call your fire department** — Non-emergency line, ask about smoke/CO alarm requirements
2. **City/county website** — Look for fire code or building code information
3. **Ask your landlord/homeowner** — They should know requirements
4. **Contact your state's fire marshal office** — State-level requirements
5. **Ask your merit badge counselor** — They may have this information

### Questions to Ask

**When researching your community's requirements:**

- "Are smoke alarms required in residential homes?" (Usually yes)
- "What type of smoke alarms are required (ionization, photoelectric, dual)?" (Some areas don't specify)
- "Are CO alarms required?" (Becoming more common)
- "Where must alarms be installed?" (Bedrooms, hallways, kitchens?)
- "What size home requires alarms?" (Usually all homes)
- "Are interconnected alarms required?" (Hard-wired or wireless?)
- "Is there a renewal date for alarm batteries or units?" (Alarms expire)

### Typical Requirements (though check YOUR community)

**Smoke Alarms:**
- Required in every home
- Usually required on every level
- Required in bedrooms (or hallway outside bedroom)
- Type may or may not be specified (many allow either ionization or photoelectric)

**CO Alarms:**
- Increasingly required (not yet universal in all areas)
- If required, usually in/near sleeping areas
- May be required only if home has fuel-burning appliances
- May not be required in all-electric homes

## Inventory Your Home

Once you know your community's requirements, inventory YOUR home:

### Smoke Alarm Inventory

For each smoke alarm in your home, record:

| Location | Type* | Battery/Hardwired | Expiration Date |
|----------|-------|------------------|-----------------|
| Bedroom | Dual | Battery | 3/2029 |
| Hallway | Ionization | Battery | 6/2027 |
| Kitchen | Photoelectric | Hardwired | 12/2028 |

*Type: Ionization, Photoelectric, or Dual-Sensor

**How to find expiration date:**
- Look on back of alarm or inside battery compartment
- Listed as "Exp date," "Replace by," or "Expiration"
- Some don't have individual expiration dates (only battery expiration)

**How to determine type:**
- Should be labeled on front or back
- If label worn, check manufacturer specs online (model number on alarm)

### CO Alarm Inventory

| Location | Type* | Battery/Hardwired | Expiration Date |
|----------|-------|------------------|-----------------|
| Hallway near bedrooms | Electrochemical | Battery | 4/2028 |

*Type: Electrochemical, Metal Oxide, or Dual-function (Smoke + CO)

## Understanding Expiration Dates

**Alarms have "replacement dates" because:**

1. **Sensor degradation** — Chemical sensors wear out over time
2. **Component failure** — Electronics age and fail
3. **Dust accumulation** — Particles build up on sensors
4. **Loss of responsiveness** — After 7-10 years, alarms become unreliable

**Typical lifespans:**
- **Smoke alarms:** 8-10 years
- **CO alarms:** 5-7 years (some 2-3 years)
- **Batteries (in battery-powered alarms):** 1 year
- **Hardwired backup batteries:** 1 year

**Important:** Replacing expiration dates is NOT optional. An expired alarm may not work when needed—they fail silently, with no warning they're no longer functional.

## Alarm Placement Matters

Different locations require different protection:

### Bedrooms

**Why critical:** Most fire deaths happen at night when people are asleep and unable to smell smoke. Alarms must wake you.

**Placement:** In the bedroom OR immediately outside the bedroom door
**Type:** Photoelectric or dual-sensor preferred (smoldering fires more common in bedrooms)

### Hallways

**Why important:** Detects fires spreading through house
**Placement:** Main hallway, near stairwells
**Type:** Any type acceptable (both flaming and smoldering possible)

### Kitchen

**Special consideration:** Cooking produces smoke/heat that can trigger false alarms
**Placement:** Not directly above stove (false alarms); instead place in hallway outside kitchen
**Type:** Photoelectric preferred (less prone to cooking smoke false alarms)

### Basement

**Why needed:** Furnaces and water heaters create fire/CO risk
**Placement:** Near furnace and/or on basement stairs
**Type:** Either smoke or CO (depending on location and fuel source)

### Interconnected Alarms

**What they are:** Multiple alarms wired together so one alarm triggers all
**Hard-wired:** Electrically connected; also have battery backup
**Wireless:** Connected by radio frequency
**Advantage:** If one alarm detects fire, ALL alarms sound (everyone wakes up)
**Increasing requirement:** Many jurisdictions now require interconnected alarms

## Red Flags for Alarm Issues

During your inventory, note:
- **No alarms in obvious locations** (bedrooms, hallways)
- **Expired alarms** (dates past expiration)
- **No battery backup** in hardwired systems (dangerous if power goes out)
- **Disabled alarms** (removed batteries or covered with dust)
- **Single alarm in large home** (insufficient coverage)

{{< drg/tip >}}
If your home doesn't have alarms where required, or has expired alarms, discuss with your family. Many fire departments provide free or low-cost alarms. The cost of an alarm ($15-40) is negligible compared to the potential loss of life.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Approximately 40% of homes with smoke alarms don't have them properly maintained—batteries missing, dead, or alarms expired. A dead alarm is as useless as no alarm. Maintenance (covered in Req 5f) is as important as having them.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Presenting Your Findings

When discussing with your counselor, have ready:

1. **Community requirements** — What does your local fire code require?
2. **Your home's inventory** — List by location, type, expiration
3. **Gaps identified** — Are there required alarms missing?
4. **Expired alarms** — Any that need replacement?
5. **Installation questions** — Proper placement? Hardwired vs. battery?
