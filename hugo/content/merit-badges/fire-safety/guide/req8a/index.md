{{< drg/requirement number="8a" >}}
Explain how the Leave No Trace Seven Principles apply to camping fires.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Camping fires are part of Scouting culture, but they must be done responsibly to protect wilderness areas. Leave No Trace is an outdoor ethics framework that guides how to minimize impact on natural areas. Applying it to campfires means future generations can enjoy the same wilderness.

## Leave No Trace Seven Principles (Quick Overview)

1. **Plan and Prepare** — Know regulations, conditions, skills
2. **Travel and Camp on Durable Surfaces** — Don't damage vegetation
3. **Dispose of Waste Properly** — Pack out what you pack in
4. **Leave What You Find** — Don't remove natural items
5. **Minimize Campfire Impacts** — Use fires responsibly
6. **Respect Wildlife** — Don't disturb animals
7. **Be Considerate of Others** — Respect other users' experience

Each principle applies to fires. Here's how:

## Principle 1: Plan and Prepare

### Before Building Any Fire

**Research:**
- Is fire allowed in destination? (Some areas ban fires; use stove instead)
- What are local fire regulations? (Fire season, fire bans, permits)
- What weather conditions exist? (High winds = higher danger)
- What's the elevation? (Above treeline, avoid fires; wood is scarce)
- What's the water availability? (Need water for extinguishing)

**Skills needed:**
- Know how to build and safely manage a fire
- Know local regulations before arriving
- Have alternative cooking method (camp stove) if fires banned
- Check fire danger ratings (Red Flag Warnings = no fires)

**Example:** Before camping, check weather.gov or fire department websites for fire danger ratings. If critical fire danger is forecasted, plan to use camp stove instead of campfire.

## Principle 2: Travel and Camp on Durable Surfaces

### Campfire Location

**Choose carefully:**
- Use established campsites with existing fire rings (don't create new rings)
- Only in areas where fires are permitted
- In deserts/arid areas: rockback (bare rock) preferred for fire ring
- In forests: clear area of dead branches, leaves, needles (12-foot radius minimum)
- Away from vegetation that could catch fire

**Don't:**
- Create new fire ring if established one exists
- Build fire on vegetation, moss, lichen
- Build fire in pristine areas (causes permanent scarring)

**Impact:** Using existing fire rings concentrates impact rather than spreading scarring across many sites. New fire rings leave permanent marks for years or decades.

## Principle 3: Dispose of Waste Properly

### Campfire Cleanup

**Ash disposal:**
- If established fire ring exists: scatter cold ashes around camp (minerals return to soil) or pack out ashes
- In pristine areas: pack out all ashes
- Spread ashes widely (not in concentrated piles)
- Never bury ashes (they re-surface, wildlife digs them up)

**Leftover wood:**
- If you didn't use it, leave it for next campers (don't pack out)
- Don't cut live branches for firewood
- Don't leave unburned logs stacked (unsightly, suggests human impact)

**Unburned garbage:**
- Completely burn all burnable waste
- Carry out non-burnable waste (cans, glass, plastic)
- Never leave anything at campsite
- Stir ashes to ensure complete burning of scraps

## Principle 4: Leave What You Find

### Campfire Supplies from Nature

**Dead wood (okay to use):**
- Dead branches on ground
- Downed trees (fallen wood)
- Aim for "armpit-thickness" or thinner (burns easier)
- Takes minimal searching; doesn't damage living trees

**Don't:**
- Chop live branches (kills tree; looks terrible)
- Strip bark (damages tree for years)
- Cut down small trees or saplings
- Take more wood than you'll burn
- Leave partially-burned logs (looks ugly, creates hazards)

**Impact:** Using only dead wood ensures trees remain healthy. Harvesting live wood damages forests for aesthetic and ecological reasons.

## Principle 5: Minimize Campfire Impacts

### This is the Core Principle for Fires

**Minimize means reduce:**
- Use camp stove instead of campfire when possible (no trace left)
- Build fire only when needed (heat/warmth, not just ambiance)
- Use small fires (heat for cooking/warmth, not entertainment)
- Build fire in one location (not multiple small fires scattered)

**Impacts campfires cause:**
- Scarring of earth (dark rings visible for decades)
- Removal of wood (affects wildlife that depends on dead wood)
- Fire risk if not managed (escapes, spreads damage)
- Aesthetic impact (wilderness looks "used")
- Smoke affecting air quality

**Solutions:**
- Camp stove: No trace, lightweight, efficient
- Cold meals: Eliminate cooking fire entirely
- Small, concentrated fire: Less wood needed, less impact
- Complete extinguishment: Reduces hazard

**When fire is justified:**
- Group size (large group, one fire instead of multiple)
- Cold weather (fire for warmth is necessary)
- Morale (occasional fire for Scouting tradition acceptable in high-use areas)

## Principle 6: Respect Wildlife

### Fire and Animals

**Don't use fire to:**
- Clear wildlife away (disrespects animals, causes stress)
- Cook strong-smelling foods (attracts bears/wildlife to camp)
- Burn waste near wildlife habitat

**Proper practices:**
- Cook downwind of sleeping area
- Use bear bags or bear canisters for food storage
- Never approach wildlife; fire might scare them
- Avoid fire in wildlife breeding/nesting areas

**Impact:** Wildlife depends on natural areas. Campfires shouldn't disrupt animals or habitat.

## Principle 7: Be Considerate of Others

### Other Campers and Users

**Fire considerations:**
- Don't block others' views with smoke
- Smoke shouldn't drift to other campsites
- Fire light shouldn't disturb others trying to sleep
- Noise associated with fire (chopping wood) limited to daytime

**Wilderness preservation:**
- Other people want to see pristine wilderness
- Multiple fire scars visible throughout area suggest overuse
- Respect others' desire for unscarred natural experience

## Practical Leave No Trace Campfire Plan

### Before Camping

1. **Check regulations** — Fire allowed? Any restrictions?
2. **Check weather** — Fire danger? Fire bans?
3. **Plan cooking** — Will you need fire or use stove?
4. **Gather knowledge** — What's the terrain? Where's water?

### At Campsite

1. **Locate fire ring** — Use existing if available
2. **Clear area** — 12-foot radius of duff, branches, dry grass
3. **Gather wood** — Only dead branches from ground
4. **Build small fire** — Just what you need
5. **Use responsibly** — Cook/warm only; minimize duration
6. **Completely extinguish** — Cool to touch, stir ashes

### Before Leaving

1. **Scatter ashes** — Return nutrients to soil
2. **Disassemble ring** — If newly built, scatter rocks
3. **Leave no trace** — Should look like no one camped there
4. **Check water source** — No ash/remnants in water

## The Philosophy Behind Leave No Trace

**Wilderness is shrinking.** Every campsite, every fire scar, every removed tree represents loss of pristine landscape. By applying Leave No Trace principles, you:

- **Preserve wilderness** — Future generations see undamaged landscape
- **Protect wildlife** — Animals' habitats remain healthy
- **Respect others** — Other users enjoy natural setting
- **Model ethics** — Show younger Scouts how to camp responsibly

**The goal:** Camping without evidence of human presence—"leave no trace."

{{< drg/tip >}}
When in doubt, use your camp stove instead of a campfire. A camp stove is Leave No Trace, no smoke, no ash, no scarring. Campfires are acceptable, but only when they're truly needed and managed responsibly.
{{< /drg/tip >}}
