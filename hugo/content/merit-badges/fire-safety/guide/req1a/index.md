## What Exactly Is Fire?

For most of human history, fire was a mystery. It felt alive—it consumed, it grew, it could be killed. But modern chemistry has revealed the truth: fire is not a "thing" at all. It's a rapid chemical reaction.

**Fire is combustion—a chemical reaction that occurs when three essential elements combine: fuel, oxygen, and heat.** When these three come together, the fuel molecules break down and release energy in the form of light and heat. That's fire.

Think of it this way: wood sitting in your fireplace is just wood. Oxygen surrounds it everywhere. But nothing happens until you add heat—a match, a spark, or warm embers. Once the wood gets hot enough, its molecules start reacting with the oxygen in the air, and boom—you have flames.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Fire is the only chemical reaction that can sustain itself. Once it starts, it produces its own heat to keep the reaction going—unless you remove one of the three essential elements.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## The Three Elements of Combustion

Each element plays a critical role:

**Fuel** is anything that can burn—wood, gasoline, paper, propane, fabric, even metal dust. Fuels contain chemical energy locked in their molecules.

**Oxygen** is the oxidizer. It's all around you in the air (about 21% of our atmosphere). When fuel molecules encounter oxygen at high enough temperatures, they combine and release their stored energy.

**Heat** is the ignition source. It provides the energy needed to break the bonds in fuel molecules and start the reaction. Heat can come from a flame, friction, electrical sparks, spontaneous chemical reactions, or even sunshine focusing through a lens.

Remove any one of these three, and combustion stops. This is why firefighters use different techniques to extinguish fires depending on what's burning.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Your House Is Full of Fuel" >}}
You live in a building filled with potential fuel for fire. Materials like wood framing, insulation, fabric, paper, plastics, and paint all burn under the right conditions. This is why house fires spread so quickly—once ignition occurs, fuel surrounds the flames in every direction.
- **Understand**: Fire doesn't choose its fuel randomly. It will consume whatever is nearest and hottest.
- **Be aware**: Clutter creates fuel pathways. Clear hallways, exits, and bedrooms of unnecessary items that could catch fire.
- **Plan ahead**: Know where the nearest exit is in every room you spend time in.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

## Products of Combustion

When fuel burns, it doesn't just disappear. It transforms. Combustion produces two main products:

**Carbon dioxide and water vapor** are the primary products when complete combustion occurs. But in reality, fires rarely have perfect combustion. Instead, they also produce:

**Carbon monoxide (CO)** is a deadly, colorless, odorless gas. It's produced when there isn't enough oxygen for complete combustion. Carbon monoxide binds to hemoglobin in your blood more readily than oxygen does, starving your cells of oxygen. Even small amounts can cause headaches, dizziness, and confusion—and high concentrations can be fatal within minutes.

**Smoke** is a mixture of tiny particles (soot) and gases. Smoke is as dangerous as flames—it can blind you, choke you, and kill you through carbon monoxide or heat before the flames even reach you. More fire deaths result from smoke inhalation than from actual burns.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
In a fire, smoke kills faster than flames. If you ever see smoke or smell burning, get low (crawl), move toward fresh air immediately, and alert others. Never hide from fire—always evacuate.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Why Understanding Fire Matters

Learning that fire is a chemical reaction might seem purely academic, but it's not. It's the foundation for everything else in this badge. When you understand what fire *is* and what it *needs*, you'll be able to:

- Prevent fires by removing or securing fuel
- Recognize potential ignition sources before they cause problems
- Choose the right fire extinguisher for different types of fires
- Know when to evacuate versus when to attempt to extinguish a small fire
- Understand why firefighters use different tactics for different fires
