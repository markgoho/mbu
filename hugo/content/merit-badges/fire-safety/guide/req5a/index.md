{{< drg/requirement number="5a" >}}
Describe how to prevent the frequent causes of home fires, including unattended cooking, electricity, smoking, clothes dryers, and rechargeable batteries.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Your home is supposed to be the safest place you spend time. Yet more fires start in homes than anywhere else, and most are preventable. Understanding the most common household fire causes means you can walk through your home and eliminate the risks before they become emergencies.

## Unattended Cooking: The #1 Home Fire Cause

Cooking fires are the leading cause of home fire injuries and deaths. The danger comes from the combination of heat, fuel (food and cooking oil), and human distraction.

### Why Unattended Cooking Causes Fires

**When you leave food cooking:**
- Temperature becomes uncontrolled (pot boils over, oil overheats)
- Splashing liquid or oil can ignite
- Other items near stove can catch fire
- Stove tops are hotter than you realize

**Hot oil is especially dangerous:**
- Boils at much higher temperature than water (350-400°F)
- Can ignite spontaneously if overheated (smoke point reached)
- Spreads rapidly if ignited
- Creates dense smoke quickly

### Prevention Strategies for Cooking Fires

**Stovetop Safety:**
- Never leave cooking food unattended—stay in the kitchen while cooking
- If you must leave, turn off the burner (it can wait)
- Use back burners when possible so kids can't reach pots
- Turn pot handles inward so no one bumps them
- Keep a clear 3-foot zone around the stove—nothing hanging, sitting, or placed there
- Don't wear loose sleeves or clothing that can drag across burners
- Keep the stovetop clear of items (paper, bags, utensils)

**Oil Safety:**
- Never fill a pan more than 2/3 full of oil
- Don't leave hot oil unattended
- Monitor temperature—oil should not smoke
- Never use water on an oil fire (use baking soda, salt, or a fire extinguisher)
- If oil catches fire, cover with a metal lid to smother
- Use a splatter screen when frying to prevent spatters

**Microwave Awareness:**
- Watch for superheated liquids (water heated in microwave without agitation becomes hotter than boiling)
- Never place metal in microwave
- Don't use microwave to heat things in closed containers
- Be careful removing containers (liquids may boil suddenly when disturbed)

**Oven Safety:**
- Don't leave oven unattended with food cooking
- Be careful opening oven door (steam can burn)
- Use oven mitts properly
- Don't touch hot racks or interior

## Electrical Hazards: Hidden Fires

Electrical fires are often hidden—the fire starts inside walls or within appliances where you can't see it developing until damage is extensive.

### Common Electrical Fire Causes

**Aging Appliances:**
- Worn insulation breaks down, exposing wires
- Loose connections generate heat
- Motors wear out and short-circuit
- Older appliances weren't designed for modern power demands

**Damaged Cords and Plugs:**
- Cut, pinched, or crushed cords expose live wires
- Frayed insulation creates electrical hazards
- Cracked plugs or missing grounding pins are dangerous
- Wet cords short out (especially dangerous)

**Overloaded Circuits:**
- Too many devices on one circuit draws excessive power
- Wires overheat (like a resistor)
- Circuit breaker may not trip fast enough
- Extension cords are especially prone to overload

**Poor Connections:**
- Loose plugs in outlets
- Outlets that no longer grip plugs firmly
- Faulty wiring done by someone without expertise
- Outlets damaged from water exposure

### Prevention Strategies for Electrical Hazards

**Appliance Safety:**
- Replace appliances that are 10+ years old or show signs of wear
- Unplug appliances when not in use (eliminates standby electrical hazards)
- Don't use damaged appliances—repair or discard them
- Look for safety certifications (UL, ETL listing) on new appliances

**Cord and Plug Safety:**
- Inspect cords regularly for damage
- Replace damaged cords before using (don't wrap with tape—this is temporary)
- Never run cords under carpets or rugs (heat builds up)
- Don't nail or staple cords to walls (breaks insulation)
- Keep cords away from water and wet areas
- Use surge protectors to regulate power

**Circuit Management:**
- Don't plug multiple high-draw devices into one outlet (no daisy-chaining power strips)
- Know which circuits power which rooms
- If a circuit keeps tripping, that's a sign of problems—call an electrician
- Use GFCI outlets in bathrooms and kitchens (special outlets that trip on ground faults)

**Home Wiring:**
- Hire licensed electricians for any electrical work
- Have wiring inspected if home is old
- Update wiring if it's outdated (knob-and-tube, aluminum)
- Ensure outlets are properly grounded

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Never attempt your own electrical repairs unless you're trained. Electricity causes electrocution, arc flash injuries, and fires. Improper repairs create hidden hazards that might not cause problems until they cause major fires.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Smoking-Related Fires

Smoking is the leading cause of fire deaths in America. Most smoking fires occur when people smoke carelessly—in bed, while drowsy, or where flammable materials are nearby.

### How Smoking Causes Fires

**Unattended Smoking Materials:**
- Burning cigarette left in ashtray ignites nearby items
- Cigarette ash falls onto furniture or bedding
- Cigarettes rolled in paper continue burning without supervision

**Smoking in Bed:**
- Most dangerous smoking behavior
- Person falls asleep with cigarette
- Bedding or mattress catches fire
- Person may be asleep during critical fire spread minutes

**Careless Disposal:**
- Cigarettes thrown in trash with other materials
- Hot cigarette butt in bag or package
- Smoldering cigarette buried in trash builds heat

**Smoking Around Flammables:**
- Smoking near curtains, upholstered furniture, newspapers
- Cigarette falls onto flammable materials
- Person distracted and doesn't notice hazard

### Prevention Strategies for Smoking-Related Fires

**Smoking Safety:**
- Never smoke in bed (the #1 smoking fire scenario)
- Use a sturdy ashtray that won't tip
- Never leave burning cigarettes unattended
- Ensure cigarette is fully extinguished before discarding
- Test disposal: "dead cold" test—the cigarette shouldn't be hot to touch

**Disposal:**
- Stub out completely in water (don't rely on ashtray alone)
- Place in fire-safe trash (not bags or paper trash)
- Check ashtrays before emptying (still-burning material)
- Never discard in flower pots or other containers

**Environmental Control:**
- Keep flammable materials (curtains, bedding, upholstery) away from smoking areas
- Don't smoke around open flames or heat sources
- Avoid smoking when drowsy or medicated
- Designate specific smoking areas away from bedrooms and sleep areas

**Support:**
- Smoking cessation is the best fire prevention
- Many free programs help people quit
- Talk with a healthcare provider about quitting

## Clothes Dryer Fires

Clothes dryer fires are often overlooked because they seem like minor appliances. But dryers generate intense heat and use lint as fuel—a dangerous combination.

### Why Clothes Dryers Catch Fire

**Lint Buildup:**
- Every load of laundry creates lint (fibers from clothing)
- Lint screen captures most lint, but some escapes
- Escaped lint collects in ducts and around the dryer
- Lint is extremely flammable (essentially tinder)

**Heat Source:**
- Dryers heat to 150-200°F
- Lint catches fire at around 400°F in the hot zone
- Confined lint + heat = fire risk
- Extended drying cycles increase temperature

**Poor Maintenance:**
- Lint screen not cleaned between loads
- Vent duct clogged with months of lint
- Dryer vent outside blocked or sealed
- No inspection for internal lint buildup

### Prevention Strategies for Dryer Fires

**After Every Load:**
- Clean lint screen (remove all lint before next load)
- Feel the screen to ensure complete removal
- Check around the dryer for escaped lint
- Ensure vent air is flowing out (feel at vent outside)

**Regular Maintenance:**
- Clean dryer vent duct annually (can be vacuumed or snake-cleaned)
- Inspect behind and underneath dryer for lint accumulation
- Replace damaged or crushed vent hoses
- Ensure outside vent opens freely (not covered by snow, leaves, etc.)

**Dryer Safety:**
- Don't run dryer unattended for extended periods
- If dryer gets excessively hot, stop using and call repairperson
- Don't dry items with flammable residues (oil-soaked rags)
- Use exhaust vents designed for dryers (not generic fan vents)

**When to Replace:**
- If dryer is 10+ years old, consider replacement (efficiency and safety)
- If vent system is damaged and can't be properly cleared
- If dryer overheats repeatedly

## Rechargeable Battery Fires

Modern rechargeable batteries (lithium-ion) power phones, laptops, power tools, and e-bikes. When damaged or overcharged, they can catch fire.

### Why Rechargeable Batteries Catch Fire

**Battery Design:**
- Lithium-ion batteries store intense energy in small space
- Separator inside battery prevents short circuits
- If separator is damaged, battery short-circuits
- Short circuit generates heat rapidly
- Heat can ignite battery chemicals and surrounding materials

**Damage Causes:**
- Physical damage (dropping, crushing, water exposure)
- Manufacturing defects
- Cheap third-party chargers (improper voltage/current)
- Overcharging
- Heat exposure (leaving in hot car)
- Age-related degradation

### Prevention Strategies for Battery Safety

**Device Charging:**
- Use manufacturer chargers when possible (third-party chargers may be unsafe)
- Don't leave devices charging overnight or unattended for extended periods
- Remove batteries from chargers once fully charged
- Keep devices away from heat sources while charging
- Never charge damaged or swollen batteries

**Device Use:**
- Don't drop or physically damage battery-powered devices
- Keep devices out of water and moisture
- Avoid extreme temperatures (very hot or very cold)
- Replace damaged devices or batteries
- Don't attempt to open or repair batteries yourself

**Storage:**
- Store charged devices in cool, dry location
- Don't store in vehicles (heat exposure)
- Keep away from metal objects that could short-circuit
- Store with some charge (not fully depleted or fully charged)

**Disposal:**
- Never throw lithium-ion batteries in trash
- Take to electronics recycling center
- Some retailers accept old batteries for recycling
- Proper disposal prevents landfill fires

{{< drg/tip >}}
If a rechargeable battery or device is swelling, getting hot, or making hissing sounds, stop using it immediately. This indicates internal failure. Move to a safe location away from flammable materials and contact the manufacturer or recycling center about safe disposal.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Home Fire Causes Summary

{{< drg/checklist title="Home Fire Prevention" subtitle="Check each area of your home" >}}
- **Kitchen:** Unattended cooking prevented, stovetop clear, oil safety understood, microwave safety practiced
- **Electrical:** Appliances inspected for damage, cords protected, circuits not overloaded, outlets functioning properly
- **Smoking:** Smoking never in bed, cigarettes fully extinguished, disposal safe, smoking areas designated
- **Laundry:** Lint screen cleaned after every load, vent duct clear, dryer exterior clean
- **Electronics:** Devices charged with proper chargers, not left charging unattended, damaged batteries not used
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

These five categories account for the vast majority of home fires. By addressing each one in your home, you dramatically reduce fire risk.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
If a home has working smoke detectors AND someone who reacts quickly, the fire death rate drops by about 50%. Smoke detectors give you those critical early minutes to escape. You'll learn more about alarms in Req 5e.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}
