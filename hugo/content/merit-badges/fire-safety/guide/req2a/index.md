## Why Fires Are Classified

Not all fires burn the same way. A wood fire behaves completely differently from an electrical fire or an oil fire. Firefighters classify fires so they know which extinguisher to use, which tactics work, and which approaches will make things worse.

Using the wrong extinguisher on the wrong fire can be ineffective—or dangerous. Water on an electrical fire creates a shock hazard. Water on a grease fire makes it spread. Understanding fire classes is literally a matter of life and death.

## Class A: Ordinary Combustible Fires

Class A fires involve **ordinary combustible materials**:
- Wood
- Paper
- Cloth
- Rubber
- Plastics (most types)
- Straw and hay

These materials have one thing in common: they're not conductive (don't conduct electricity), and they leave ash when burned.

**Best Extinguisher:** Water or wet chemical. Water absorbs heat and cools the fuel below ignition temperature.

**Where You'll Find Them:** Homes, offices, forests, campfires, construction sites—most everyday environments.

{{< drg/tip >}}
If you see a small Class A fire (trash can, cardboard box, small woodpile) and you're confident in your ability and have a clear escape route, a garden hose or water bucket can extinguish it. But if in doubt, evacuate and call the fire department.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Class B: Flammable Liquid Fires

Class B fires involve **flammable and combustible liquids and gases**:
- Gasoline
- Diesel fuel
- Oil and grease
- Paint thinner
- Propane and butane
- Acetone

These materials burn hot and fast. Gasoline can ignite at -46°F, and the vapors themselves burn—not the liquid.

**The Water Problem:** Water doesn't mix with oil or gasoline; it sinks to the bottom of the container and causes the fire to spread as the heated fuel boils violently.

**Best Extinguisher:** Foam, dry chemical, or CO₂. These smother the fuel or disrupt the chemical reaction.

**Where You'll Find Them:** Garages, gas stations, workshops, boats, kitchens (grease fires), and anywhere flammable liquids are stored or used.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Never, ever use water on a grease or oil fire. If a pan of oil catches fire in your kitchen, smother it with a lid or use a Class B extinguisher. Using water will cause the fire to spread explosively.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Class C: Electrical Fires

Class C fires involve **energized electrical equipment**:
- Electrical wires and outlets
- Appliances (ovens, microwaves, TVs)
- Electrical panels
- Motors and machinery
- Batteries (especially lithium-ion)

The fire isn't the electricity; it's the insulation, wiring, and surrounding materials burning when exposed to high heat or when electrical current causes arcing or overheating.

**The Water Problem:** Water conducts electricity. Using water on an electrical fire could electrocute you.

**The Challenge:** Once the equipment cools and depressurizes, it's no longer a Class C fire—it becomes Class A or Class B depending on what's burning. Many Class C fires are initially Class A or B that involve electrical equipment.

**Best Extinguisher:** Dry chemical or CO₂. These extinguish without conducting electricity. Once the fire is out and the equipment cools, the classification changes.

**Where You'll Find Them:** Everywhere there's electricity—homes, offices, workshops, cars, boats.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
If an electrical appliance catches fire, always unplug it first (if you can do so safely without touching metal) or turn off power at the breaker. This converts it from a Class C fire to Class A or B, making it easier to fight.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Class D: Metal Fires

Class D fires involve **combustible metals**:
- Magnesium
- Titanium
- Aluminum (in powder or dust form, not solid blocks)
- Potassium
- Sodium
- Lithium (common in rechargeable batteries)

These metals burn at extremely high temperatures—sometimes over 3,000°F—and react violently with water.

**The Water Problem:** Water causes violent reactions with metals like sodium and potassium. Some metals produce hydrogen gas which ignites and explodes.

**Best Extinguisher:** Special Class D dry powder (Class D extinguishers contain agents like sodium chloride, graphite, or other specialized compounds). Each metal may require a different agent, so the extinguisher should be labeled for the specific metal.

**Where You'll Find Them:** Less common in homes, but present in:
- Workshops and laboratories
- Aircraft and aerospace facilities
- Some vehicle batteries
- Magnesium tools and equipment

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
If you encounter a metal fire and don't know what metal is burning, evacuate immediately. Metal fires burn at extreme temperatures and require specialized equipment. Never use water. Even attempting to use the wrong extinguisher can make the fire worse.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Class K: Cooking Oil and Fat Fires

Class K fires are a special subset of Class B fires—they involve cooking oils and fats at high temperatures.

**Why a Separate Class?** Cooking oil fires require different techniques than other flammable liquid fires. Water causes them to spread explosively, and standard Class B extinguishers can sometimes make them worse.

**Best Extinguisher:** Wet chemical extinguishers specifically designed for Class K. These cool the oil below ignition temperature while creating a foam layer that smothers vapors.

**In Your Kitchen:** Many fire departments recommend simply turning off the heat and covering the pan with a lid or baking sheet. This smothers the fire. Let it cool completely before moving it.

**Where You'll Find Them:** Commercial kitchens, restaurants, and home kitchens where you cook with oil.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Responding to a Grease Fire" >}}
A pan of hot oil catches fire on your stovetop. What do you do?
- **Step 1**: Stay calm. Grease fires look scary but are containable.
- **Step 2**: Turn off the heat immediately.
- **Step 3**: If safe, cover the pan with a metal lid, baking sheet, or wet cloth to smother flames.
- **Step 4**: Leave the cover on until the oil cools completely (usually 30+ minutes).
- **Step 5**: If you can't safely cover it, use a Class K extinguisher if available, or evacuate and call 911.
- **Never do this**: Don't move the pan. Don't throw water on it. Don't try to carry it outside.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

## Quick Reference: Fire Classes

| Class | Fuel Type | Examples | Best Extinguisher | Never Use |
|-------|-----------|----------|-------------------|-----------|
| **A** | Ordinary combustibles | Wood, paper, cloth, trash | Water, Wet Chemical | Dry chem, CO₂ |
| **B** | Flammable liquids | Gasoline, oil, paint, propane | Foam, Dry Chem, CO₂ | Water |
| **C** | Electrical equipment | Outlets, appliances, wiring | Dry Chem, CO₂ | Water |
| **D** | Combustible metals | Magnesium, sodium, lithium | Class D powder | Water, Foam, CO₂ |
| **K** | Cooking oils and fats | Deep fryers, commercial kitchens | Wet Chemical | Water, Standard B agents |
