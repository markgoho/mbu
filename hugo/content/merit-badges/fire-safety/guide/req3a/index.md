## The Life-Saving Sequence

If your clothing catches fire—the most terrifying fire emergency you can experience—the correct response in the first 10 seconds determines the difference between minor injuries and catastrophic burns or death.

The **Stop, Drop, Roll, Cool, Call** technique is taught to firefighters, emergency responders, and Scouts worldwide. It's simple, memorable, and it saves lives.

## Step 1: STOP

**Don't panic. Don't run.**

The instant you realize your clothing is on fire, **stop moving immediately**. Running accelerates oxygen flow to the flames and fans the fire, making it spread faster and burn hotter. A person running while on fire looks like a human torch—the flames stream behind them and intensify.

Stopping gives you a moment to assess the situation and begin the next steps.

**Do this:** Plant your feet and take a breath.

## Step 2: DROP

**Drop to the ground immediately.**

Your goal is to get the flames as low as possible and make them less likely to reach your face and lungs. Lying flat on the ground prevents flames from reaching your head and face during the roll.

**How to do it:** Fall or drop to the ground. Don't jump; just let gravity do the work. You're not trying to land gracefully—you're trying to get horizontal quickly.

**Do this:** Fall flat on your back or front side (whichever gets you down fastest).

## Step 3: ROLL

**Roll back and forth repeatedly to smother the flames.**

This is the critical action. By rolling, you:
- Use the friction and pressure of your body to smother flames
- Move the fire around your body so no one spot stays on fire
- Push flames away from your face and lungs
- Eventually extinguish the fire through lack of oxygen

Roll continuously from front to back to front until flames are out. Don't stop rolling until you're completely confident the fire is extinguished.

**Do this:** Roll in one direction, then back in the other direction. Keep rolling for 10–15 seconds or until flames stop.

**Important:** Your arms should cover your face and head as you roll to protect these critical areas from flames and heat.

## Step 4: COOL

**Cool your burns as quickly as possible.**

Once the flames are out, burn treatment begins immediately. Cool water applied to burns:
- Stops the burn from progressing deeper into skin
- Reduces pain significantly
- Helps prevent infection
- Prevents burn shock

**Cool water action:** Get to running water or douse yourself with cool (not ice-cold) water for at least 10–20 minutes. Cool water should be applied until pain is relieved or significantly reduced.

**Where to find water:**
- Sink or shower
- Hose
- Bucket
- Any source of cool (not cold) water

**What NOT to do:**
- Don't use ice water (causes ice-crystal damage to burned tissue)
- Don't apply ice directly (same reason)
- Don't use any ointments, grease, or oils
- Don't break blisters

**Duration:** Keep applying cool water for at least 10 minutes. Longer cooling (20–30 minutes) is better for serious burns.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Burns continue to damage deeper layers of skin even after the flames are out. The heat has to be transferred away from the wound. Cool water is the most effective immediate treatment. Hospital burn centers are clear: cool water, immediately, for as long as possible.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Step 5: CALL

**Call 911 immediately.**

Even if burns seem minor, call 911 if:
- Any burn is larger than a postage stamp
- The person is in pain
- There's visible charring or deep redness
- The burn wraps around the body (restricts circulation)
- The person is young (children) or elderly
- The burn is on the face, hands, genitals, or joints (high-sensitivity areas)

**What to tell the dispatcher:**
- "I have a burn injury"
- Describe the size and severity
- Give your location clearly
- Follow dispatcher instructions

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Demonstration: Stop, Drop, Roll, Cool, Call" >}}
Here's how to demonstrate or practice this technique safely:

**Practice (without fire, obviously):**
- **Stop**: Stand still and take a breath
- **Drop**: Drop to the ground (in a controlled way during practice)
- **Roll**: Roll back and forth across the ground 5–10 times
- **Cool**: If practicing, simulate running to cool water
- **Call**: Simulate calling 911 or have someone call while you're managing the "burn"

**Real emergency:**
- **Stop**: Don't run (I know it's hard, but running makes it worse)
- **Drop**: Fall to the ground
- **Roll**: Roll back and forth for 10–15 seconds or until fire is out
- **Cool**: Get to water immediately and apply for 10+ minutes
- **Call**: Call 911 while applying cool water if possible (or have someone call)

The entire sequence takes less than 30 seconds. Speed matters.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

## Why Each Step Matters

| Step | Why It Helps | Consequence of Skipping |
|------|-------------|------------------------|
| **Stop** | Prevents running, which fans flames | Flames intensify; more burn area |
| **Drop** | Gets you horizontal, face away from flames | Flames reach face and lungs; more severe burns |
| **Roll** | Smothers flames and distributes heat | Flames continue burning until clothing burns through |
| **Cool** | Stops the burn at the skin surface | Burn continues into deeper layers; worse scarring |
| **Call** | Gets professional medical help | Burns may go untreated; infection risk increases |

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

**Mistake 1: Running**
The instinct is to run and find help or water. But running creates an oxygen vacuum that feeds the fire and makes it worse. **Stop first, then roll.**

**Mistake 2: Wrapping in a blanket instead of rolling**
A blanket can trap heat and continue burning you. **Rolling is better**—it actually smothers the flames.

**Mistake 3: Incomplete rolling**
People sometimes "pat out" flames instead of rolling. Patting doesn't generate enough friction. **Keep rolling until you're absolutely sure flames are out.**

**Mistake 4: Not cooling long enough**
Impatient people cool for 30 seconds and stop. The burn continues progressing into deeper skin. **Cool for at least 10 minutes, longer if you can manage it.**

**Mistake 5: Using ice water**
Ice water feels better but damages tissue more. **Use cool tap water, not cold or ice.**

## Clothing Fires: How They Happen

Understanding how clothing fires start helps you prevent them:

**Loose clothing near flames:** Sleeves dangling over a stovetop, nightgowns near candles, scarves near fireplaces—loose fabric ignites easily and spreads quickly.

**Synthetic fabrics:** Polyester and other synthetics melt instead of just burning, which causes deeper burns. Natural fibers (cotton, wool) burn but don't melt.

**Flammable liquids on clothing:** Gasoline, paint thinner, or other flammable liquids on your clothes can be ignited by any spark.

**Dust or lint on clothing:** Sawdust, flour dust, or lint are extremely flammable and can ignite explosively.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Wear fitted clothes around flames. Avoid loose sleeves, scarves, and long nightgowns when cooking or near fireplaces. If you must wear long, loose clothing, tie it back or tuck it in.
{{< /drg/tip >}}
