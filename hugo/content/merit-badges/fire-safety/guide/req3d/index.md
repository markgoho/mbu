{{< drg/requirement number="3d" >}}
Review the prevention of, symptoms and signs of, and treatment of airway burns and carbon monoxide (CO) poisoning.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Airway burns and carbon monoxide poisoning are invisible killers in fires. Unlike skin burns you can see, these injuries damage the inside of your body where you can't see them. Understanding how they happen and recognizing early symptoms can mean the difference between survival and tragedy.

## Airway Burns: The Hidden Damage

### How Airway Burns Happen

**During fires, three things can damage airways:**

**1. Inhaling Hot Gas and Smoke**
- In intense fires, the air itself becomes dangerously hot
- Hot air can burn throat, lungs, and breathing passages
- May not feel hot to skin but extremely hot in lungs
- More likely in enclosed spaces (rooms, buildings) than open fires

**2. Breathing in Toxic Smoke**
- Burning materials release chemicals and particulates
- Plastic releases chlorine gas
- Foam releases ammonia
- Wood releases formaldehyde
- These chemicals irritate and damage airways even without heat

**3. Swelling from Chemical Irritation**
- Smoke irritates throat and windpipe
- Tissues swell (edema) which can block airway
- This swelling can develop over hours, not immediately
- Most dangerous: swelling that completely closes the airway

### Recognizing Airway Burns: Early Signs Matter

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Recognizing Airway Burns in a Fire Victim" >}}
If someone has been exposed to smoke or hot gases in a fire, watch for these signs:

- **Singed nose hairs or nasal hair removal** — Most obvious early sign; shows intense heat exposure
- **Soot in mouth or throat** — Black particles visible in mouth or spit
- **Hoarseness or voice changes** — Throat irritation altering voice
- **Difficulty swallowing** — Painful or impossible swallowing
- **Drooling or coughing** — Body's reaction to throat irritation
- **Rapid or labored breathing** — Airway narrowing makes breathing harder
- **Wheezing or stridor** — High-pitched or whistling breathing sounds indicate airway problem
- **Shortness of breath** — Even at rest, struggling to breathe

**Critical:** Even if someone seems okay immediately after a fire, airway burns can develop over the next few hours as swelling increases. Never assume someone without immediate respiratory distress is fine.

{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

### Treating Airway Burns

**Immediate response:**

1. **Remove from smoke/heat source immediately** — Get outside into fresh air
2. **Call 911** — Airway burns are always a medical emergency, even if symptoms seem mild
3. **Position for breathing** — Sit upright or slightly forward (helps open airway)
4. **Don't put anything in mouth** — Don't try to soothe throat with drinks or food
5. **Be ready to perform CPR** — Airways can close suddenly; know CPR
6. **Keep them calm** — Fear makes breathing harder; reassurance helps
7. **Never give pain medication that causes drowsiness** — May suppress breathing reflexes

**Hospital treatment:**
- May require intubation (breathing tube) if swelling worsens
- May receive steroids to reduce swelling
- Antibiotics for infection prevention
- Oxygen therapy
- Close monitoring for airway closure

### Prevention of Airway Burns

{{< drg/tip >}}
The single best prevention for airway burns is escaping smoke quickly. If you're in a building with smoke, get out immediately—don't search for people or belongings. Rescuing items is not worth your lungs.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

**At home:**
- Install smoke detectors that will alert you to fire before heavy smoke
- Develop and practice evacuation plans—everyone should know the fastest exit
- Teach children to crawl under smoke (cooler, cleaner air near floor)
- Have a meeting place outside so you're not tempted to re-enter looking for people

**During a fire:**
- Get out immediately; don't wait or investigate
- Crawl low under smoke (breathable air is 12-18 inches from the floor)
- If smoke is thick, close your eyes and follow walls
- Plug your nose and mouth if possible (reduces heat inhalation)
- Never go back inside for anything

## Carbon Monoxide Poisoning: The Colorless Killer

### What is Carbon Monoxide?

**Carbon monoxide (CO) is a poisonous gas produced by:**
- Incomplete combustion (when fuel doesn't burn completely)
- Fires (especially in enclosed spaces where oxygen is limited)
- Car engines, generators, camp stoves used indoors
- Furnaces and water heaters (usually safe when working properly)
- Appliances venting improperly (blocked chimney, loose vent)

**Why it's dangerous:**
- Colorless and odorless—you can't see, smell, or taste it
- Binds to hemoglobin (the protein that carries oxygen) stronger than oxygen does
- Your red blood cells pick up CO instead of oxygen
- Organs are starved for oxygen while CO is riding in blood
- Can cause permanent brain damage or death even at moderate exposure levels

### How CO Poisoning Occurs in Fires

**In a burning building:**
- Oxygen becomes depleted (fire consumes it)
- Fuel burns incompletely, producing more CO
- CO concentration rises quickly in enclosed spaces
- CO combines with blood faster than oxygen can
- Within minutes, a person can lose consciousness

**Victims often:**
- Don't realize they're being poisoned (no smell, no warning)
- Feel confused and disoriented (thinking is affected first)
- Make poor decisions (try to hide instead of escape)
- Can't escape because they're too confused or weak

### Symptoms and Signs of CO Poisoning

**Early symptoms (mild exposure):**
- Headache (often the first sign)
- Dizziness
- Weakness or fatigue
- Nausea or upset stomach
- Chest pain or shortness of breath
- Confusion or difficulty concentrating

**These symptoms mimic flu!** Many people think they have the flu and don't realize it's CO poisoning.

**Moderate exposure:**
- Headache becomes severe
- Dizziness prevents standing
- Confusion increases
- Unconsciousness may occur
- Heart palpitations or chest pain

**Severe exposure:**
- Loss of consciousness happens quickly
- Seizures possible
- No response to surroundings
- Death can occur within minutes

**Warning:** Symptoms can develop in any order. Some people have only one or two symptoms but are still being poisoned. Confusion is one of the most dangerous symptoms because it prevents escape.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
If you suspect CO poisoning in a building fire, get everyone out immediately. DO NOT go back inside for any reason. Rescue from CO poisoning must be done in fresh air, not inside the contaminated building. Call 911 from outside.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Emergency Response to CO Poisoning

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Responding to Suspected CO Poisoning" >}}
If someone shows signs of CO poisoning in a fire situation:

1. **Get them out of the contaminated area immediately** — Fresh air is the only treatment in an emergency
2. **Call 911** — Describe symptoms clearly (headache, confusion, chest pain, etc.)
3. **Move to fresh air outdoors** — Even if person is unconscious, move them outside
4. **If unconscious, position on side** — Recovery position prevents aspiration
5. **Be prepared to give CPR** — If they stop breathing, CPR may be needed
6. **Never re-enter to rescue** — CO is spreading; rescuers also become victims
7. **Tell rescue workers about CO exposure** — They need to know to transport to facility with hyperbaric oxygen
8. **Don't assume recovery means safety** — CO can cause permanent brain damage even after symptoms improve

**Hospital treatment for CO poisoning:**
- High-flow oxygen (removes CO from blood faster)
- Hyperbaric oxygen chamber (increases oxygen pressure to speed recovery)
- Monitoring for complications
- Tests for organ damage (heart, brain)
- Long-term evaluation for neurological damage

{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

### Prevention of CO Poisoning at Home

**Appliance Safety:**
- Have furnace and water heater inspected annually
- Keep furnace vents and chimney clear (snow, leaves, debris)
- Never use generators, camp stoves, or car engines indoors
- Don't use gas-powered equipment in garages (even with door open)

**Warning Signs of CO Problems:**
- Furnace flame is yellow instead of blue (indicates incomplete combustion)
- Soot or water stains around furnace vents
- Strong smell of exhaust in home
- Rust on water heater or furnace vent pipe
- Back-drafting (smoke coming down chimney)

**Install CO Detectors:**
- Every home should have CO detectors on every level
- Place one near bedrooms (so it wakes you if triggered)
- Test monthly and replace batteries yearly
- Replace detector every 5-7 years
- If detector sounds alarm: get everyone outside and call 911

### The Difference Between Smoke Inhalation and CO Poisoning

**Both can happen in a fire, but they're different:**

| Aspect | Smoke Inhalation | CO Poisoning |
|--------|------------------|--------------|
| **Cause** | Hot gases, particles, chemicals from burning materials | Incomplete combustion of fuel |
| **Noticeable** | Often see smoke, can smell it | Colorless, odorless |
| **Symptom onset** | Often minutes as throat irritates | Can be very fast in high concentration |
| **Visible signs** | Soot, singed nasal hair, difficulty breathing | No visible signs; confusion is key |
| **Treatment** | Fresh air, medical monitoring, airway support | Fresh air, oxygen, hyperbaric oxygen |
| **Long-term damage** | Permanent airway scarring possible | Permanent brain damage possible |

**In a real fire, both happen together,** which is why getting out quickly is so critical. You need to escape before either becomes severe.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The most dangerous moment in a fire is often the first few minutes when smoke and CO levels are rising but the building is still structurally sound. By the time thick smoke is visible, CO may already be at dangerous levels. This is why getting out immediately—not investigating or looking around—saves lives.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Home Fire Escape Plan Connection

What you learned in Requirement 5g (home fire escape plan) applies directly here:
- **Know your exits** before the fire, so you don't waste time searching
- **Establish a meeting place outside** so you're not tempted to re-enter
- **Practice escaping in the dark** to prepare for low-visibility conditions
- **Know that you have seconds** to escape before smoke and CO become dangerous
