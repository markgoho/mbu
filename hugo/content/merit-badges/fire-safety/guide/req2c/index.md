## When to Use an Extinguisher (and When NOT To)

Before learning *how* to use an extinguisher, you need to know *when* it's appropriate to use one. **Using an extinguisher is only appropriate for small, contained fires under specific conditions.**

**Use an extinguisher ONLY if:**
- The fire is small and contained (not spreading)
- You have a clear, unblocked escape route behind you
- You're confident in your ability to use the extinguisher
- The correct type of extinguisher is available for that fire class
- You can keep your face and hands away from the flames
- The fire hasn't produced heavy smoke yet
- You have a working fire alarm nearby or someone nearby to call 911

**Evacuate and call 911 if:**
- The fire is growing or spreading rapidly
- Heavy smoke is present
- You're unsure about your safety
- The fire blocks your exit
- You don't have the right type of extinguisher
- The fire reignites after you've used the extinguisher

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Your safety is more important than saving property. If in doubt, evacuate and call 911. Firefighters are trained, equipped, and insured for fighting fires. You are not. A small moment of hesitation can trap you. Don't be a hero.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## The PASS Technique for Using a Fire Extinguisher

PASS is an acronym that describes the four steps for properly using a fire extinguisher:

### **P — Pull the Pin**

At the top of the extinguisher, there's a safety pin that prevents accidental discharge. Grab the extinguisher firmly and pull the pin straight out. You'll hear a small "click" as the safety is released.

This step removes the locking mechanism—the extinguisher is now armed and ready to discharge.

### **A — Aim at the Base of the Fire**

Point the nozzle or horn at the **base of the flames**, not at the flames themselves. You want to disrupt the fuel, not just extinguish the visible flames.

**Key positioning:**
- Stand 6–10 feet away from the fire (depends on extinguisher type and range)
- Keep the extinguisher upright
- Point downward at the flames' source
- Keep an escape route clear behind you

For a fire in a trash can, aim at the trash. For a fire on a stovetop, aim at the base of the flames on the pan. For a spilled gasoline fire, aim at the puddle.

### **S — Squeeze the Handle (or Lever)**

Squeeze the handle steadily to discharge the agent. Don't jab it—use steady, controlled pressure. This gives you better control over the stream and prolongs discharge time.

The agent will flow out in either a steady stream (foam, water, CO₂) or a cloud (dry chemical powder). Continue squeezing as you move to the next step.

### **S — Sweep Side to Side**

Using a sweeping motion, move the nozzle from side to side across the base of the fire. This spreads the extinguishing agent across all the burning fuel, ensuring complete coverage.

**Sweep pattern:**
- Slowly sweep left to right
- Maintain steady pressure
- Cover the entire burn area
- Stop when flames are out OR when the extinguisher is depleted

## Special Considerations by Extinguisher Type

**ABC Dry Chemical:**
- Aim at the base and sweep
- The dry powder clouds visibility—wear eye protection
- Leave the area immediately after (powder blocks vision and the fire can reignite)

**Foam Extinguisher:**
- Aim at the base and sweep gently
- Foam works by smothering, so slower is better
- Continue spraying until the foam layer is thick

**CO₂ Extinguisher:**
- Short range (3–8 feet)
- Aim at the base and sweep
- The horn becomes extremely cold; use insulated gloves
- The discharge makes a loud hissing sound

**Water Extinguisher:**
- Aim at the base and sweep
- Allow water to soak into burning material
- Full penetration is key
- May need to refill or have backup water source

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Using an Extinguisher: A Practice Scenario" >}}
You're cooking and a small pan of oil catches fire on the stovetop. You have an ABC extinguisher nearby.
- **Step 1**: Turn off the heat immediately.
- **Step 2**: Grab the extinguisher from the cabinet.
- **Step 3**: Pull the pin.
- **Step 4**: Stand 6 feet from the pan, aiming at the base of the flames.
- **Step 5**: Squeeze the handle and sweep from left to right across the fire.
- **Step 6**: Stop when flames are out.
- **Step 7**: Never return to cooking in that pan until it has cooled and been cleaned.
- **Step 8**: Call 911 if the fire reignites or if you feel unsafe at any point.

The whole process takes 10–15 seconds. If it takes longer than that or the fire isn't responding, abandon the extinguisher, evacuate, and call 911.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

## What NOT to Do

- **Don't aim at the flames**: Aim at the source (base) of the fire
- **Don't squeeze intermittently**: Use steady pressure for best results
- **Don't get too close**: Maintain 6–10 feet distance
- **Don't turn your back on the fire**: Keep it in view so you know if it reignites
- **Don't block your own exit**: The fire should be between you and safety
- **Don't wait for the extinguisher to be completely empty**: If the fire isn't controlled after 30 seconds, evacuate
- **Don't use water on electrical, oil, or metal fires**: It will make the situation worse
- **Don't assume the fire is out**: Watch for reignition and be ready to evacuate

## Fire Blankets: An Alternative Tool

A **fire blanket** is a non-flammable blanket made from fiberglass or other fire-resistant material. It's designed to smother small fires, primarily in the kitchen.

### How to Use a Fire Blanket

**Step 1: Remove the blanket from its case** (usually mounted on a wall or stored nearby)

**Step 2: Hold it by the two upper corners** (keep your hands protected by the blanket)

**Step 3: Approach the fire slowly** and carefully drape the blanket over the flames, completely covering the fire

**Step 4: Turn off the heat source** if you can do so safely (without moving the blanket)

**Step 5: Leave the blanket in place** until the fire cools completely (30+ minutes). Don't peek underneath.

**Step 6: Dispose of the blanket** after use (it has served its purpose and may have damage)

### When to Use a Fire Blanket

Fire blankets work best for:
- **Small cooking fires** (pan fires, small stove fires)
- **Grease fires** (they smother without water)
- **Contained fires** (not spreading)
- **Fires where you want to avoid the mess** of chemical extinguishers

Fire blankets do NOT work well for:
- **Large fires** (insufficient coverage)
- **Fires in confined spaces** (hard to deploy safely)
- **Fires above head height** (risk of being burned)
- **Fires that produce heavy smoke** (can't see what you're doing)

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Fire blankets are popular in European kitchens but less common in the U.S. However, they're excellent for grease fires because they smother the fire without spreading the burning oil. If you cook frequently with oil, a fire blanket hanging in your kitchen is a smart safety measure.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

### Fire Blanket vs. Extinguisher

| Situation | Blanket | Extinguisher |
|-----------|---------|--------------|
| Small pan fire | Excellent | Good |
| Large fire | Poor | Better |
| Grease fire | Excellent | Acceptable (wet chem best) |
| Electrical fire | Not ideal | Good (CO₂ best) |
| Multiple fires | Poor (one-time use) | Better (longer discharge) |
| Mess concern | None | Powder everywhere |
| Visibility | Can't see | Can be cloudy |
| Reignition risk | Moderate | Moderate |

## Practical Advice: Equip Your Home

**Kitchen:**
- ABC fire extinguisher (accessible, not hidden)
- Fire blanket hanging on wall
- Know how to turn off the stove/gas
- Keep baking soda nearby (for small grease fires)

**Garage/Workshop:**
- ABC fire extinguisher
- Class B extinguisher if you store gasoline or flammable liquids
- Clear pathways to exits

**Bedroom:**
- No extinguisher needed
- Working smoke alarm
- Clear exit routes

**Best Practice:**
Label your extinguishers clearly. Paint them red or get stickers. Make sure family members know where they are and that they know the PASS technique.
