Develop a lesson plan to teach a fire safety skill or topic, such as lighting a candle or a match or a charcoal fire or a lantern or a camp stove or using flint and steel or setting up a fire lay. Review your lesson plan with your counselor. Teach your skill or topic to one or more Scouts using the EDGE method.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Teaching others reinforces your own learning. When you explain how to light a campfire safely or use a camp stove to someone else, you deepen your understanding of those skills. This requirement uses the EDGE method—a proven teaching approach that helps learners master new skills.

## The EDGE Method

EDGE stands for **Explain, Demonstrate, Guide, Enable**. It's how experienced Scouts, instructors, and leaders teach new skills effectively.

### Explain (E)

**What it means:** Tell your learner what you're teaching and why it matters.

- Give your skill a clear name
- Explain what they'll be able to do when they learn it
- Explain why this skill is important
- Answer questions before you start

**Example:** "Today you're going to learn to light a match safely. Knowing how to light a match without burning yourself is a basic campfire skill that protects you from injury. You'll be able to light fires confidently, which lets you cook outdoors and enjoy camping."

### Demonstrate (D)

**What it means:** Show the learner exactly how it's done.

- Perform the entire skill from start to finish
- Do it at normal speed (not in slow motion)
- Explain what you're doing as you do it
- Point out the tricky parts
- Do it again if asked

**Example:** For lighting a match, you'd show: holding the match firmly, striking at the right angle, letting the flame stabilize, applying it to tinder, and safe extinguishing.

### Guide (G)

**What it means:** Let the learner try while you watch and coach.

- Have them attempt the skill
- Stay close and observe
- Correct mistakes immediately (before they become habits)
- Offer encouragement
- Ask questions: "What comes next?" "Why are you holding it that way?"

**Example:** "Now you try. Hold the match the same way I showed you. Good—now strike it. That's the right angle. Now let it stabilize before you apply it to the tinder."

### Enable (E)

**What it means:** Let them do it independently with minimal help.

- Step back and let them practice
- Only intervene if safety is at risk
- Let them make small mistakes and learn from them
- Celebrate success
- Give them opportunities to teach others

**Example:** "Go ahead and light the next three matches. I'll be right here if you need me, but you've got this."

## Choosing Your Topic

You can teach any fire safety skill or topic from the badge requirements. Popular choices include:

**Easy topics (good for beginners):**
- Lighting a match safely
- Safe match extinguishing
- Using a lighter safely
- Candle safety

**Moderate topics (mid-level skill):**
- Using flint and steel
- Starting a charcoal fire
- Setting up a fire lay
- Using a camp stove

**Advanced topics:**
- Full campsite safety setup
- Fire tetrahedron application
- Wildland fire prevention

**Non-hands-on topics:**
- Fire extinguisher classes
- Smoke alarm testing
- Fire escape planning

## Developing Your Lesson Plan

A lesson plan is a simple document showing what you'll teach, how long it takes, what materials you need, and what success looks like.

### Basic Lesson Plan Format

**Skill Title:** What are you teaching?

**Learning Objective:** After this lesson, the learner will be able to _____.

**Materials Needed:**
- List everything required
- Include safety equipment

**Time Estimate:** How long will this take?

**The Lesson (EDGE Format):**

1. **Explain (2–3 minutes)**
   - What is the skill?
   - Why does it matter?
   - What will learners be able to do?

2. **Demonstrate (3–5 minutes)**
   - Show the complete skill
   - Narrate what you're doing
   - Highlight the important steps
   - Point out common mistakes

3. **Guide (5–10 minutes)**
   - Have learners attempt the skill
   - Provide coaching feedback
   - Correct immediately if safety is at risk
   - Encourage questions

4. **Enable (5–10 minutes)**
   - Let learners practice independently
   - Observe and offer encouragement
   - Celebrate success

**Safety Considerations:**
- What could go wrong?
- How will you prevent it?
- What's your emergency plan?

**Success Criteria:**
- How will you know they've learned?
- Can they do it safely?
- Can they explain why?

## Example Lesson Plan

**Skill Title:** Safe Match Lighting

**Learning Objective:** After this lesson, the learner will be able to safely light a match without burning their fingers.

**Materials Needed:**
- 1 box of safety matches
- Tinder (dry leaves or cardboard)
- Water bucket
- Open area away from flammables

**Time Estimate:** 15 minutes

**Explain (2 minutes):**
"We're learning to light a match safely today. A match is a simple tool that lets you start fires for cooking and warmth when camping. The dangerous part is the flame—it can burn you if you're not careful. By the end of today, you'll be able to light a match confidently, and you'll know how to protect your fingers from the heat."

**Demonstrate (3 minutes):**
Show the full process: Hold match firmly between thumb and index finger, strike at a 45-degree angle along the box, wait for the flame to stabilize (about 2 seconds), apply to tinder, and safely extinguish by blowing out or waving it.

**Guide (7 minutes):**
Have them light their first match while you watch. Coach: "Hold it firmer. That's good. Now strike at an angle like this. Perfect! Wait for the flame to get bigger. Now you can light the tinder. Good work! Try another one."

**Enable (3 minutes):**
"Light three more matches. I'm right here if you need me." Step back and observe. Offer encouragement but let them practice independently.

**Safety Considerations:**
- Fingers can burn if held too close to flame
- Matches can fall and start unwanted fires
- Extinguishing must be complete
- No loose clothing or long sleeves
- Open area only

**Success Criteria:**
- Can light match without burning fingers
- Can safely extinguish the flame
- Can explain the steps to someone else
- Can demonstrate safely multiple times

## Teaching Tips

**Before you teach:**
- Practice the skill yourself multiple times
- Go through your lesson plan aloud
- Gather all materials beforehand
- Scout your teaching location for safety
- Tell your learner what to expect

**During the lesson:**
- Stay calm and confident
- Use clear, simple language
- Watch for confusion and adjust
- Praise effort, not just success
- Keep the pace manageable
- Take breaks if needed

**For safety-focused skills:**
- Never skip safety steps for speed
- Prioritize fire prevention over elegance
- Stop immediately if something feels wrong
- Have a backup plan
- Know your learner's age and abilities

**After the lesson:**
- Ask them what they learned
- Have them teach it back to you
- Give them opportunities to practice more
- Celebrate their success
- Document what happened

## Reviewing with Your Counselor

Your counselor will want to:
- See your written lesson plan
- Understand your teaching approach (EDGE method)
- Discuss your choice of topic
- Review safety considerations
- Answer questions about how you'd adjust if something went wrong

Be ready to explain:
- Why you chose this particular skill
- How the EDGE method helps learners master fire safety
- What safety precautions you'll take
- How you'll know if your teaching was successful

## Teaching Multiple Scouts

Teaching a group is slightly different from teaching one person:

- **Explain:** Address the whole group
- **Demonstrate:** Make sure everyone can see
- **Guide:** Watch for learners who are struggling
- **Enable:** Let each person practice while you rotate between them

Managing group dynamics:
- Keep everyone engaged
- Prevent faster learners from rushing ahead
- Help struggling learners without embarrassing them
- Make sure no one is idle or bored
- Celebrate individual progress

## After You Teach

Reflecting on your teaching helps you improve:

- What went well?
- What was harder than you expected?
- How did learners respond?
- Would you teach it differently next time?
- What did you learn about the skill itself?

Teaching fire safety skills is rewarding because you're helping others develop the confidence and knowledge to stay safe. When your learner successfully lights their first match or confidently starts a campfire, you've given them a skill they'll use for life.
