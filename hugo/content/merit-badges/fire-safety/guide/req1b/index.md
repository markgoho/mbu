## The Fire Tetrahedron Explained

You learned in Req 1a that fire needs three things: fuel, oxygen, and heat. Firefighters have added a fourth element that makes fire both ignite and sustain itself: **the chemical reaction itself**. These four elements together form the **fire tetrahedron**.

Imagine a four-sided pyramid (tetrahedron). Each face represents one essential element:

**Heat** — the ignition source. Without sufficient heat, fuel and oxygen won't react, no matter how much of each is present. Think of heat as the spark that gets the party started.

**Fuel** — any material that can burn. Solids (wood, paper, fabric), liquids (gasoline, oil), and gases (propane, methane) all serve as fuel.

**Oxygen** — the oxidizer that combines with fuel. Oxygen makes up about 21% of the air around you, but fire can occur with lower oxygen percentages.

**Chemical Reaction** — the sustained combustion process itself. Once heat, fuel, and oxygen come together correctly, a chain reaction begins where the heat from combustion sustains the reaction, keeping it going even if the original ignition source is removed.

## Why the Tetrahedron Matters

The fire triangle (heat, fuel, oxygen) was the older model, but the tetrahedron is more accurate because it includes the chemical chain reaction. This matters because removing the reaction itself can stop a fire even when all three physical elements are still present.

{{< drg/checklist title="Fire Tetrahedron Components" subtitle="What firefighters use to fight fires" >}}
- **Heat**: Remove it by cooling with water or foam (this is why water works on wood fires)
- **Fuel**: Remove it by clearing flammable materials away from the fire (this is why you clear brush around wildland fires)
- **Oxygen**: Remove it by smothering with a blanket, soil, or by using a CO₂ extinguisher (this works on oil fires where water would cause the fire to spread)
- **Chemical Reaction**: Interrupt it with dry chemical extinguishers or halons (this works on electrical fires where water would conduct electricity)
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

## The Products of Combustion

When the fire tetrahedron is "complete" and combustion occurs, the process produces:

**Primary Products (Complete Combustion):**
- Carbon dioxide (CO₂)
- Water vapor (H₂O)
- Energy (heat and light)

**Secondary Products (Incomplete Combustion—more common in real fires):**
- Carbon monoxide (CO) — a toxic gas that kills by depriving the body of oxygen
- Smoke — a mixture of tiny soot particles and gases
- Unburned fuel particles — what make smoke visible and toxic
- Other toxic gases — depending on what's burning (chlorine from PVC plastics, cyanide compounds from wool, etc.)

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Not all combustion is complete. Real-world fires are chaotic and often oxygen-limited, producing dangerous gases like carbon monoxide in lethal quantities. Smoke from a fire is toxic, not just hot—it's as dangerous as flames.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Carbon Monoxide: The Silent Killer

Carbon monoxide is produced in nearly every fire. It's colorless, odorless, and tasteless—you have no natural warning system to detect it. Even at low concentrations, it causes:

- Headache and confusion within minutes
- Dizziness and loss of coordination
- Nausea and weakness
- Death at high concentrations

Carbon monoxide reaches your brain before you realize you're in danger, which is why firefighters wear oxygen masks—they can't rely on their own senses in a smoky environment.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Install carbon monoxide detectors in your home near sleeping areas and in any room with a fuel-burning appliance (furnace, water heater, fireplace). Battery-powered ones cost less than $30 and could save your life.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Smoke: More Dangerous Than Flames

Smoke is produced when combustion is incomplete. It's a visible mixture of:
- Soot particles (burned and partially burned fuel)
- Water vapor and other gases
- Toxic chemicals from burning materials
- Carbon monoxide

**Smoke kills in three ways:**
1. **Asphyxiation** — it displaces breathable air and fills your lungs with inert gases
2. **Toxicity** — the chemical compounds are poisonous
3. **Heat** — hot smoke burns your lungs and airways

In real fire deaths, autopsy studies show that most victims inhaled smoke and died from its effects before flames ever reached them. Smoke can incapacitate a person in seconds, long before they even see flames.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
In many house fires, smoke and toxic gases become life-threatening within 1-2 minutes. This is why fire safety emphasizes getting out fast rather than trying to fight the fire—you don't have time to be a hero.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Fighting Different Types of Fires

Understanding the fire tetrahedron is how firefighters choose their tactics:

**Wood/Paper Fires (Class A)** — Remove heat by cooling with water. Water absorbs heat and cools the fuel below its ignition temperature.

**Liquid Fires (Class B)** — Remove oxygen by smothering with foam or dry chemical. Water doesn't work because oil floats on water and continues burning on top.

**Electrical Fires (Class C)** — Use dry chemical or CO₂ to interrupt the chemical reaction. Never use water because electricity conducts through water, creating a shock hazard.

**Metal Fires (Class D)** — Special dry powder extinguishers that smother and cool. Ordinary extinguishers can make metal fires worse.

You'll learn much more about fire classes and extinguishers in Requirement 2, but now you understand *why* different fires need different approaches.
