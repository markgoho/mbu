{{< drg/requirement number="5i" >}}
Explain how you would report a fire to have the fire department respond.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

After you've escaped, reporting the fire accurately and completely is your next critical action. Fire departments respond rapidly but need correct information to get to the right location with the right equipment. This requirement teaches you how to call 911 and what information to provide.

## The 911 System

### How 911 Works

When you dial 911:

1. **Call connects to dispatchers** — Public Safety Answering Point (PSAP)
2. **Computer captures your phone location** — Helps dispatchers find you
3. **Dispatcher answers and directs your call** — To fire, police, or EMS
4. **Information is logged** — For emergency response and records
5. **Fire department is dispatched** — Trucks roll while dispatcher is on phone
6. **Responders are given details** — Address, situation, hazards
7. **Dispatcher stays on line with you** — Provides instructions, updates information

**Critical point:** Dispatchers are trained to keep you on line and get details. They may ask questions while firefighters are rolling.

## Information You Need to Provide

When you call 911, dispatchers need specific information:

### Location (Most Critical)

**You must clearly state:**
- **Full address** — Street number, street name, city
  - Example: "2547 Maple Drive, Anytown, Arizona 85123"
  - Not just "the house on the corner"
  - Include apartment/unit number if applicable
- **Nearest cross street** (if you don't know full address)
  - "On Third Avenue between Oak and Pine Streets"
- **Building type** — House, apartment, office building, school
- **Landmarks** — "Next to the blue water tower"

**Why this is critical:**
- Fire trucks need exact location immediately
- Seconds count; dispatchers can't search
- Multiple people may be calling same fire; locations must match
- Wrong address = wasted response time

### Nature of Fire/Hazard

**Clearly state:**
- "There is a structure fire"
- "House is on fire"
- "Apartment fire"
- "Car fire"
- "Grass fire/wildfire"

**Include details if obvious:**
- "Smoke coming from kitchen area"
- "Flames visible from back of house"
- "Second floor is burning"
- "Garage fire"

### Immediate Hazards

**Tell dispatcher if:**
- **People are inside** — "There are people upstairs" (dispatcher may dispatch rescue)
- **Explosions possible** — "There's a gas smell" (hazmat response)
- **Hazardous materials** — "There are propane tanks" (special precautions)
- **Downed power lines** — "Power line is down across the street"
- **People trapped** — "Someone is at the upstairs window" (rescue priority)

### Your Contact Information

**Dispatcher needs:**
- **Your name**
- **Phone number** (the number you're calling from)
- **Your location** — Are you at fire scene, blocks away, at meeting point?

**Why they ask:**
- To confirm information if connection drops
- To call back if more information is needed
- To ensure you're in safe location

## How to Call 911 (Scenario Practice)

### Before You Need It: Know This Information

**About your home/location:**
- Full street address (memorize it)
- Nearest cross streets
- Phone number (so dispatcher can identify you)
- Directions from main road

**Practice scenario:** Pretend to call 911 and state your address 5 times. You should be able to say it clearly without thinking.

### During an Actual Fire: The Call

**Step-by-step:**

1. **Dial 911** — From safe location (outside home, at meeting point, neighbor's house)
   - Don't dial from near fire
   - If outside area without cell service, use landline or ask for help

2. **Stay calm and speak clearly** — Dispatcher understands panicked people; speak slowly and distinctly

3. **Wait for dispatcher to ask questions** — Don't hang up; let dispatcher get information

4. **Answer the opening question** — Dispatcher will ask:
   - **"What is the emergency?"** — Answer: "There's a fire at [address]"
   - **"What's your address?"** — Give complete address, repeat if needed
   - **"What's on fire?"** — House, apartment, car, etc.
   - **"Are there people inside?"** — Yes/no, how many
   - **"Are you in a safe location?"** — Outside, at safe distance

5. **Answer follow-up questions** — Dispatcher may ask:
   - How big is the fire?
   - Where is fire located?
   - Can you see flames?
   - Is there an explosion risk?
   - What's your name and location?

6. **Keep phone ready for dispatcher** — Don't hang up until told to

7. **Leave line open** — Dispatcher may ask you to stay on phone while fire trucks respond

8. **Don't assume they know** — Tell dispatcher details:
   - "Second floor window"
   - "Heavy smoke"
   - "Flames shooting out"
   - "No visible fire but lots of smoke"

### Example 911 Call

**Dispatcher:** "911, what's your emergency?"

**You:** "There's a fire at my house."

**Dispatcher:** "What's your address?"

**You:** "2547 Maple Drive, Anytown, Arizona 85123."

**Dispatcher:** "Is anyone inside the house?"

**You:** "No, we all got out."

**Dispatcher:** "Where are you right now?"

**You:** "We're in the front yard at the end of the driveway."

**Dispatcher:** "Is the fire inside or outside the house?"

**You:** "Inside. We can see flames in the kitchen window."

**Dispatcher:** "Fire trucks are on the way. Stay on the line with me."

**You:** "Okay."

(Dispatcher will continue to ask questions and keep you calm while trucks respond)

## Special Calling Situations

### Calling from Cell Phone

**Challenges:**
- Dispatcher may not get exact location (GPS helps but not perfect)
- Battery might die
- Reception might be poor

**What to do:**
- Stay on line; let dispatcher trace location
- State address clearly if reception is poor
- Have numbers in phone's favorites (so you can call even if phone locked/disabled)

### Calling from Neighbor's Phone

**Include:**
- Your address (not neighbor's)
- That you're calling from neighbor's phone (different number)
- Neighbor's address if different from fire location

**Example:** "Fire at 2547 Maple Drive. I'm calling from my neighbor's phone at 2551 Maple Drive."

### If You Can't Talk

**If you're injured, panicked, or can't speak:**
- Let dispatcher know (nod your head into phone)
- Dispatcher will get information from your phone location
- Important: DON'T HANG UP (connection tells dispatcher you're at that location)

### No Phone Available

**If you have no phone:**
- Flag down passing vehicle
- Run to neighbor's house
- Use public phone if available
- Use building phone if in apartment/workplace

**Get information to someone who can call:**
- Your address
- That there's a fire
- Any people at risk

## What Happens After You Call

### Dispatcher's Actions (While You're on Phone)

- Logs your call with timestamp
- Dispatches closest available fire trucks
- Gets additional information from you
- Alerts paramedics if injuries reported
- Alerts police if hazards reported
- Provides instructions (e.g., "evacuate neighbors")

### Fire Truck Response

**Typical response times:**
- Urban areas: 3-5 minutes
- Suburban areas: 5-10 minutes
- Rural areas: 15-30+ minutes

**Why variations:**
- Distance to nearest fire station
- Road conditions
- Number of available trucks

**Your job:** Just stay safe and let them do theirs. They'll find you.

## Special Situations

### If Someone is Trapped

**If you know someone is inside:**
- Tell dispatcher immediately: "My brother is upstairs in the back bedroom"
- Provide description: "He's a teenager, red shirt"
- Description helps rescuers search

**Dispatcher will:**
- Adjust response (priority becomes rescue)
- Alert firefighters to the situation
- Brief arriving trucks before they arrive

### If Fire is Actively Spreading

**Tell dispatcher if:**
- Fire spreading to neighbor's house
- Wind is blowing fire toward other structures
- Trees are burning near house
- Ground fire approaching other buildings

**Dispatcher will:**
- Send additional trucks
- Alert nearby residents
- Prepare for mass evacuation if needed

### If There's Explosion Risk

**Tell dispatcher if:**
- You smell gas
- You see propane tanks
- You know chemicals are stored there
- You hear explosions

**Response will be different:**
- More trucks will stage at safe distance
- Hazmat team may respond
- Area may be evacuated wider

## What NOT to Do

**Don't:**
- **Hang up** — Stay on line until dispatcher says it's okay
- **Mislead dispatcher** — Be accurate, not dramatic
- **Assume they know** — Tell them everything; they have no other source
- **Get angry at questions** — Dispatcher needs details to respond properly
- **Use 911 for non-emergencies** — Fire departments are funded by your taxes; use non-emergency lines for other questions
- **Provide false information** — False reports waste critical resources
- **Stay at fire scene** — Move to safe location

## Practice for This Requirement

### With Your Family

1. **Everyone learns home address** — Can they say it from memory?
2. **Practice dialing** (don't actually call)
   - What would you say?
   - How would you describe the fire?
   - Do you know nearest cross streets?
3. **Role play**
   - One person is dispatcher
   - Others call in with fire report
   - Practice clear communication

## The Bottom Line

Calling 911 is your responsibility once you've escaped. Fire departments respond immediately to calls, but they need:

1. **Correct location** — So they go to the right place
2. **Nature of emergency** — So they bring right equipment
3. **Hazard information** — So they respond safely
4. **Your cooperation** — Staying on line, answering questions

**By calling correctly, you're giving firefighters the information they need to save lives.**

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Approximately 1 in 5 fire 911 calls contain incorrect address information. This delays response and can cost lives. Knowing and being able to clearly state your address is one of the most important fire safety skills.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

{{< drg/tip >}}
Consider writing your address on a piece of paper and posting it somewhere visible (refrigerator, bedroom wall). Young children should be able to provide this information. Teach them early.
{{< /drg/tip >}}
