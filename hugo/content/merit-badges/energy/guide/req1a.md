{{< drg/requirement number="1a" >}}
With your parent or guardian's permission, use the internet to find a blog, podcast, website, or an article on the use or conservation of energy. Discuss with your counselor what details in the article were interesting to you, the questions it raises, and what ideas it addresses that you do not understand.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A solar farm in Texas just broke cost records. A new battery design might make electric planes possible. A family in Ohio cut their electric bill by 60% with simple changes. Energy stories are everywhere — and this requirement asks you to find one that grabs your attention and dig into it.

### How to Choose a Good Source

Not all energy articles are created equal. Some are clickbait, some are advertisements disguised as news, and some are solid reporting. Here is what to look for:

**Reliable sources include:**
- Government agencies (U.S. Energy Information Administration, Department of Energy, EPA)
- University research pages and science communication sites
- Major news outlets with science sections (NPR, BBC, Scientific American, National Geographic)
- Established energy organizations (National Renewable Energy Laboratory, International Energy Agency)
- Well-known podcasts focused on energy or climate (The Energy Gang, Volts, How to Save a Planet)

**Be cautious of:**
- Sites that are clearly selling a product or service
- Articles with no author listed and no sources cited
- Social media posts without links to original reporting
- Sources that use extreme language ("miracle breakthrough" or "total disaster")

{{< drg/tip >}}
When choosing your article, pick something that genuinely interests you — not just the first result in a search engine. Scan a few different sources first. You will get more out of the requirement if you are curious about the topic rather than just checking a box.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Reading Like a Researcher

Once you have your article, do not just skim it. Read it carefully and take notes on three things:

1. **Details that interest you** — What facts, numbers, or ideas stood out? Why did they catch your attention?
2. **Questions it raises** — What did the article make you wonder about? What would you want to know more about?
3. **Ideas you do not understand** — Be honest about what confused you. Technical terms, statistics, or references to processes you have not learned yet are all fair game.

{{< drg/checklist title="Article Analysis Prep" subtitle="Notes to prepare for your counselor discussion" >}}
- Write down the title, author, and URL of your source.
- Summarize the main point of the article in 2-3 sentences.
- List at least three specific details that interested you.
- Write down at least two questions the article raised.
- Identify any terms, concepts, or claims you did not fully understand.
- Note whether the article focuses on energy use, conservation, or both.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA) publishes a free "Energy Explained" section on its website that covers everything from how oil is refined to how a nuclear reactor works. It is written for a general audience and is one of the most trusted energy information sources in the world.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

### Why This Requirement Comes First

You might wonder why the badge starts with finding an article rather than learning energy basics. There is a good reason: this requirement creates a "before and after" snapshot of your knowledge. Right now, you are reading about energy with a beginner's perspective. By the time you finish the rest of the badge and come back for [Req 1b](/merit-badges/energy/guide/req1b/), you will understand things in the article that puzzled you the first time around.

Save your notes — you will need them later.
