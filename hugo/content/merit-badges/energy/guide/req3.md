This requirement asks you to pick one energy system and analyze it completely. You will need to cover four aspects:

- **Req 3a** — Identify the parts of the system affected by the energy movement.
- **Req 3b** — Name the system's primary source of energy.
- **Req 3c** — Identify the useful outcomes of the system.
- **Req 3d** — Identify the energy losses of the system.

### What Is Energy Efficiency?

Energy efficiency is the ratio of useful energy output to total energy input. No energy system is 100% efficient — some energy always escapes as waste heat, sound, vibration, or light. The question is always: how much of the energy you put in actually does the job you want?

Here is a simple formula:

**Efficiency = (Useful Energy Output ÷ Total Energy Input) × 100%**

A device that converts 30 out of every 100 units of energy into useful work is 30% efficient. The other 70 units go somewhere — usually they become heat that dissipates into the surroundings.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The average gasoline car engine is only about 20-25% efficient. That means roughly three-quarters of the energy in every gallon of gas becomes waste heat — blown out the exhaust pipe and through the radiator. Electric motors, by contrast, are typically 85-95% efficient, which is one reason electric vehicles use so much less total energy per mile.

### Example System: A Gas-Powered Car

This is an excellent example because the energy losses are dramatic and easy to identify.

#### 3a — Parts of the System

{{< drg/requirement number="3a" >}}
Identify the parts of the system that are affected by the energy movement.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

- **Fuel tank** — stores gasoline (chemical energy)
- **Engine** — burns fuel, converts chemical energy to kinetic energy
- **Transmission** — transfers mechanical energy to the wheels
- **Wheels and tires** — contact the road, converting rotational energy to forward motion
- **Exhaust system** — routes hot combustion gases away from the engine
- **Cooling system** (radiator, coolant) — removes excess heat from the engine
- **Alternator** — converts some mechanical energy to electricity for the battery and electronics
- **Brakes** — convert kinetic energy to thermal energy (friction) when stopping

#### 3b — Primary Energy Source

{{< drg/requirement number="3b" >}}
Name the system's primary source of energy.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

The primary source is **chemical energy stored in gasoline**. Gasoline is a refined petroleum product containing hydrocarbons. When these hydrocarbons combust (burn) inside the engine's cylinders, the chemical bonds break and release energy as heat and expanding gas.

#### 3c — Useful Outcomes

{{< drg/requirement number="3c" >}}
Identify the useful outcomes of the system.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

- **Forward motion** — the primary purpose. Kinetic energy moves the vehicle and its passengers.
- **Electrical power** — the alternator generates electricity to run headlights, radio, power windows, and charge the battery.
- **Cabin heat** — in cold weather, waste engine heat is redirected into the passenger compartment.

#### 3d — Energy Losses

{{< drg/requirement number="3d" >}}
Identify the energy losses of the system.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

- **Exhaust heat** — About 30-35% of the fuel's energy exits as hot exhaust gas.
- **Engine cooling** — Another 25-30% is removed by the cooling system to prevent the engine from overheating.
- **Friction** — Internal engine friction, tire rolling resistance, and brake friction all waste energy as heat.
- **Air resistance (drag)** — At highway speeds, a significant amount of energy is used pushing air out of the way.
- **Sound** — Engine noise and road noise represent small but real energy losses.
- **Accessories** — Air conditioning, power steering pump, and other systems consume some mechanical energy.

### Other Systems You Could Analyze

You are not limited to a car. Here are other systems that work well for this requirement:

| System | Primary Source | Useful Output | Key Losses |
|--------|---------------|---------------|------------|
| Natural gas furnace | Chemical (gas) | Heat for home | Exhaust gases, duct leaks |
| Coal power plant | Chemical (coal) | Electricity | Waste heat, transmission losses |
| Human body (riding a bike) | Chemical (food) | Motion | Metabolic heat, friction |
| Hydroelectric dam | Gravitational potential | Electricity | Friction, turbulence, heat |
| Campfire for cooking | Chemical (wood) | Heat to cook food | Radiant heat, smoke, light |

{{< drg/tip >}}
Pick a system you can actually observe or interact with. If you choose a campfire, you can feel the waste heat escaping in all directions and see the light that is not helping cook your food. That firsthand experience makes your explanation to your counselor much more convincing than describing something you only read about.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### The First Law: Energy Is Never Destroyed

An important principle underlies this entire requirement: the **First Law of Thermodynamics** says energy cannot be created or destroyed — it can only change forms. When we say energy is "lost" in a system, we do not mean it vanishes. It just converts into a form (usually low-grade heat) that is no longer useful for the task at hand. Every joule that enters a system leaves the system — the question is whether it left as useful work or as waste.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Explaining Efficiency to Your Counselor" >}}
When you discuss your system, try this approach:
- **Start with the source.** Name the primary energy input.
- **Trace the path.** Walk through each component and what it does to the energy.
- **Identify the useful output.** What is the system's job?
- **Quantify the losses.** Where does the "wasted" energy go? Use approximate percentages if you can.
- **Suggest improvements.** How could the system be made more efficient?
