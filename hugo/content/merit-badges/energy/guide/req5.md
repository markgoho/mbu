This requirement has two sub-parts:

- **Req 5a** — Explain how your suggested changes would lower costs, reduce pollution, or improve your community.
- **Req 5b** — Explain what changes to routines, habits, or convenience are necessary, and why people might resist them.

In [Req 4](/merit-badges/energy/guide/req4a/), you investigated energy in your own home. Now you are looking at a larger scale — your school, your community center, your town. Energy waste at this level is often bigger and more visible than at home, but it is also harder to fix because it involves convincing groups of people to change.

### Finding Energy Waste: Where to Look

Walk through your school or community with fresh eyes. Here are categories of waste to watch for:

**Lighting waste**
- Lights left on in empty classrooms, hallways, or bathrooms
- Old fluorescent fixtures that could be replaced with LEDs
- Exterior lights running during daylight hours
- Parking lot lights that stay on long after business hours

**Heating and cooling waste**
- Windows or doors propped open while heating or air conditioning runs
- Thermostats set too high in winter or too low in summer
- Gyms or auditoriums heated or cooled when not in use
- Poor insulation around old windows and doors (you can sometimes feel drafts)

**Transportation waste**
- School buses idling for long periods during pickup and drop-off
- No bike racks or walking routes encouraging alternatives to car rides
- Car pickup lines where dozens of vehicles idle for 20-30 minutes

**Equipment and electronics waste**
- Computers, monitors, and printers left on overnight or over weekends
- Vending machines and display cases running 24/7 with old, inefficient compressors
- Old appliances in cafeterias that use two or three times the energy of modern models

**Water heating waste**
- Hot water running unnecessarily in restrooms and kitchens
- Old, uninsulated water heaters and pipes

### The Idea of Trade-offs

A **trade-off** is what you give up in exchange for what you gain. Every energy decision involves trade-offs, and understanding them is essential to making good choices.

For example, replacing all the lights in a school with LEDs would save significant electricity over time — but it requires a large upfront investment. The trade-off is spending money now to save money (and energy) later. Similarly, turning off the heat in the gym when it is empty saves energy, but if there is a surprise assembly, the room will be cold.

Here are more trade-offs to consider:

| Change | Benefit | Trade-off |
|--------|---------|-----------|
| LED lighting upgrade | 60-80% less electricity for lighting | Upfront purchase and installation cost |
| Anti-idling policy for buses | Less fuel waste and pollution | Buses may be uncomfortable for drivers waiting |
| Motion sensor lights | Lights off automatically in empty rooms | Sensors cost money; occasional false offs annoy occupants |
| Lower winter thermostat | Lower heating bills | Some people will feel cold and complain |
| Turning off computers overnight | Saves electricity | Computers take a few minutes to start up each morning |
| Solar panels on roof | Free electricity from sunlight | High installation cost; years to pay back |

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
American school districts collectively spend about $8 billion per year on energy — making it the second-highest operational expense after staff salaries. The Department of Energy estimates that 25-30% of that energy is wasted, meaning schools could potentially redirect $2 billion toward education just by improving efficiency.

## Req 5a — Benefits of Your Changes

{{< drg/requirement number="5a" >}}
Explain how the changes you suggest would lower costs, reduce pollution, or otherwise improve your community.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

For each of your five examples, explain the concrete benefits. Be as specific as you can:

**Lower costs** — Calculate savings if possible. "If our school replaces 200 fluorescent tubes with LEDs, and each LED uses 18 watts instead of 32 watts, the school saves 14 watts per fixture × 200 fixtures × 10 hours per day × 180 school days = 504,000 watt-hours, or 504 kWh per year."

**Reduce pollution** — Every kilowatt-hour of electricity from fossil fuel plants produces about 0.9 pounds of CO2. Less energy used means fewer greenhouse gas emissions.

**Community improvement** — Less money spent on energy can fund other priorities. Cleaner air improves health. Energy-efficient buildings are more comfortable.

## Req 5b — Habits, Routines, and Resistance

{{< drg/requirement number="5b" >}}
Explain what changes to routines, habits, or convenience are necessary to reduce energy waste. Tell why people might resist the changes you suggest.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This is the human side of energy conservation — and it is often harder than the technical side. People resist change for real reasons:

**Comfort** — Nobody wants to be cold, hot, or inconvenienced. Asking people to accept a room that is 68°F instead of 72°F feels like a sacrifice, even though the energy savings are significant.

**Habit** — Leaving lights on, letting computers sleep instead of shutting down, and idling in the car are deeply ingrained habits. Changing habits requires constant reminders until the new behavior becomes automatic.

**Cost** — Even when energy-saving upgrades pay for themselves over time, the upfront cost can be a barrier, especially for schools and organizations with tight budgets.

**Skepticism** — Some people do not believe their individual actions matter. "What difference does it make if I turn off one light?" The answer is that individual actions, multiplied across thousands of people, create real impact — but convincing skeptics requires evidence, not lectures.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Making Your Case" >}}
When you present your ideas to your counselor, try framing them the way a real energy consultant would:
- **Identify the problem.** Describe the specific waste you observed.
- **Quantify it.** Use numbers where possible — even rough estimates help.
- **Propose a solution.** Be specific about what you would change.
- **Acknowledge the trade-off.** What does it cost in money, convenience, or effort?
- **Address resistance.** Why might people push back, and how would you respond?
