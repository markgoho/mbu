Flip a light switch and a room goes from dark to bright. Charge your phone and it runs all day. Drive to a campsite and you burn gasoline to get there. Every single thing you do — from waking up to falling asleep — depends on energy. But where does all that energy actually come from, how does it change forms, and why does it matter how we use it? The Energy merit badge answers those questions and gives you the knowledge to make smarter choices about one of the most important resources on the planet.

Energy is not just a science topic — it is the foundation of modern life. Understanding how energy works, where it comes from, and how we can use it more wisely puts you in a position to make a real difference, whether that means lowering your family's electric bill or understanding the global debates about climate, power grids, and new technology.

## Then and Now

### Then

For most of human history, energy came from muscles — your own and those of animals. Wood fires provided heat and light. Waterwheels and windmills ground grain and pumped water. In the 1700s, the steam engine transformed everything. Burning coal to boil water created mechanical power that could run factories, trains, and ships. The Industrial Revolution was really an energy revolution. By the 1800s, petroleum (oil) and natural gas joined coal as major fuel sources, and Thomas Edison's electric grid brought power directly into homes and businesses.

### Now

Today the world uses roughly 580 exajoules of energy per year — a number so large it is almost meaningless without context. Picture every person on Earth running about 20 old-fashioned 100-watt light bulbs, 24 hours a day, 365 days a year. That is the scale of global energy consumption. The mix of sources is shifting fast: solar and wind power have gone from expensive experiments to the cheapest new electricity in most of the world. Battery technology is making electric vehicles practical. Nuclear energy is being reconsidered as a low-carbon option. And energy efficiency — getting more useful work from less fuel — is being called the "first fuel" because every unit of energy you do not waste is one you never need to produce.

## Kinds of Energy

Energy comes in many forms, and understanding the major categories will help you see connections throughout this badge. Here are the forms you will encounter most often.

### Kinetic Energy

Kinetic energy is the energy of motion. A rolling bowling ball, a flowing river, and the wind all have kinetic energy. The faster something moves or the more massive it is, the more kinetic energy it has. When you pedal a bike, your legs convert chemical energy from food into kinetic energy.

### Potential Energy

Potential energy is stored energy waiting to be released. A stretched rubber band, a rock at the top of a hill, and the water behind a dam all have potential energy. Gravitational potential energy depends on height — the higher you lift something, the more energy it stores.

### Chemical Energy

Chemical energy is stored in the bonds between atoms. Food, gasoline, natural gas, wood, and batteries all contain chemical energy. When those bonds break during combustion or digestion, the stored energy is released as heat, light, or motion.

### Thermal Energy (Heat)

Thermal energy is the energy of vibrating atoms and molecules. Everything has thermal energy — even ice. The hotter something is, the faster its particles move and the more thermal energy it contains. Heat always flows from warmer objects to cooler ones.

### Electrical Energy

Electrical energy is the flow of charged particles (electrons) through a conductor. It is not really a "source" of energy — it is a carrier. Power plants convert other forms of energy (chemical, nuclear, kinetic) into electricity because electricity is incredibly convenient to transport and use.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Lightning bolts carry about 1 billion joules of energy each — but that burst lasts only a fraction of a second. If you could capture a single lightning bolt's energy perfectly (you cannot), it would power an average U.S. home for less than a day.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

### Nuclear Energy

Nuclear energy comes from the nucleus of an atom. In nuclear fission, heavy atoms like uranium are split apart, releasing enormous amounts of energy. In nuclear fusion, light atoms like hydrogen are forced together — this is the process that powers the Sun. A single kilogram of uranium fuel contains as much energy as about 14,000 kilograms of coal.

### Radiant Energy (Light)

Radiant energy travels as electromagnetic waves — visible light, infrared, ultraviolet, radio waves, and more. The Sun is Earth's primary source of radiant energy. Solar panels capture radiant energy and convert it into electricity.

### Renewable vs. Nonrenewable

Energy sources also fall into two critical categories:

- **Renewable** sources replenish naturally on a human timescale — solar, wind, hydropower, geothermal, and biomass.
- **Nonrenewable** sources exist in fixed quantities and take millions of years to form — coal, oil, natural gas, and uranium.

Understanding this distinction is central to almost every debate about energy policy, and you will explore it throughout this badge.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Energy in all its forms demands respect. Electricity can cause fatal shocks. Fuels can burn or explode. Even solar panels and batteries have safety considerations. Throughout this guide, we will highlight specific safety concerns whenever they arise. Never experiment with electricity, fuels, or chemical reactions without adult supervision and proper safety equipment.
