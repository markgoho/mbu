## Req 2a — Device Energy Conversions

{{< drg/requirement number="2a" >}}
Explain how THREE of the following devices use energy, and explain their energy conversions: toaster, greenhouse, lightbulb, bow drill, cell phone, nuclear reactor, sauna, or electric vehicles.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Every device you use takes energy in one form and converts it into another. That conversion is the entire point of the device. Understanding what happens at each step is the key to this requirement. Choose three devices from the list and be ready to trace the energy through each one.

### Toaster

A toaster converts **electrical energy → thermal energy (heat) → radiant energy (infrared)**. When you push the lever down, electrical current flows through thin nichrome wire elements. The wire resists the current, and that resistance generates heat — enough to glow red-hot. The glowing elements emit infrared radiation that toasts your bread. Some energy is also lost as heat that warms the toaster's housing rather than your bread.

### Greenhouse

A greenhouse converts **radiant energy (sunlight) → thermal energy (heat)**. Sunlight passes through the glass panels. Inside, the light is absorbed by soil, plants, and surfaces, which warm up and emit infrared radiation. But here is the key: glass lets visible light in but blocks most infrared from escaping. The trapped infrared radiation keeps the interior warm — sometimes 20–30 degrees warmer than the outside air. This is the same principle behind the "greenhouse effect" in Earth's atmosphere.

### Lightbulb

The conversion depends on the type. An incandescent bulb converts **electrical energy → thermal energy → radiant energy (light and heat)**. Current heats a tungsten filament to over 4,000°F until it glows white-hot. About 90% of the energy becomes heat, with only 10% becoming visible light — extremely inefficient. An LED bulb converts **electrical energy → radiant energy (light)** much more directly, with far less wasted heat. LEDs are roughly 90% efficient at producing light.

### Bow Drill

A bow drill converts **chemical energy (muscles) → kinetic energy (motion) → thermal energy (heat)**. Your muscles, powered by food energy, move the bow back and forth. The spinning spindle creates friction against the fireboard, generating heat. When the temperature reaches the wood's ignition point (around 800°F), an ember forms. This is one of the oldest energy conversion devices in human history.

### Cell Phone

A cell phone involves multiple conversions. During charging: **electrical energy → chemical energy** (stored in the lithium-ion battery). During use: **chemical energy → electrical energy → radiant energy (screen light) + sound energy (speaker) + thermal energy (heat) + electromagnetic energy (radio signals)**. Your phone is actually a remarkably complex energy conversion device that handles dozens of conversions every second.

### Nuclear Reactor

A nuclear reactor converts **nuclear energy → thermal energy → kinetic energy → electrical energy**. Uranium atoms undergo fission (splitting), releasing enormous heat. That heat boils water into steam. The high-pressure steam spins a turbine (kinetic energy). The spinning turbine drives a generator that produces electricity. The chain of conversions is long, but the energy density of uranium is extraordinary — a fuel pellet the size of your fingertip contains as much energy as a ton of coal.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
A single uranium fuel pellet, about the size of a pencil eraser, contains as much energy as 17,000 cubic feet of natural gas, 1,780 pounds of coal, or 149 gallons of oil. That is why nuclear power plants can run for 18–24 months before needing to refuel.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

### Sauna

A traditional sauna converts **chemical energy (wood) → thermal energy (heat) → thermal energy (air and stones)**. Burning wood releases heat that warms special sauna stones. Water poured on the stones creates steam, increasing the humidity and the sensation of heat. An electric sauna skips the chemical conversion: **electrical energy → thermal energy** through resistance heating elements.

### Electric Vehicles

An EV converts **electrical energy → chemical energy (charging the battery) → electrical energy → kinetic energy (motion)**. During charging, electricity from the grid is stored as chemical energy in the battery pack. When you drive, the battery releases that chemical energy as electricity, which powers electric motors that spin the wheels. EVs also use **regenerative braking**, which converts **kinetic energy → electrical energy** when you slow down, recharging the battery.

## Req 2b — Build an Energy Conversion System

{{< drg/requirement number="2b" >}}
Construct a system that makes at least two energy conversions and explain this to your counselor.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This is your chance to build something that demonstrates energy conversion in action. Your system must perform at least two conversions — meaning energy changes form at least twice.

### Project Ideas

Here are some systems that work well and are buildable with common materials:

**Rubber band car** — Chemical energy (stretched rubber band stores elastic potential energy) → kinetic energy (car rolls) → thermal energy (friction slows it down). Materials: cardboard, rubber bands, wooden dowels, bottle caps for wheels.

**Solar-powered fan** — Radiant energy (sunlight) → electrical energy (solar cell) → kinetic energy (motor spins fan blade). Materials: small solar panel, hobby motor, fan blade or propeller.

**Hand-crank flashlight** — Kinetic energy (hand motion) → electrical energy (generator) → radiant energy (LED light). Some hand-crank flashlights also store energy in a capacitor, adding another conversion.

**Water wheel generator** — Potential energy (water held above the wheel) → kinetic energy (falling water spins wheel) → electrical energy (if connected to a small generator) or → kinetic energy (if connected to a simple mechanism).

{{< drg/tip >}}
Keep your build simple. A rubber band car made from a cardboard box, two pencils as axles, and a rubber band stretched between them is a legitimate two-conversion system. You do not need to build something fancy — you need to explain clearly what energy conversions are happening.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

{{< drg/checklist title="Build Checklist" subtitle="Make sure your system demonstrates these elements" >}}
- Identify the starting form of energy in your system.
- Identify at least two points where energy changes form.
- Be able to trace the energy path from start to finish.
- Note where energy is "lost" (usually as heat from friction or resistance).
- Be ready to explain why each conversion happens (what causes it).
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
If your project involves any wiring, soldering, or sharp tools, always work with adult supervision. Never work with household electrical outlets or high-voltage sources. Stick to low-voltage components like small solar cells, hobby motors, and battery packs rated for educational use.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}
