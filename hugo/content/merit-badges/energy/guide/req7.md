{{< drg/requirement number="7" >}}
Tell what is being done to make FIVE of the following energy systems produce more usable energy. In your explanation, describe the technology, cost, environmental impacts, and safety concerns.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This is one of the most research-intensive requirements in the badge. You need to choose five of nine energy systems and, for each one, explain the technology, what is being done to improve it, its costs, environmental impacts, and safety concerns. Read through all nine options below, then pick the five that interest you most.

**Remember: choose FIVE of the nine systems listed.**

### A. Biomass Digesters or Waste-to-Energy Plants

{{< drg/requirement number="7a" >}}
Biomass digesters or waste-to-energy plants
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

**How it works:** Biomass digesters (also called anaerobic digesters) use bacteria to break down organic waste — manure, food scraps, crop residue — in an oxygen-free tank. The bacteria produce biogas, which is mostly methane and can be burned to generate electricity or heat. Waste-to-energy plants burn municipal solid waste (trash) at very high temperatures to produce steam that drives a turbine.

**What is being improved:** Modern digesters are being scaled up on farms and at wastewater treatment plants. Co-digestion, where multiple waste streams are mixed, increases gas output. Waste-to-energy plants are getting better at capturing pollutants before they enter the atmosphere, using advanced scrubbers and filters.

**Cost:** Moderate. Digesters on dairy farms can pay for themselves in 5-10 years through energy savings and revenue from selling excess electricity. Waste-to-energy plants are expensive to build but reduce landfill costs.

**Environmental impact:** Digesters reduce methane emissions from manure and landfills — a major win, since methane is 80 times more potent than CO2 as a greenhouse gas over 20 years. Waste-to-energy reduces landfill volume by up to 90%. However, burning waste still produces some air pollution and ash that must be disposed of safely.

**Safety concerns:** Methane is flammable and can be explosive if it leaks. Digester facilities require leak detection and ventilation systems. Waste-to-energy plants must manage toxic ash and ensure combustion temperatures are high enough to destroy harmful compounds.

### B. Cogeneration Plants

{{< drg/requirement number="7b" >}}
Cogeneration plants
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

**How it works:** Cogeneration (also called combined heat and power, or CHP) captures the waste heat from electricity generation and puts it to use — heating buildings, industrial processes, or hot water systems. A conventional power plant might be 35% efficient (65% of the energy goes up the cooling tower as waste heat). A cogeneration plant can reach 80-90% efficiency by using that heat instead of wasting it.

**What is being improved:** Micro-CHP systems are being developed for individual homes and small buildings. Advanced turbine designs and fuel cell-based CHP systems are increasing efficiency further. District heating networks that distribute waste heat through underground pipes are expanding in cities across Europe and some U.S. locations.

**Cost:** CHP systems have higher upfront costs than simple boilers or power plants, but the energy savings typically pay back the investment in 3-7 years. They are most cost-effective for facilities with steady, year-round heat demand — hospitals, universities, industrial plants.

**Environmental impact:** By using fuel more efficiently, CHP reduces total fuel consumption and greenhouse gas emissions compared to separate heat and electricity systems. Less fuel burned means less air pollution.

**Safety concerns:** Similar to any facility burning fuel — fire risk, combustion gas management, and noise from turbines. Natural gas-fired CHP units require proper ventilation and gas leak detection.

### C. Fossil Fuel Power Plants

{{< drg/requirement number="7c" >}}
Fossil fuel power plants
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

**How it works:** Coal, natural gas, and oil are burned to produce heat, which boils water into steam, which spins a turbine connected to a generator. Natural gas plants can also use gas turbines (similar to jet engines) where hot combustion gases directly spin the turbine.

**What is being improved:** Combined-cycle natural gas plants use both a gas turbine and a steam turbine in sequence, pushing efficiency above 60% — nearly double the efficiency of older coal plants. Carbon capture and storage (CCS) technology aims to trap CO2 from exhaust gases before they reach the atmosphere and store it underground. Ultra-supercritical coal plants operate at higher temperatures and pressures to extract more energy from each ton of coal.

**Cost:** Natural gas is currently the cheapest fossil fuel for electricity in most of the U.S. Carbon capture adds 30-80% to the cost of electricity, which has slowed its adoption. Coal plants are becoming uneconomical in many regions as natural gas and renewables undercut them on price.

**Environmental impact:** Fossil fuels are the largest source of greenhouse gas emissions worldwide. Coal is the most carbon-intensive, followed by oil and natural gas. Burning fossil fuels also produces sulfur dioxide, nitrogen oxides, particulate matter, and mercury (especially coal). Even with improvements, fossil fuel plants have significantly larger environmental footprints than renewables.

**Safety concerns:** Coal ash contains toxic heavy metals and must be stored carefully. Natural gas pipelines and storage present explosion risks. Power plant workers face industrial hazards. Coal mining is one of the most dangerous occupations.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
A single large coal power plant can burn 10,000-15,000 tons of coal per day and produce enough electricity for about 500,000 homes. But it also produces about 30,000 tons of CO2 per day — along with hundreds of tons of fly ash and sulfur compounds.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

### D. Fuel Cells

{{< drg/requirement number="7d" >}}
Fuel cells
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

**How it works:** A fuel cell generates electricity through a chemical reaction, typically combining hydrogen and oxygen. Unlike combustion, this is an electrochemical process — similar to a battery, but a fuel cell runs continuously as long as fuel is supplied. The only byproduct when using pure hydrogen is water.

**What is being improved:** Proton exchange membrane (PEM) fuel cells are getting smaller, lighter, and cheaper, making them practical for vehicles. Solid oxide fuel cells operate at very high temperatures and can use natural gas directly, making them suitable for buildings and industrial use. Researchers are working on reducing the amount of expensive platinum catalyst needed and developing cheaper membrane materials.

**Cost:** Still expensive compared to batteries and internal combustion engines, but costs have dropped more than 60% in the past decade. Hydrogen fuel itself must be produced — and most hydrogen today comes from natural gas reforming, which emits CO2. "Green hydrogen" produced by electrolysis using renewable electricity is cleaner but currently costs 2-3 times more.

**Environmental impact:** If fueled by green hydrogen, fuel cells produce zero emissions — only water. If fueled by hydrogen from natural gas, the upstream emissions are significant. Fuel cells are quiet and produce no direct air pollution at the point of use.

**Safety concerns:** Hydrogen is extremely flammable and burns with an invisible flame. Storage and transport require specially designed high-pressure tanks. Proper ventilation and leak detection are critical. However, hydrogen is lighter than air and disperses quickly, which can actually be safer than gasoline spills in some scenarios.

### E. Geothermal Power Plants

{{< drg/requirement number="7e" >}}
Geothermal power plants
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

**How it works:** Geothermal plants tap heat from deep underground — typically from hot water or steam reservoirs near tectonic plate boundaries or volcanic areas. Wells drilled into these reservoirs bring hot fluid to the surface, where it drives a turbine to generate electricity. The cooled fluid is then injected back underground to be reheated.

**What is being improved:** Enhanced Geothermal Systems (EGS) can create artificial reservoirs by injecting water into hot dry rock deep underground and fracturing it to allow water flow. This technology could unlock geothermal power almost anywhere on Earth, not just in geologically active areas. Advanced drilling techniques borrowed from the oil and gas industry are making deeper, hotter reservoirs accessible.

**Cost:** Geothermal plants have high upfront drilling costs, but very low operating costs since the fuel (Earth's heat) is free. Once built, geothermal electricity costs are competitive with fossil fuels. EGS technology is still expensive but costs are declining.

**Environmental impact:** Geothermal is one of the cleanest energy sources. Plants produce minimal CO2 (about 5% of a coal plant's emissions) and have a tiny land footprint. However, geothermal fluids can contain dissolved minerals and gases (including small amounts of hydrogen sulfide) that must be managed.

**Safety concerns:** Drilling into high-pressure underground reservoirs carries geological risks. In rare cases, geothermal operations have been linked to small induced earthquakes. Hydrogen sulfide gas (rotten egg smell) is toxic at high concentrations and must be captured.

### F. Nuclear Power Plants

{{< drg/requirement number="7f" >}}
Nuclear power plants
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

**How it works:** Nuclear fission splits uranium-235 atoms, releasing enormous heat. That heat boils water into steam, which spins a turbine to generate electricity. A single nuclear plant can power over a million homes and runs 24/7 regardless of weather.

**What is being improved:** Small modular reactors (SMRs) are smaller, factory-built reactor designs that can be deployed faster and at lower upfront cost than traditional large reactors. Advanced reactor designs use different coolants (molten salt, liquid sodium) that operate at higher temperatures and lower pressures, improving safety and efficiency. Some new designs can even use spent fuel from older reactors as fuel.

**Cost:** Traditional large nuclear plants are extremely expensive to build — often $10-20 billion and 10+ years of construction. Operating costs are low once running. SMRs aim to bring construction costs down through factory manufacturing and simpler designs.

**Environmental impact:** Nuclear produces zero greenhouse gas emissions during operation and has the smallest land footprint per unit of energy of any power source. The challenge is radioactive waste — spent fuel remains dangerous for thousands of years and must be stored securely. Currently, most spent fuel sits in cooling pools and dry casks at reactor sites because no permanent repository has been built in the U.S.

**Safety concerns:** Reactor meltdowns, though extremely rare, have caused major disasters (Chernobyl 1986, Fukushima 2011). Modern reactor designs include passive safety systems that shut down the reactor automatically without human intervention or external power. Radiation protection for workers and surrounding communities is strictly regulated.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Nuclear energy is one of the most heavily regulated industries in the world. The Nuclear Regulatory Commission (NRC) oversees every aspect of U.S. nuclear plant design, construction, operation, and decommissioning. Multiple redundant safety systems are required, and plants must be designed to withstand earthquakes, floods, and other extreme events.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### G. Solar Power Systems

{{< drg/requirement number="7g" >}}
Solar power systems
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

**How it works:** Photovoltaic (PV) solar panels convert sunlight directly into electricity using semiconductor materials (usually silicon). When photons strike the solar cell, they knock electrons loose, creating an electrical current. Concentrated solar power (CSP) uses mirrors to focus sunlight onto a receiver, heating a fluid that drives a steam turbine.

**What is being improved:** PV cell efficiency has been climbing — commercial panels now achieve 20-23% efficiency, with laboratory cells exceeding 47%. Perovskite solar cells are a newer technology that could be cheaper and easier to manufacture than silicon. Bifacial panels capture reflected light on both sides. Battery storage integration (especially lithium-ion) allows solar power to be stored for use after sunset.

**Cost:** The cost of solar electricity has dropped 90% since 2010. In many regions, new solar is now the cheapest source of electricity ever built. Residential rooftop systems typically pay for themselves in 6-10 years through reduced electric bills.

**Environmental impact:** Solar panels produce zero emissions during operation. Manufacturing panels requires energy and some hazardous materials (cadmium, lead in some designs), but lifecycle emissions are extremely low. Large solar farms use significant land area, though they can coexist with agriculture ("agrivoltaics").

**Safety concerns:** Rooftop installation involves fall hazards and electrical shock risk during wiring. Panels produce DC electricity that can cause arc faults. Firefighters must be trained to handle solar-equipped buildings because panels cannot be shut off while the sun is shining. End-of-life panel recycling is an emerging concern.

### H. Tidal, Wave, or Ocean Thermal Energy

{{< drg/requirement number="7h" >}}
Tidal energy, wave energy, or ocean thermal energy conversion devices
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

**How it works:** These technologies harvest energy from the ocean. Tidal energy uses the predictable rise and fall of tides to drive turbines in underwater barrages or tidal stream generators (like underwater wind turbines). Wave energy captures the up-and-down motion of surface waves using floating devices or underwater pressure systems. Ocean thermal energy conversion (OTEC) exploits the temperature difference between warm surface water and cold deep water to drive a heat engine.

**What is being improved:** Tidal stream turbines are being scaled up and tested in locations with strong tidal currents (Scotland, France, Canada). Wave energy converters are being made more durable to survive storms. OTEC pilot plants are being tested in tropical locations like Hawaii, where the surface-to-deep temperature difference is greatest.

**Cost:** Currently expensive — these technologies are still in early commercial stages. Tidal barrages are the most proven but require massive infrastructure. Costs are expected to fall as deployment increases and designs mature.

**Environmental impact:** No fuel, no emissions, and highly predictable (tides follow lunar cycles precisely). Potential concerns include impacts on marine life from underwater turbines, changes to sediment transport in tidal zones, and visual impact of installations.

**Safety concerns:** Harsh ocean environments make construction and maintenance dangerous. Underwater turbines must be designed so marine life can avoid them. Saltwater corrosion requires specialized materials and frequent inspection.

### I. Wind Turbines

{{< drg/requirement number="7i" >}}
Wind turbines.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

**How it works:** Wind turbines convert kinetic energy from wind into electricity. Wind pushes against angled blades, making them spin. The spinning rotor connects to a generator that produces electricity. Modern utility-scale turbines can stand over 500 feet tall with blade spans wider than a football field.

**What is being improved:** Turbines keep getting bigger — larger rotors capture more wind and generate more power per unit. Offshore wind farms are being built in ocean waters where wind is stronger and more consistent than on land. Floating turbine platforms allow wind farms in deep water. Advanced materials (carbon fiber blades) and better gearbox designs improve reliability and reduce maintenance.

**Cost:** Onshore wind is one of the cheapest sources of new electricity in the U.S. Offshore wind is more expensive due to the challenges of building in the ocean, but costs are falling rapidly. Wind power purchase agreements now routinely come in below $0.03 per kWh — cheaper than any fossil fuel.

**Environmental impact:** Wind produces zero emissions during operation. Concerns include bird and bat collisions (though deaths are far fewer than from cats, buildings, or vehicles), noise for nearby residents, and visual impact on landscapes. End-of-life blade disposal is a growing issue, though recycling programs are being developed.

**Safety concerns:** Tower climbing for maintenance is inherently dangerous. Blade throw (a blade breaking loose) is extremely rare but requires setback distances from buildings. Lightning strikes are a risk — modern turbines have lightning protection systems. Ice formation on blades in cold climates can be a hazard.
