{{< drg/requirement number="4a" >}}
List the types of energy used in your home such as electricity, wood, oil, liquid petroleum, and natural gas, and tell how each is delivered and measured, and the current cost; OR record the transportation fuel used, miles driven, miles per gallon, and trips using your family car or another vehicle.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Every home runs on energy, but most of us never think about where it comes from, how it gets to our door, or what it costs. This requirement changes that. You are going to investigate your home like a detective and figure out exactly what fuels keep your household running.

You have two options for this requirement. You can analyze your home's energy sources **OR** track your family vehicle's fuel use. Either way, you will need to gather real data.

### Option 1: Home Energy Sources

Walk through your home and identify every type of energy used. Most homes use at least two types. Here is what to look for and how each one works:

**Electricity**
- **Delivered by**: Power lines from the local utility company to your home's meter and breaker panel.
- **Measured in**: Kilowatt-hours (kWh). One kWh is enough to run ten 100-watt light bulbs for one hour.
- **How to find cost**: Look at your family's electric bill or ask a parent or guardian. The cost per kWh varies by location — the national average is roughly $0.16/kWh but it ranges widely by state.

**Natural Gas**
- **Delivered by**: Underground pipelines to a gas meter on the side of your home.
- **Measured in**: Therms or cubic feet (CCF = 100 cubic feet). One therm equals about 100,000 BTU.
- **Common uses**: Furnace, water heater, stove/oven, clothes dryer, fireplace.
- **How to find cost**: Check the gas bill. National average is roughly $1.00–1.50 per therm.

**Heating Oil**
- **Delivered by**: Truck to an oil storage tank, usually in the basement or outside the house.
- **Measured in**: Gallons.
- **Common uses**: Furnace for home heating.
- **How to find cost**: Ask a parent or check delivery receipts.

**Liquid Petroleum Gas (Propane)**
- **Delivered by**: Truck to an outdoor propane tank.
- **Measured in**: Gallons.
- **Common uses**: Heating, cooking, water heating (common in rural areas without natural gas pipelines).
- **How to find cost**: Check delivery receipts or propane company statements.

**Wood**
- **Delivered by**: Purchased or cut yourself, stacked near the home.
- **Measured in**: Cords (a cord is a stack 4 feet wide × 4 feet tall × 8 feet long).
- **Common uses**: Fireplace, wood stove for supplemental heat.

{{< drg/tip >}}
The easiest way to find costs and measurements is to look at your family's utility bills. Most bills show the amount of energy used, the unit of measurement, and the cost per unit. If you cannot find a bill, check your utility company's website — many show usage history online.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Option 2: Vehicle Fuel Tracking

If you choose the vehicle option, you need to track real trips over your 14-day log period. Here is what to record:

| Data Point | How to Get It |
|-----------|---------------|
| Fuel type | Gasoline, diesel, or electric (check the fuel door or owner's manual) |
| Miles driven | Note the odometer at the start and end of each day or trip |
| Miles per gallon | Calculate by dividing miles driven by gallons used at each fill-up |
| Trips taken | Log each trip with its purpose (school, grocery, practice, etc.) and distance |
| Fuel cost | Record the price per gallon at the pump and the total cost per fill-up |

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The average American household spends about $2,300 per year on home energy (electricity and heating fuels combined) and another $2,000-3,000 on transportation fuel. That means a family can spend more than $5,000 a year just on energy — and much of it is wasted through inefficiency.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

### Keeping Your 14-Day Log

Remember, this data feeds into a 14-day log that the overall requirement asks for. Start recording now so you have real numbers. A simple notebook or spreadsheet works well. For home energy, you might not see daily usage changes on the meter, but you can track daily habits (lights left on, heater running, appliances used) and compare your beginning and ending meter readings.
