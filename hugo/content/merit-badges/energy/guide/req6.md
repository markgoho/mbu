{{< drg/requirement number="6" >}}
Prepare pie charts showing the following information, and explain to your counselor the important ideas each chart reveals. Tell where you got your information. Explain how cost affects the use of a nonrenewable energy resource and makes alternatives practical.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This requirement asks you to create five pie charts about energy in the United States and the world. You will need to find reliable, current data for each one. Here are all five topics:

- **6a** — Energy resources that supply the U.S. with most of its energy
- **6b** — Share of U.S. energy resources from other countries
- **6c** — Proportion of energy used by homes, businesses, industry, and transportation
- **6d** — Fuels used to generate America's electricity
- **6e** — World's known and estimated primary energy resource reserves

### Where to Find the Data

The single best source for all five charts is the **U.S. Energy Information Administration (EIA)**. Their data is authoritative, current, and free.

### Chart-by-Chart Guidance

Here is what each chart should show and what patterns to look for:

#### 6a — U.S. Energy Supply by Source

{{< drg/requirement number="6a" >}}
The energy resources that supply the United States with most of its energy
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This chart shows the overall energy mix — what fuels America runs on. The major categories are petroleum (oil), natural gas, coal, nuclear, and renewables (which include hydro, wind, solar, biomass, and geothermal). Look for how fossil fuels still dominate the mix and how the renewable share has been growing.

#### 6b — Imported vs. Domestic Energy

{{< drg/requirement number="6b" >}}
The share of energy resources used by the United States that comes from other countries
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This chart compares domestically produced energy to imported energy. The U.S. has shifted dramatically in recent years. Thanks to expanded domestic oil and gas production, the U.S. became a net total energy exporter in 2019 for the first time since the 1950s. Your chart should reflect the current balance.

#### 6c — Energy Use by Sector

{{< drg/requirement number="6c" >}}
The proportion of energy resources used by homes, businesses, industry, and transportation
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This chart divides energy consumption into four sectors: residential (homes), commercial (offices, stores, schools), industrial (factories, refineries, mining), and transportation (cars, trucks, planes, trains). Notice which sector uses the most — industrial and transportation typically lead.

#### 6d — Electricity Generation by Fuel

{{< drg/requirement number="6d" >}}
The fuels used to generate America's electricity
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This chart focuses specifically on electricity, not total energy. It shows what fuels power plants burn to generate electricity. The mix is different from the overall energy supply because transportation uses almost no electricity (yet). Natural gas and renewables have been gaining share while coal has been declining sharply.

#### 6e — Global Energy Reserves

{{< drg/requirement number="6e" >}}
The world's known and estimated primary energy resource reserves.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This chart shows how much oil, natural gas, coal, and uranium the world has left in proven reserves. "Proven reserves" means quantities that geological surveys confirm can be recovered with current technology at current prices. Estimated reserves include additional resources believed to exist but not yet fully confirmed.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
At current consumption rates, the world's proven oil reserves would last roughly 50 years, natural gas about 50 years, and coal over 130 years. But these numbers change constantly — new reserves are discovered, consumption patterns shift, and new extraction technologies emerge. In 2000, many experts predicted the U.S. would run out of natural gas within two decades. Instead, hydraulic fracturing technology unlocked vast new reserves.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

### How to Make Your Pie Charts

You can create pie charts by hand on paper or using software. Both approaches are acceptable — check with your counselor on their preference.

**By hand:**
1. Draw a circle using a compass or a round object.
2. Calculate the angle for each slice: multiply the percentage by 3.6 (since a full circle is 360°).
3. Use a protractor to measure each angle from the center.
4. Label each slice with the category and percentage.
5. Add a title and your data source.

**Using software:**
- Google Sheets or Microsoft Excel can create pie charts from data you enter.
- Type your categories in one column and percentages in another, then insert a chart.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Color-code your charts and include a legend. Use consistent colors across charts where possible — for example, use the same green for renewables in every chart. This makes it easy to compare across charts during your counselor discussion.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Cost and Nonrenewable Resources

The requirement also asks you to explain how cost affects the use of nonrenewable resources and makes alternatives practical. Here is the key idea:

When a nonrenewable resource like oil is cheap and abundant, there is little economic incentive to switch to alternatives. But as reserves decline, extraction becomes more difficult and expensive — companies have to drill deeper, in more remote locations, or use more complex technology. As the cost of the nonrenewable source rises, renewable alternatives that were once considered "too expensive" become competitive.

This is exactly what has happened with solar and wind power. A decade ago, solar electricity cost 3-5 times more than coal or natural gas electricity. Today, new solar and wind installations are often the cheapest source of new electricity, even without government subsidies. The cost crossover point is when alternatives become practical — and for many renewables, that crossover has already happened.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Discussing Your Charts" >}}
When you present your charts to your counselor, go beyond just reading the numbers. For each chart, explain:
- What is the biggest slice, and why?
- What surprised you about the data?
- How has this mix been changing in recent years?
- What do you think the chart will look like in 10 or 20 years?
