{{< drg/requirement number="1" >}}
Handling a First Aid Emergency. Do the following:
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Before you can treat a wound or splint a leg, you need a clear mental framework for every emergency you walk into. Requirement 1 gives you that framework — a repeatable set of skills and habits you can run through in seconds, no matter how chaotic the scene looks. Work through each sub-requirement in order; they build on each other and mirror the real sequence every trained responder follows.

## Requirement 1a

{{< drg/requirement number="1a" >}}
Explain the steps necessary to assess and handle a first aid emergency, including a safety evaluation of the scene.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

The single biggest mistake untrained bystanders make is sprinting straight to the victim without sizing up the scene. A few seconds of observation can tell you whether the area is safe, how many people need help, and what resources you have available. Rushing past that step can turn one injured person into two.

### The Three Cs: Check, Call, Care

Almost every first-aid training system — Red Cross, American Heart Association, BSA — organizes the initial response around the same basic sequence. Some programs expand or label it differently, but the logic is identical.

#### Step 1 — Check the Scene

Before you touch anyone, scan the surroundings and answer these questions:

- **Is the scene safe?** Look for traffic, downed power lines, unstable structures, fire, aggressive animals, or any other hazard that could hurt you.
- **What happened?** A fall? A car crash? A sudden illness? The mechanism of injury helps you predict hidden problems (a fall from height suggests possible spine injury; a car crash suggests both blunt and penetrating trauma).
- **How many victims are there?** Look beyond the loudest person. Quiet victims may be more seriously injured.
- **What resources are available?** Other bystanders, nearby first-aid kits, an AED, a phone signal?

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Never enter an unsafe scene. If there is a live electrical hazard, a burning building, active violence, or unstable ground, call 911 and wait for trained responders. You cannot help anyone if you become a victim yourself.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

#### Step 2 — Call for Help

Once the scene is safe and you have a quick sense of what happened, alert the emergency medical system. For most situations, that means calling 911. For a wilderness setting it may mean sending a runner, activating a personal locator beacon, or using a satellite communicator. The key rule: **call early, not late.** Do not wait to see whether things improve on their own before calling.

#### Step 3 — Care for the Victim

Only after the scene is confirmed safe and help has been summoned (or is on the way) do you move to hands-on assessment and treatment.

### Initial Patient Assessment

When you reach the patient, run a rapid check of the life threats in priority order:

1. **Level of consciousness** — Does the patient respond when you speak or tap their shoulder? (Use: "Are you okay?")
2. **Airway** — Is the airway open and clear?
3. **Breathing** — Is the patient breathing? Normally? Labored?
4. **Circulation / Severe Bleeding** — Is there life-threatening bleeding that must be controlled immediately?
5. **Disability** — Are there obvious signs of serious injury (severe head injury, suspected spinal problem, altered mental state)?
6. **Expose / Environment** — Is the patient in danger from the environment (cold, heat, further falling debris)?

This sequence is sometimes remembered as **ABCDE** (Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Expose).

{{< drg/tip >}}
Practice saying the check sequence out loud until you can do it from memory in any order. Under stress, the brain needs rehearsed habits, not new thinking. A few minutes of verbal practice during a troop meeting is better preparation than reading the steps once.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Requirement 1b

{{< drg/requirement number="1b" >}}
Tell how you would obtain emergency medical assistance from your home and from a remote location on a wilderness camping trip.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Getting help fast is as important as the first-aid steps you perform. How you call for help depends entirely on where you are when the emergency happens.

### From Home or an Urban / Suburban Setting

This is the most straightforward situation. The 911 system in the United States is designed to get emergency services to you quickly once you make contact.

{{< drg/checklist title="What to Tell 911" subtitle="Give the dispatcher this information in order" >}}
- Your exact location (street address and cross street, or a landmark the dispatcher can map).
- The nature of the emergency (injured person, chest pain, unconscious victim, etc.).
- How many patients there are.
- The patient's current condition (conscious, breathing, bleeding, etc.).
- Your name and callback phone number.
- Any specific hazards on scene (gas leak, downed wire, aggressive dog).
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/tip >}}
Stay on the line. Dispatchers are trained to provide instructions while help is on the way — they may walk you through CPR, bleeding control, or other life-saving steps. Hang up only if the dispatcher tells you to.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

**Other resources at home:**

- Post emergency numbers where family members can find them (refrigerator, beside every landline phone).
- Know your exact home address — young children and people in an emergency can forget.
- Know the location of the nearest hospital emergency department.
- Ensure your household has a working first-aid kit and that everyone knows where it is.

### From a Remote or Wilderness Location

Distance, terrain, poor weather, and lack of cell service change the rules completely. You may need to treat a patient for hours — or even days — before evacuation is possible. This is where preparation matters most.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Communication Options in the Backcountry" >}}
- **Cell phone** — Check coverage maps before you go. In many wilderness areas there is no signal, or only a weak signal on ridgelines. Keep your phone charged and stored in a waterproof case.
- **Satellite communicator** (SPOT, Garmin inReach) — Sends an SOS signal and your GPS coordinates to a rescue coordination center anywhere on earth. Highly recommended for remote trips.
- **Personal Locator Beacon (PLB)** — A one-time use (or subscription-free) emergency beacon registered with NOAA. Activating it alerts search-and-rescue authorities directly.
- **Satellite phone** — Allows voice communication where there is no cell signal.
- **Ground-to-air signals** — If rescue aircraft are likely, create a large signal in a clearing (SOS in rocks, a bright-colored tarp, a signal mirror).
- **Send a runner** — If none of the above are available, send the fastest, most capable member of your group to the nearest trail junction, road, or ranger station. Send them with a written note containing the patient's location (GPS coordinates or map description), condition, and time of injury.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Before any wilderness trip, file a trip plan with a responsible person who is not on the trip. Tell them your route, campsites, expected return date, and what to do — and who to call — if you do not return on time. This is your safety net if all other communication fails.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Requirement 1c

{{< drg/requirement number="1c" >}}
Demonstrate the precautions you must take to reduce the risk of transmitting an infection between you and the victim while administering first aid, including the safe disposal of used first aid supplies.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

When you provide first aid, you may be exposed to blood, saliva, vomit, or other body fluids. Standard precautions assume that any body fluid from any person could carry an infectious agent. This is not pessimism — it is the same practice used by emergency medical professionals, and it protects both you and your patient.

### Personal Protective Equipment (PPE)

The most important step is putting on a barrier before you begin care.

{{< drg/checklist title="PPE for First Aid" subtitle="Always use what is available in your kit" >}}
- **Disposable gloves** — Put these on before touching any patient or body fluid. If you do not have gloves, use a plastic bag, extra cloth, or any available barrier. Double-gloving (one pair over another) adds protection if gloves are thin.
- **Eye protection** — Use if there is any risk of blood or fluid splashing toward your face (arterial bleeding, CPR with vomiting).
- **Face mask or shield** — Use during rescue breathing to create a barrier between your mouth and the patient's.
- **Gown or apron** — Rarely available in field situations, but useful if significant blood exposure is expected.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

### During Care

- Avoid touching your face, eyes, or mouth while treating a patient.
- If gloves tear or are punctured, replace them as soon as possible.
- If you have a cut or open wound on your hands, cover it with a bandage before gloving up.

### After Care — Safe Removal and Disposal

How you take off gloves matters as much as wearing them. A contaminated outer surface must never touch your skin.

**The correct glove removal technique:**
1. Grasp the outside of one glove at the wrist (do not touch skin).
2. Peel it off inside-out and hold it in your gloved hand.
3. Slide two fingers of the ungloved hand under the wrist of the second glove.
4. Peel the second glove off inside-out, enclosing the first glove inside it.
5. Drop the bundle into a waste bag without touching the outside surfaces.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Used gloves, bandages, dressings, and anything contaminated with blood or body fluids are biohazardous waste. Seal them in a plastic bag before placing in the trash. Never leave them on the ground, in a latrine, or where others may touch them.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

**After removing PPE:**
- Wash your hands thoroughly with soap and water for at least 20 seconds, paying attention to the backs of your hands, between fingers, and under your nails.
- If soap and water are not available, use an alcohol-based hand sanitizer (at least 60% alcohol) as a temporary measure — then wash with soap and water as soon as possible.

## Requirement 1d

{{< drg/requirement number="1d" >}}
Demonstrate evaluation of and management of a patient's airway and breathing.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

If a patient is not breathing — or if their airway is blocked — the brain begins to suffer damage within minutes. Airway and breathing are always assessed before anything else (except stopping massive bleeding, which can also kill in seconds).

### Opening and Checking the Airway

For an unresponsive patient who has not suffered trauma, use the **head-tilt, chin-lift** technique:

1. Place one hand on the patient's forehead.
2. Place two fingers of the other hand under the bony part of the chin.
3. Tilt the head back gently while lifting the chin to open the airway.

If trauma (a fall, vehicle collision, diving accident) may have injured the neck or spine, use the **jaw-thrust** technique instead — this opens the airway without moving the spine.

### Checking for Breathing

Once the airway is open:

- **Look** — Does the chest rise and fall?
- **Listen** — Can you hear breath sounds?
- **Feel** — Can you feel air moving against your cheek?

Take no more than 10 seconds for this check. Occasional gasping or irregular breathing (agonal breathing) is **not** normal breathing — treat the patient as if they are not breathing.

### Managing the Airway

| Situation | Action |
|---|---|
| Patient is breathing normally | Position in recovery position (on side) if unconscious and no spinal injury suspected |
| Airway blocked by visible object | Use finger sweeps only if you can see the object — blind finger sweeps can push an object deeper |
| Airway blocked by fluid (blood, vomit) | Roll patient on side to drain; use a cloth to clear the mouth |
| Patient is not breathing | Begin rescue breathing and/or CPR as trained |

{{< drg/tip >}}
When demonstrating this for your counselor, narrate your actions. "I am placing one hand on the forehead, two fingers under the chin — I'm going to tilt the head back and lift the chin to open the airway." Narrating helps your counselor verify your knowledge and keeps you focused.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Requirement 1e

{{< drg/requirement number="1e" >}}
Demonstrate a thorough examination of an accident victim.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

After handling the immediate life threats (airway, breathing, catastrophic bleeding), a thorough physical examination helps you find injuries you may have missed in the first rush of activity. This is sometimes called a **secondary survey** or **head-to-toe exam**.

### How to Do a Head-to-Toe Exam

Work from the head toward the feet, using both hands, looking and feeling for anything abnormal: deformities, open wounds, tenderness, swelling, bruising, or unusual sounds.

{{< drg/checklist title="Head-to-Toe Exam Sequence" subtitle="Conduct gently, and stop if the patient says something hurts" >}}
- **Head and face** — Check the skull for deformity or bleeding; look in the ears for blood or fluid; check the mouth and nose; look at the pupils (should be equal and reactive to light).
- **Neck** — Feel gently for tenderness or deformity; look for medical alert jewelry.
- **Collar bones and shoulders** — Feel for tenderness or abnormal movement.
- **Chest** — Gently squeeze from both sides; watch for equal rise of both sides during breathing; listen for unusual breath sounds.
- **Abdomen** — Press gently on all four quadrants; rigidity or guarding suggests internal injury.
- **Pelvis** — Gently press inward and downward; instability suggests a serious fracture.
- **Legs** — Feel along the full length of each leg; check for deformity, swelling, tenderness.
- **Feet** — Check circulation (skin color, warmth, capillary refill); ask the patient to wiggle toes if able.
- **Arms** — Same as legs; check for deformity, wounds, sensation.
- **Back** — If safe to do so, gently run your hands along the spine and the muscles on either side.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

**After the exam, document and report:**
- What you found and where
- Time of your exam
- Patient's response to examination
- Any changes in condition since your initial check

{{< drg/tip >}}
When practicing with a partner, have one person play the patient with a hidden "injury" (a piece of tape on one leg, a bent arm, shoes on the wrong feet). The "responder" should find it during the head-to-toe exam. This makes a dry drill into an actual skill test.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Requirement 1f

{{< drg/requirement number="1f" >}}
Discuss why shock is an emergency.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Shock is a life-threatening condition in which the body's organs and tissues are not receiving enough oxygen-rich blood. It is not simply feeling faint or scared — medical shock is a genuine physiological emergency that can kill if untreated.

### What Happens During Shock

The cardiovascular system depends on three things working together: a functioning pump (the heart), enough fluid (blood), and open tubes (blood vessels). Shock occurs when any one of these fails.

**Common causes:**
- **Hypovolemic shock** — Too little blood or fluid (severe bleeding, severe burns, dehydration)
- **Cardiogenic shock** — The heart is not pumping effectively (heart attack, severe trauma to the chest)
- **Distributive shock** — Blood vessels dilate abnormally, dropping blood pressure (severe allergic reaction / anaphylaxis, severe infection / sepsis, spinal injury)
- **Obstructive shock** — Blood flow is blocked (tension pneumothorax, pulmonary embolism)

### Recognizing Shock

Early shock is easy to miss because the body compensates. By the time the classic signs are obvious, the patient is in serious trouble.

{{< drg/checklist title="Signs and Symptoms of Shock" subtitle="Look for clusters — one sign alone may not mean shock" >}}
- Pale, cool, clammy skin (the body redirects blood from the skin to vital organs)
- Rapid, weak pulse
- Rapid, shallow breathing
- Altered mental state — confusion, anxiety, restlessness, or reduced responsiveness
- Nausea or vomiting
- Extreme thirst
- In late shock: very low blood pressure, blue-gray skin color around the lips and fingernails
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

### Why Shock Is an Emergency

Shock is self-reinforcing and progressive. As blood pressure drops, the heart works harder to compensate — which uses more oxygen — which it cannot get because of the low blood pressure. Without intervention this cycle accelerates rapidly. Even if a patient appears stable, unrecognized or untreated shock can lead to organ failure and death within minutes to hours.

### First Aid for Shock

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Any patient you suspect may be in shock needs emergency medical care. Call 911 immediately and do not leave the patient alone.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

While waiting for emergency services:

- Control any external bleeding (the most common cause of shock you will encounter).
- Have the patient lie flat and, if no spinal injury is suspected and breathing allows, elevate the legs about 12 inches — this helps return blood to the core and brain.
- Keep the patient warm (hypothermia makes shock worse).
- Do not give anything by mouth.
- Monitor and reassure — a calm responder reduces the patient's anxiety, which slows the spiral.
- Reassess frequently; be ready to begin CPR.

## Requirement 1g

{{< drg/requirement number="1g" >}}
Define the term triage and describe examples of triage situations that you may encounter.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

**Triage** (from the French *trier*, "to sort") is the process of quickly assessing multiple patients and prioritizing who receives care first based on the severity of their injury or illness and their likelihood of survival with treatment.

Triage is used any time there are more patients than rescuers — or when one person is managing a scene alone and must decide where to focus limited resources.

### The Core Principle

In triage, you do not always help the most seriously hurt person first. You help the person who will benefit most from immediate care. A patient who is already unresponsive with no pulse may need CPR, but if there is another patient who is bleeding severely and will die without a tourniquet in the next 60 seconds, you stop the bleed first.

This is a hard mental shift. Triage prioritizes the group's survival over the individual's need for attention.

### Triage Categories

Most simple triage systems use color tags or categories:

| Priority | Color | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Immediate | Red | Life-threatening condition, survives with prompt care |
| Delayed | Yellow | Serious injury but stable for a short time |
| Minor | Green | Walking wounded — can wait for treatment |
| Expectant | Black | Unlikely to survive even with full resources, or already dead |

### Scenarios You May Encounter as a Scout

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Common Triage Scenarios" >}}
- **Multi-victim vehicle accident on a camping trip** — One person is unconscious and not breathing; another has a broken arm and is calling out; a third is standing, dazed but responsive. You would go to the unconscious person first (most immediate threat), instruct someone to stay with the broken-arm patient, and reassure the walking victim.
- **Severe thunderstorm at summer camp** — Lightning strikes near a campsite, injuring several Scouts. Some are stunned and standing; one is on the ground and motionless; one has a visible laceration bleeding steadily. Triage helps you prioritize who needs CPR, who needs bleeding control, and who can wait.
- **Waterfront accident** — Two swimmers are in trouble at the same time, one near the shore and one farther out. Limited rescuers must decide which patient is reachable and which presents the most immediate life threat.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

{{< drg/tip >}}
When talking to your counselor about triage, walk through a specific scenario rather than speaking in abstractions. Describe the scene, the patients, your categories, and the order of care. This shows understanding — not just vocabulary.
{{< /drg/tip >}}
