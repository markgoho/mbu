{{< drg/requirement number="13" >}}
Miscellaneous Conditions. Describe the symptoms and signs of, show first aid for, and explain prevention of the following conditions:
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

These five conditions may seem unrelated, but they share a common theme: each one can look minor at first while carrying a real risk of serious harm if the right steps are not taken immediately. For each condition you must be able to **describe** what it looks like, **show** what to do about it, and **explain** how to prevent it.

## Requirement 13a

{{< drg/requirement number="13a" >}}
Object in the eye
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

**Describe — Symptoms and Signs:**
- Pain, burning, or stinging in the eye
- Redness and watering (tearing) of the eye
- The sensation that something is in the eye even after the object is removed
- Sensitivity to light
- Difficulty keeping the eye open
- Visible foreign body on the surface of the eye or under an eyelid

**Show — First Aid for an Object in the Eye:**

**For a loose, floating particle (dust, grit, eyelash):**
1. Do not rub the eye — rubbing can scratch the cornea.
2. Wash your hands before touching the eye area.
3. Have the person blink several times. Tears often flush small particles out naturally.
4. Gently pull the upper eyelid down over the lower eyelid and release.
5. Flush the eye with clean water or saline using an eye cup or a clean cup held against the open eye, or pour a gentle stream from a bottle from the inner corner outward.
6. If flushing does not work, look under the eyelid: gently pull down the lower lid and examine the inner surface. If the particle is visible, carefully remove it with the corner of a clean, moistened cloth — never a dry cloth or sharp object.
7. If the particle is under the upper eyelid, carefully fold the eyelid back over a cotton swab and remove the particle with a moist cloth corner.

**For an embedded or penetrating object:**
1. Do **not** attempt to remove it.
2. Cover both eyes loosely with a clean cloth or sterile dressing (covering both eyes reduces movement of the injured eye).
3. Seek emergency medical care immediately.

**For chemical splash:**
1. Immediately flush with large amounts of clean water for at least 15–20 minutes.
2. Remove contact lenses if present.
3. Call 911 or poison control, and seek emergency care.

**Explain — Prevention:**
- Wear safety glasses or goggles when working with tools, chemicals, or in dusty or windy environments.
- Use face shields when operating power equipment or doing metalwork.
- Wear polycarbonate sports glasses or protective eyewear during contact sports.

## Requirement 13b

{{< drg/requirement number="13b" >}}
Broken, chipped, loosened, or knocked out tooth
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

**Describe — Symptoms and Signs:**
- Pain, sensitivity to heat, cold, or pressure at the tooth
- Visible crack, chip, or fragment
- Looseness when the tooth is touched
- Bleeding from the gum around the tooth
- For a knocked-out tooth: an empty socket with bleeding, and the whole tooth or a fragment found at the scene

**Show — First Aid for Dental Injuries:**

**Broken or chipped tooth:**
1. Rinse the mouth gently with warm water.
2. Save any tooth fragments — wrap them in a moist cloth and bring them to the dentist.
3. If there is bleeding from the gum, press a clean gauze pad gently against the area.
4. Seek dental care as soon as possible.

**Knocked-out (avulsed) tooth:**
Time matters — the sooner a knocked-out tooth is reimplanted, the better the chance of saving it. Ideally within 30–60 minutes.
1. Pick up the tooth by the **crown** (the white part), never by the root.
2. If dirty, rinse briefly with milk or saline — do not scrub, and do not use tap water if possible (it can damage the root cells).
3. **Best option:** Gently push the tooth back into its socket and hold it there with light pressure.
4. **If reimplanting is not possible:** Store the tooth in milk, saline, or between the inside of the cheek and the gum (for an older child or adult — not a young child who might swallow it).
5. Seek emergency dental care immediately.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Never store a knocked-out tooth in plain tap water for extended periods — it damages the root surface cells needed for reimplantation. Milk is the best emergency storage medium if reimplantation is not immediately possible.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

**Explain — Prevention:**
- Wear a properly fitted mouthguard for contact sports, mountain biking, skateboarding, and similar activities.
- Wear a helmet for activities where falls are possible.
- Be careful around slippery surfaces and avoid running near pools or hard-floored surfaces.

## Requirement 13c

{{< drg/requirement number="13c" >}}
Vomiting and diarrhea associated with food poisoning
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

**Describe — Symptoms and Signs:**

Food poisoning occurs when food or water contaminated by bacteria, viruses, or toxins is consumed. Symptoms usually appear within a few hours to two days of eating the contaminated food.

- Nausea and vomiting
- Diarrhea, which may be watery or bloody
- Abdominal cramps and pain
- Fever (often mild, but may be high)
- Headache and muscle aches
- Weakness and fatigue
- Signs of dehydration: dry mouth, dizziness, decreased urine output

**Show — First Aid for Food Poisoning:**
1. **Rest.** Have the person lie down in a comfortable position.
2. **Replace fluids.** Encourage small, frequent sips of clear fluids — water, diluted sports drinks, or oral rehydration solution. Avoid dairy, caffeine, and sugary drinks initially.
3. **Avoid solid food** until vomiting stops, then reintroduce bland foods gradually (crackers, toast, plain rice).
4. **Monitor closely** for signs of severe dehydration (very dry mouth, no tears, no urination for several hours, dizziness on standing) or worsening symptoms.
5. **Call for help** or seek medical care if:
   - There is blood in the vomit or stool
   - The person cannot keep any fluids down for more than 12 hours
   - The person shows signs of severe dehydration
   - A very young child or elderly person is affected
   - Symptoms began after eating wild mushrooms, shellfish, or canned food (botulism risk)

**Explain — Prevention:**
- Wash hands thoroughly with soap and water before handling food and after using the restroom.
- Cook food to safe internal temperatures.
- Keep raw meat separate from other foods to prevent cross-contamination.
- Refrigerate perishables promptly — never leave food in the "danger zone" (40°F–140°F) for more than two hours.
- Treat water from natural sources before drinking — boil, filter, or use purification tablets when camping.
- When in doubt, throw it out.

## Requirement 13d

{{< drg/requirement number="13d" >}}
Abdominal pain
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

**Describe — Symptoms and Signs:**

Abdominal pain has dozens of possible causes. As a first-aider, your job is not to diagnose but to recognize when the pain is serious enough to require professional medical care.

General abdominal pain signs include:
- Cramping, aching, or sharp pain anywhere from the lower chest to the groin
- Nausea, vomiting, or loss of appetite
- Distension (swelling of the abdomen)
- Tenderness when the abdomen is touched

Signs that indicate a potentially serious problem:
- Severe pain that comes on suddenly and is unrelenting
- Pain that begins around the navel and moves to the lower right abdomen (may indicate appendicitis)
- Rigid or board-like abdomen when touched
- Pain accompanied by fever, vomiting blood, or blood in the stool
- The person cannot find a comfortable position

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Do not give the person food, fluids, or pain medication for severe abdominal pain unless directed by medical personnel — it can mask symptoms that doctors need to evaluate. Keep the person comfortable and call for help.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

**Show — First Aid for Abdominal Pain:**
1. Have the person rest in the position most comfortable for them — often lying on their side with knees drawn up.
2. Do not apply heat to the abdomen — it may increase swelling in certain emergencies.
3. Do not give food or drink for severe abdominal pain.
4. Monitor vital signs and level of consciousness.
5. **Call 911 or seek immediate care if:**
   - The pain is sudden and severe
   - The person has symptoms of appendicitis (right lower quadrant pain, fever, vomiting)
   - Abdomen is hard or rigid to the touch
   - There is blood in vomit or stool
   - Pain follows an abdominal injury

**Explain — Prevention:**
- Practice food safety to reduce the risk of food poisoning (see 13c).
- Eat regular, balanced meals and stay hydrated to reduce gas pain and indigestion.
- Avoid eating unknown wild plants — many cause severe gastrointestinal illness.
- Get vaccinated against illnesses that cause abdominal problems (hepatitis A, typhoid for travel).

## Requirement 13e

{{< drg/requirement number="13e" >}}
Stroke
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A stroke occurs when blood flow to part of the brain is cut off — either by a blocked artery (ischemic stroke, the most common type) or a ruptured blood vessel (hemorrhagic stroke). Brain cells begin to die within minutes. Fast action is critical: the phrase **"time is brain"** is used in emergency medicine because every minute of treatment delay means more neurons lost.

**Describe — Symptoms and Signs:**

Use the **BE-FAST** acronym to recognize a stroke:

| Letter | What to check | Stroke sign |
|---|---|---|
| **B** | Balance | Sudden loss of balance or coordination |
| **E** | Eyes | Sudden vision loss, blurriness, or double vision in one or both eyes |
| **F** | Face | Ask the person to smile — does one side droop? |
| **A** | Arms | Ask the person to raise both arms — does one drift downward? |
| **S** | Speech | Ask the person to repeat a simple phrase — is it slurred or strange? |
| **T** | Time | If any sign is present, call 911 **immediately** |

Other stroke signs include:
- Sudden severe headache with no known cause ("worst headache of my life")
- Sudden numbness or weakness on one side of the face, arm, or leg
- Sudden confusion or difficulty understanding speech
- Sudden difficulty walking

**Show — First Aid for Stroke:**
1. **Call 911 immediately.** Do not wait to see if symptoms improve.
2. Note the exact time symptoms started — this information is critical for doctors deciding on treatment (certain clot-busting medications can only be given within a narrow window).
3. Keep the person calm and still. Have them sit or lie down in a comfortable position.
4. Do not give food or drink — stroke can impair swallowing.
5. If the person is unconscious and not breathing, begin CPR.
6. Reassure the person that help is coming. Anxiety raises blood pressure, which can worsen a stroke.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Some people experience a transient ischemic attack (TIA), sometimes called a "mini-stroke," in which stroke symptoms appear for only a few minutes and then resolve. TIAs are serious warning signs and require immediate medical evaluation — they often precede a major stroke within days.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

**Explain — Prevention:**
- Stroke risk factors include high blood pressure, smoking, high cholesterol, diabetes, and physical inactivity.
- Most prevention strategies are lifestyle-based and long-term, but Scouts can support family members and themselves by knowing the risk factors and encouraging healthy habits.
- Knowing the BE-FAST signs and acting quickly if someone has them is the most important prevention-adjacent action a Scout can take.
