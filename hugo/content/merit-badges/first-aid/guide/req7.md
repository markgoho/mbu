{{< drg/requirement number="7" >}}
Heart Attack. Do the following:
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Cardiac emergencies are the leading cause of death in the United States — and many of those deaths happen before anyone reaches a hospital. The skills in this requirement exist because bystander action in the first few minutes makes a measurable difference. Work through each sub-requirement in order; they build logically from understanding the event (7a), to recognizing it (7b), to knowing when to act (7c), to performing the skills themselves (7d, 7f). Requirements 7e and 7g give you the knowledge to use and find the device that can restart a heart.

## Requirement 7a

{{< drg/requirement number="7a" >}}
Explain what a heart attack is.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

The heart is a muscle. Like any muscle, it needs a constant supply of oxygen-rich blood to keep working. It gets that blood through its own dedicated vessels — the **coronary arteries**. A heart attack (myocardial infarction) happens when one of those arteries is blocked, cutting off blood supply to part of the heart muscle.

### The Sequence of Events

1. **Atherosclerosis builds up over years.** Fatty deposits called plaque accumulate inside coronary artery walls, narrowing the channel through which blood flows.
2. **A plaque ruptures.** Something (physical stress, blood pressure spike, inflammation) causes the plaque to crack or tear.
3. **A clot forms.** The body's clotting system responds to the damage, forming a blood clot that can quickly block the artery entirely.
4. **Heart muscle is deprived of oxygen.** The area of heart muscle beyond the blockage begins to die within minutes.
5. **If the blockage is not cleared, damage becomes permanent.** The longer the artery is blocked, the more muscle is lost. This is why "time is muscle" — every minute matters.

### Heart Attack vs. Cardiac Arrest

These terms are often confused, but they describe different events:

| Condition | What Is Happening | Consciousness |
|---|---|---|
| Heart attack | A coronary artery is blocked; heart muscle is dying | Person is usually conscious, in pain |
| Cardiac arrest | The heart stops pumping effectively; no circulation | Person is unresponsive, no pulse |

A heart attack can lead to cardiac arrest if the damaged heart muscle triggers a fatal rhythm disturbance. But cardiac arrest can also happen without a preceding heart attack (from drowning, choking, electrocution, or other causes). The first-aid responses are different, so understanding the distinction matters.

## Requirement 7b

{{< drg/requirement number="7b" >}}
Describe the symptoms and signs of a heart attack and first aid for this condition.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Heart attacks do not always look like the dramatic movie version — a person clutching their chest and collapsing. Many present subtly, especially in women, older adults, and people with diabetes. Knowing the full range of symptoms helps you recognize what might otherwise be mistaken for something less serious.

### Symptoms and Signs

{{< drg/checklist title="Classic and Atypical Heart Attack Symptoms" subtitle="Any combination of these warrants immediate medical evaluation" >}}
- **Chest discomfort** — pressure, squeezing, fullness, or pain in the center or left side of the chest, lasting more than a few minutes or coming and going
- **Discomfort radiating to other areas** — one or both arms (especially the left), the back, jaw, neck, or stomach
- **Shortness of breath** — with or without chest discomfort
- **Sweating** — sudden cold sweat or clammy skin
- **Nausea or vomiting**
- **Lightheadedness** or sudden dizziness
- **Unusual fatigue** — especially in women, extreme unexplained tiredness for days before the event
- **Sense of impending doom** — patients often report a feeling that something is very wrong
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/tip >}}
Women, older adults, and people with diabetes are more likely to have atypical presentations — jaw pain, unusual fatigue, shortness of breath with no chest pain. If something feels wrong and you cannot explain it, err on the side of calling for help.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### First Aid for a Suspected Heart Attack

1. **Call 911 immediately.** Do not drive the person to the hospital yourself unless no ambulance is available — EMS can begin treatment en route.
2. **Have the person stop what they are doing and rest** — sit or lie in the position that is most comfortable; often semi-reclined.
3. **Loosen any tight clothing** around the neck and chest.
4. **If the person is not allergic to aspirin**, has no bleeding conditions, and you have standard adult aspirin available, they may chew (not swallow whole) one adult aspirin (325 mg) or four low-dose (81 mg) aspirins. Ask if they have been told by a doctor to avoid aspirin.
5. **Keep the person calm and reassured.** Emotional stress increases the heart's oxygen demand.
6. **Be ready to perform CPR.** Heart attacks can progress to cardiac arrest. Monitor the person's consciousness and breathing continuously.
7. **If the person becomes unresponsive and stops breathing normally**, begin CPR and use an AED if one is available.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Do not leave a person with suspected heart attack symptoms alone, even if they insist they are fine. Heart attack patients often minimize their symptoms. Stay with them until EMS arrives.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Requirement 7c

{{< drg/requirement number="7c" >}}
Describe the conditions that must exist before performing CPR on a person.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

CPR (cardiopulmonary resuscitation) is a powerful intervention — but it is also physically demanding for the rescuer and carries risks for the patient if used incorrectly. Knowing **when** to start is as important as knowing **how** to perform it.

### The Two Conditions for CPR

CPR is indicated when **both** of the following are true:

1. **The person is unresponsive.** They do not react to calling their name loudly or tapping their shoulder firmly.
2. **The person is not breathing normally.** They are not breathing at all, or they are only making occasional gasps (agonal breathing — a sign of cardiac arrest, not normal breathing).

### Checking for These Conditions

When you reach an unresponsive person:

1. **Tap and shout:** "Are you okay?" Tap both shoulders firmly.
2. **Call for help:** Shout for someone to call 911 and get an AED. If alone, call 911 yourself on speaker before starting CPR.
3. **Scan for breathing:** Look at the chest for 5–10 seconds. Is it rising and falling? Are there normal breath sounds? Occasional gasping does not count as normal breathing.
4. **Check for a pulse (optional for lay rescuers):** Current AHA guidelines say untrained lay rescuers should not delay CPR to look for a pulse. Trained responders may check the carotid pulse for up to 10 seconds; if unsure, begin CPR.

### When NOT to Start CPR

- The person is conscious and breathing, even if in distress — CPR is for cardiac arrest, not for someone who is awake.
- The person has a valid Do Not Resuscitate (DNR) order available.
- The scene is unsafe and you cannot make it safe.
- There are obvious signs of irreversible death (rigor mortis, dependent lividity, decapitation).

{{< drg/tip >}}
If you are ever in doubt, start CPR. The risk of performing CPR on a person who does not need it is low. The risk of not performing it on someone who does is death. When in doubt, act.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Requirement 7d

{{< drg/requirement number="7d" >}}
Demonstrate proper CPR technique using a training device approved by your counselor.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

CPR keeps oxygenated blood moving to the brain and vital organs when the heart has stopped. Current guidelines from the American Heart Association emphasize **high-quality compressions** — fast, deep, and with minimal interruption. Every second without compressions means less blood reaching the brain.

### Adult Hands-Only CPR (the most common scenario for Scouts)

{{< drg/checklist title="Adult CPR Steps" subtitle="For an unresponsive adult not breathing normally" >}}
- **Position yourself** — kneel beside the person's chest on a firm, flat surface.
- **Heel of hand placement** — place the heel of one hand on the center of the chest (lower half of the sternum). Place your second hand on top, fingers interlaced. Keep fingers off the chest.
- **Body position** — arms straight, shoulders directly over hands.
- **Compression depth** — push down at least 2 inches (5 cm) but no more than 2.4 inches (6 cm).
- **Rate** — 100–120 compressions per minute. The song "Stayin' Alive" by the Bee Gees is 103 bpm — a useful mental metronome.
- **Full recoil** — let the chest rise completely between compressions; do not lean on the chest.
- **Minimize interruptions** — pause only to check rhythm with AED, deliver breaths (if trained), or hand off to another rescuer.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

### With Rescue Breaths (Trained Rescuers: 30:2)

If you are trained in rescue breathing:
- Give 30 chest compressions.
- Open the airway with head-tilt chin-lift; pinch the nose closed.
- Give 2 breaths over 1 second each; watch for chest rise.
- Resume compressions immediately.

### Hands-Only CPR (Lay Rescuers)

For untrained or less-confident rescuers, hands-only CPR (compressions only, no rescue breaths) is effective for adults. Push hard and fast, and keep going until an AED is available or EMS takes over.

### For Children and Infants

Different techniques apply for children (age 1 to puberty) and infants (under 1 year):
- Children: 2 inches depth, one or two hands; 30:2 or 15:2 ratio
- Infants: 2 fingers on the chest, 1.5 inches depth, 30:2 or 15:2 ratio

Your counselor will specify which technique you need to demonstrate. Most Scout-level requirements use an adult manikin.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Only practice CPR on an approved training manikin. Never practice on a living person. Even correct CPR technique can cause rib fractures — this is an acceptable risk in a real cardiac arrest but not in training.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Requirement 7e

{{< drg/requirement number="7e" >}}
Explain the use of an automated external defibrillator (AED).
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

An AED is a portable electronic device that analyzes the heart's rhythm and, if a shockable rhythm is detected, delivers a controlled electrical shock to reset the heart. It is designed to be used by anyone — no medical training required.

### Why AEDs Work

Most cardiac arrests are caused by **ventricular fibrillation (VF)** — a chaotic, disorganized electrical storm in the heart's lower chambers that produces no effective pumping. The heart quivers instead of beating. An AED detects VF and delivers a defibrillation shock that briefly stuns the entire heart, giving it a chance to restart in an organized rhythm.

CPR without defibrillation can sustain a patient but rarely converts VF to a normal rhythm. Each minute without defibrillation reduces survival by approximately 10%. AED + CPR within the first few minutes of cardiac arrest dramatically improves survival.

### How to Use an AED

AEDs are intentionally simple. Every model provides voice and visual prompts that walk you through every step.

{{< drg/checklist title="AED Steps" subtitle="Follow the device's voice prompts exactly" >}}
- **Power on** — Open the case (some models power on automatically when opened).
- **Apply electrode pads** — Bare the patient's chest; dry it if wet. Attach pads exactly as shown in the pictures on the pads: one below the right collarbone, one on the left side below the armpit.
- **Analyze** — The AED will instruct you to stop touching the patient and will analyze the heart rhythm. Make sure no one is touching the patient during analysis.
- **Deliver shock (if advised)** — Ensure no one is touching the patient. Press the shock button when prompted. Say "Clear!" loudly before pressing.
- **Resume CPR** — Immediately resume compressions after the shock. Do not wait to see if the heart restarts. The AED will prompt you when to stop for re-analysis (usually 2 minutes).
- **Continue** — Follow the AED's prompts until EMS arrives or the patient recovers.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

### Special Situations

- **Wet chest:** Dry it before applying pads — water conducts electricity and reduces shock effectiveness.
- **Implanted pacemaker or defibrillator:** Avoid placing a pad directly over it; adjust pad position slightly.
- **Medication patch:** Remove any medication patches and wipe the area before applying pads.
- **Child under 8 or under 55 lbs:** Use pediatric pads if available; if not, adult pads can be used in an emergency.
- **Hairy chest:** If pads do not adhere, quickly shave the area if a razor is available, or firmly press and then rapidly remove the pad to pull away hair, then use a new pad.

## Requirement 7f

{{< drg/requirement number="7f" >}}
Demonstrate or simulate the proper use of an AED, using an AED training device if available.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This requirement puts the steps from 7e into your hands. You must physically work through the AED process, either on a training device that mimics the actual device's prompts, or by simulating the steps if no training unit is available.

### What Your Counselor Is Looking For

During this demonstration, your counselor wants to see that you can:

- Turn on the device or recognize that it is on
- Correctly identify pad placement (right sub-clavicular and left lateral chest)
- Narrate or physically demonstrate clearing everyone from the patient before analysis and before the shock
- Verbalize "Clear!" before pressing the shock button
- Return to CPR immediately after the shock without waiting to assess the patient

### Practice Points

{{< drg/checklist title="AED Demonstration Checklist" subtitle="Go through each step aloud, even if simulating" >}}
- Open case → power on
- Bare and dry the chest (say so if simulating)
- Peel and apply pad 1: right chest, below collarbone
- Peel and apply pad 2: left side, below armpit, angled
- Plug in connector if required by the device
- "Stand clear — analyzing rhythm" (ensure no one is touching the patient)
- If shock advised: say "Clear!" loudly, visually sweep to confirm no contact, press shock button
- Immediately resume CPR: 30 compressions, 2 breaths (or hands-only)
- Continue until AED re-analyzes or EMS arrives
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/tip >}}
Practice the narration out loud. Under stress, narrating your actions ("I'm applying the second pad — right here, below the left armpit — now I'm plugging in the connector") helps you stay on track and shows your counselor that you understand each step rather than just going through motions.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Requirement 7g

{{< drg/requirement number="7g" >}}
Identify the typical location(s) of one or more AED(s) at public facilities in your community, such as, your school, place of worship, unit meeting place, sports facilities, and/or camp or by using a smart phone app. Discuss the reasons for choosing locations like these.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Knowing how to use an AED is only valuable if you can find one quickly. AEDs must be reached within 3–5 minutes of a cardiac arrest for best effect — the time it takes to go find one, return, and set it up is part of the survival equation.

### Where AEDs Are Typically Located

Public AEDs are usually placed where large numbers of people gather or where there is an elevated risk of cardiac events:

- **Main hallways and lobbies** — near entrances, where they are visible and accessible without keys or codes
- **Gymnasiums, fitness centers, and sports fields** — high physical activity increases cardiac event risk
- **Cafeterias** — high density and stress
- **Main offices** — typically staffed and easy to direct emergency callers to
- **Near elevators** — in multi-story buildings
- **Scout camps** — typically at the camp health lodge or central gathering areas

### Why These Locations Matter

The two main considerations for AED placement are:

1. **Time to access:** A person experiencing cardiac arrest has the best chance of survival if defibrillation happens within 3–5 minutes. Every 30 seconds of travel time to retrieve an AED matters.
2. **Coverage of high-risk areas:** Locations where people exercise, experience emotional stress, or gather in large numbers have higher rates of cardiac events per person-hour.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Find AEDs Before You Need Them" >}}
Before your next major Scouting event, spend five minutes locating the nearest AED. Find out if it is alarmed or locked. If you are at a new camp or facility, ask the staff at check-in where the AEDs are and how to access them. This is the same logic as knowing where the fire exits are — you want to know before the emergency, not during it.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

### For Your Discussion with Your Counselor

Do not just name locations — explain the logic. "The AED in our school is by the gymnasium entrance because that is where PE classes happen and where sports practices and events take place. During a busy game, there can be hundreds of people, including older community members in the stands, and the response time from the main office would be too slow."

Requirement 7 covers the complete cardiac emergency chain: understanding the event, recognizing it early, knowing when to intervene, performing CPR with proper technique, and using an AED effectively. These are the skills that close the gap between cardiac arrest and hospital care — and in that gap, the difference between a bystander who acts and one who does not is often the difference between life and death.
