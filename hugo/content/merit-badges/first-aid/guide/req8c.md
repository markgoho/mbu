{{< drg/requirement number="8c" >}}
Describe the symptoms and signs of, and potential complications of, a sprain, a fracture, and a dislocation.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Sprains, fractures, and dislocations are the three most common structural injuries a Scout will encounter, and all three produce a similar cluster of symptoms — pain, swelling, tenderness, and reduced movement. This requirement asks you to go beyond identification and understand the **potential complications** of each injury if it is not recognized and managed properly. That is the key focus of this page.

## Sprain

A sprain is a stretch or tear of a ligament — the fibrous tissue that connects bone to bone and stabilizes joints. Ankle sprains are the most common joint injury in outdoor settings; wrist and knee sprains are also frequent.

### Symptoms and Signs

- **Pain over the joint** — localized to the joint line, not the muscle belly
- **Swelling** — appears quickly, centered on the joint; the ankle especially swells fast
- **Bruising (ecchymosis)** — may develop within hours to days as blood disperses through soft tissue
- **Tenderness** — pressing directly on the ligament reproduces pain
- **Joint instability** — the joint may feel loose or give way, especially with higher-grade sprains
- **Limited range of motion** — the person guards against movement that stresses the injured ligament
- **Difficulty bearing weight** — a sprained ankle may or may not support walking, depending on severity

### Potential Complications of an Unrecognized or Untreated Sprain

- **Chronic joint instability:** Ligaments that heal improperly or are not rehabilitated can leave the joint permanently loose. Recurrent sprains become more likely.
- **Post-traumatic arthritis:** Significant ligament damage changes joint mechanics, accelerating cartilage wear.
- **Missed fracture:** The "Ottawa Rules" exist because severe sprains and small avulsion fractures (where the ligament pulls a chip of bone away) look almost identical on examination. An ankle or wrist that does not improve within a few days, or that cannot bear any weight, may be fractured.
- **Compartment syndrome (rare):** Severe swelling in an enclosed muscle compartment can compress blood vessels and nerves. Signs: severe pain disproportionate to the injury, pain with passive stretch of the fingers or toes, a tensely swollen limb that feels "wooden." This is a surgical emergency.

{{< drg/tip >}}
In the field, treat a severe sprain like a possible fracture. Immobilize, ice, and evaluate properly. The cost of overtreating a sprain is a longer rest period; the cost of undertreating a fracture is permanent damage.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Fracture

A fracture is a complete or partial break in a bone. It may be closed (simple) — skin intact — or open (compound) — bone has penetrated the skin. Both involve broken bone; the open fracture adds infection risk to the list of dangers.

### Symptoms and Signs

- **Point tenderness** — pressing exactly on the broken bone causes sharp, focal pain; this is the most reliable physical sign of a fracture
- **Swelling and bruising** — develop quickly at the fracture site
- **Deformity** — the limb may appear angled, shortened, or rotated compared to the uninjured side
- **Crepitus** — a grating or crackling sensation (or sound) if the fracture ends move against each other; do not deliberately move the limb to elicit this
- **Loss of function** — the patient will strongly guard the injured area and may be unable to use it
- **Abnormal movement** — movement at a location where there should be none (mid-shaft of a long bone, for example)
- **Open wound (compound fracture)** — a wound near the fracture site with possible visible bone

### Potential Complications of an Unmanaged Fracture

- **Neurovascular injury:** Bone fragments can lacerate arteries or compress nerves adjacent to the fracture. Check circulation, sensation, and movement (CSM) distal to every fracture — feel for a pulse, ask if there is numbness or tingling, ask the person to move their fingers or toes. Loss of any of these is an emergency.
- **Compartment syndrome:** The same closed-space pressure emergency described for sprains — more common with fractures, especially of the tibia and forearm. Rapidly increasing pain, firmness of the limb, and pain with passive stretch of the digits are warning signs.
- **Infection (compound fractures):** An open fracture connects the broken bone directly to the outside world. Without prompt surgical debridement and antibiotics, deep bone infection (osteomyelitis) can develop and become chronic and disabling.
- **Fat embolism:** After long bone fractures (femur, tibia), fat from bone marrow can enter the bloodstream and cause respiratory distress and altered mental status. Rare but serious.
- **Malunion or nonunion:** Fractures that are not properly aligned and immobilized may heal in the wrong position (malunion) or fail to heal (nonunion), causing permanent deformity and loss of function.
- **Shock:** The femur (thigh bone) can hold 1–2 liters of blood at a fracture site. Pelvic fractures can hold far more. Fractures of major bones can cause hypovolemic shock from internal bleeding alone.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Always check for and document circulation, sensation, and movement (CSM) below any suspected fracture both before and after applying a splint or dressing. If CSM is abnormal — especially if there is no distal pulse — this is a vascular emergency requiring immediate medical intervention.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Dislocation

A dislocation is the displacement of a bone from its normal position within a joint — the joint surfaces are no longer in contact. The shoulder is the most commonly dislocated joint; the finger, kneecap, elbow, and hip also dislocate with some frequency.

### Symptoms and Signs

- **Obvious deformity** — the joint looks visibly wrong; a dislocated shoulder makes the upper arm appear to "drop" or the shoulder contour look flat
- **Severe pain** — immediate and intense at the moment of dislocation
- **Complete loss of normal joint movement** — the joint locks in an abnormal position and resists all movement
- **Swelling** — rapid; the joint capsule and surrounding structures are torn
- **Numbness, tingling, or weakness** — nerves run near most major joints and are frequently stretched or compressed during dislocation

### Potential Complications of an Unmanaged Dislocation

- **Vascular injury:** The brachial artery (near the shoulder and elbow), popliteal artery (behind the knee), and femoral artery (near the hip) are vulnerable to injury during dislocation. A pulseless limb after a dislocation is a surgical emergency.
- **Nerve injury:** The axillary nerve is commonly stretched in shoulder dislocations, causing numbness over the outer shoulder and deltoid weakness. Most nerve injuries recover with proper reduction and rest, but recovery can take months.
- **Recurrent dislocation:** Once a joint has been dislocated, the capsule and ligaments are stretched and weakened. Future dislocations occur with progressively less force, leading to chronic instability — especially in the shoulder.
- **Avulsion fracture:** The force of dislocation often pulls off a small chip of bone where the capsule or ligament attaches. This is an associated fracture, not just a soft-tissue injury.
- **Avascular necrosis (hip dislocation):** The blood supply to the femoral head (ball of the hip joint) runs close to the joint. Hip dislocation can cut off this supply, causing the bone to die — a devastating complication. This is why hip dislocations are reduced as quickly as possible in a hospital setting.
- **Damage to nearby structures:** The joint capsule, cartilage, and associated tendons are often torn during dislocation, leading to long-term instability and arthritis if not properly treated.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Do not attempt to reduce (put back in place) a dislocated joint in the field unless you are specifically trained to do so and there is no possibility of timely medical care. Incorrect reduction attempts can fracture bones, tear blood vessels, and trap nerves. Immobilize the joint in the position found and transport for definitive care.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Comparing the Three Injuries

| Feature | Sprain | Fracture | Dislocation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tissue involved | Ligament | Bone | Joint / capsule |
| Point tenderness on bone | No | **Yes** | No (tenderness over joint) |
| Obvious deformity | Rare | Common with displaced fractures | **Classic sign** |
| Skin broken | No | Only in compound fracture | No |
| Compartment syndrome risk | Low (severe) | **Moderate–High** | Low–Moderate |
| Nerve/vessel injury risk | Low | Moderate | **Moderate–High** |
| Needs imaging to confirm | Often | Yes | Yes |

When in doubt in the field, treat the injury as the most serious possibility. A suspected fracture gets splinted; a suspected dislocation gets immobilized in the position found; neither is "walked off."
