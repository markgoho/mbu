{{< drg/requirement number="2a" >}}
Obtain a copy of the Scout Annual Health and Medical Record and discuss the importance of the form including information on immunizations, allergies, medications, health history, and medical examinations to providing first aid at Scouting events.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

The best first aid is the kind you almost never have to use — because you prepared before the emergency. The Scout Annual Health and Medical Record (also called the BSA Health Form or AHMR) is one of the most powerful preparation tools a troop has. It turns scattered personal health information into a single, organized document that any trained adult can read under pressure.

This requirement asks you to actually **obtain** a copy of the form, not just know it exists. Pick one up from your unit leader, download it from the Scouting America website, or ask your counselor for a copy. Read through it before your discussion so you can speak to specifics.

### What the Form Contains

The AHMR has three parts that apply to different types of activities. For most unit campouts and events, Parts A and B are required. Part C (a full physical examination by a licensed health-care provider) is required for high-adventure and longer trips.

Regardless of which parts apply to a given event, the full form includes:

{{< drg/checklist title="Key Sections of the Scout Health Form" subtitle="Information that directly affects how you provide first aid" >}}
- **Personal information** — Name, date of birth, emergency contacts, insurance information, and primary care physician.
- **Immunization history** — Dates and types of vaccines received (tetanus/Td, MMR, varicella, hepatitis B, and others). Immunization gaps affect infection risk after wounds.
- **Allergies** — Food, medication, insect, environmental, and contact allergies; severity of past reactions; whether an epinephrine auto-injector is prescribed.
- **Current medications** — Name, dose, frequency, and what the medication treats. Certain medications affect bleeding, alertness, and response to treatment.
- **Health history** — Past surgeries, chronic conditions (asthma, diabetes, seizure disorders, heart conditions), mental health conditions, and hospitalizations.
- **Physical examination notes** — Height, weight, blood pressure, and physician comments from the most recent exam.
- **Activity restrictions** — Any activities the provider says the Scout should avoid or limit.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

### Why Each Section Matters to a First Aider

#### Immunizations

If a Scout steps on a nail at camp, knowing whether their tetanus vaccination is current affects how urgently they need medical follow-up. A wound that could wait until Monday becomes a same-day urgent care visit if the patient has not had a tetanus booster in more than five years — or if their immunization history is unknown.

#### Allergies

This section can be a matter of life and death. A Scout who is severely allergic to bee stings needs an epinephrine auto-injector at the ready. If that Scout is stung and the first aider does not know about the allergy, precious minutes may pass before the right treatment is started.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Before any Scouting event, the adult leader responsible for first aid should review the health forms of every participant and know which Scouts carry epinephrine auto-injectors, which have food allergies, and which have conditions that may require early intervention. Treat this information as confidential — share it on a need-to-know basis with adults who are supervising the Scout.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

#### Medications

A Scout who takes blood thinners (anticoagulants) for a heart condition may bleed significantly more than expected from even a minor wound. A Scout with diabetes may have altered mental status that can look like shock or a head injury. A Scout taking certain anti-seizure medications may have specific protocols if a seizure occurs. These are not edge cases — Scouts of all health backgrounds participate in BSA programs.

#### Health History

Knowing that a Scout has asthma before they start gasping on a high-elevation hike is vastly better than learning it in an emergency. Chronic conditions do not disqualify Scouts from most activities, but they change how adults plan for and respond to medical events.

#### Medical Examinations

The physician's signature and notes on Part C confirm that a trained clinician cleared the Scout for strenuous activity. Any restrictions noted by the provider should be communicated to adult leaders.

### How to Discuss the Form with Your Counselor

Come prepared to do more than recite the sections. Your counselor will want to hear you reason through it:

{{< drg/tip >}}
Try this approach: walk through a realistic scenario. "Say a Scout at camp has a severe allergic reaction to something he ate at dinner. If we have a current health form on file, we would immediately know that he carries an epinephrine auto-injector, that he is allergic to peanuts, who his parents are and how to reach them, and who his doctor is. Without the form, we are making those calls blind in the middle of an emergency." Real scenarios make your discussion concrete and memorable.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

---

Health forms are not paperwork for paperwork's sake. They are the bridge between a Scout's personal medical situation and the adults responsible for keeping them safe. A complete, current form on file means the first aider already knows critical facts before the emergency starts — and that is the most powerful preparation there is.
