{{< drg/requirement number="8d" >}}
Demonstrate bandages for these injuries:
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Bandaging for limb injuries has two goals: support the injured area and reduce swelling. The three techniques in this requirement — arm slings, elastic wraps, and cravat bandages — cover the most common bandaging needs for arm, shoulder, wrist, and ankle injuries. Each one must be demonstrated correctly, not just described. Practice each technique until you can apply it confidently on a partner.

Before any demonstration, check circulation, sensation, and movement (CSM) in the fingers or toes below the injury both before and after applying any bandage. A bandage that is too tight can cut off circulation and cause serious damage.

---

## Requirement 8d1

{{< drg/requirement number="8d1" >}}
Arm slings for forearm or upper arm or collarbone fractures
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

An arm sling suspends the injured arm against the body, using the torso and shoulder as a support structure. It reduces pain by taking the weight of the arm off the fracture site and preventing movement that could worsen the injury or damage surrounding tissue.

### When to Use a Sling

- Suspected or confirmed fracture of the forearm, wrist, or hand
- Suspected or confirmed fracture of the humerus (upper arm bone)
- Suspected or confirmed fracture of the clavicle (collarbone)
- Shoulder injuries where immobilization provides comfort

### How to Apply a Triangular Bandage Sling

A triangular bandage (cravat, or improvised from a square cloth folded diagonally) is the standard material. Commercial slings are also acceptable.

{{< drg/checklist title="Arm Sling — Step by Step" subtitle="Practice on a partner before demonstrating to your counselor" >}}
- Check CSM in the fingers before you begin (capillary refill, sensation, ability to wiggle fingers).
- Position the triangle with the point at the elbow side. The long edge runs vertically along the outside of the arm.
- Slide one end of the triangle over the uninjured shoulder and around the back of the neck.
- Bring the other end up from below the elbow, over the forearm, and tie it to the first end at the side of the neck — not on the spine (for comfort).
- The arm should be supported with the hand slightly higher than the elbow (wrist above elbow level) to reduce swelling.
- Fold or pin the excess fabric at the elbow.
- Re-check CSM after application — ensure the sling is not cutting off circulation. Fingers should be warm and pink.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

### Collarbone Fracture Note

For a clavicle fracture, the patient typically holds their arm against the body and is reluctant to raise it. Apply a sling as above, then add a **swathe** — a folded cravat or bandage wrapped around the upper arm and chest to bind the arm to the torso. This reduces movement of the shoulder girdle.

{{< drg/tip >}}
If no triangular bandage is available, improvise a sling from a long-sleeved shirt (pin the bottom hem up around the forearm), a belt, a scarf, or a jacket. Any material strong enough to support the arm and soft enough not to cut into the neck will work. BSA first aid tests improvisation skills for exactly this reason.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Requirement 8d2

{{< drg/requirement number="8d2" >}}
Elastic wrap and cravat bandages for ankle sprain
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

An ankle sprain is one of the most frequent outdoor injuries. The goal of bandaging a sprained ankle is to control swelling, provide mild support, and remind the patient not to load the joint aggressively. It does not replace rest, ice, and elevation.

### Elastic Wrap (ACE Bandage) for Ankle Sprain

The **figure-eight wrap** is the standard technique for ankle bandaging. It provides support around the ankle joint while allowing some movement.

{{< drg/checklist title="Figure-Eight Ankle Wrap — Step by Step" subtitle="Start at the foot, work upward" >}}
- Check CSM in the toes before you begin.
- Start with the roll at the ball of the foot. Make two or three anchor wraps around the foot, just above the toes.
- Angle the bandage diagonally up and across the top of the foot toward the inner ankle.
- Loop behind the ankle, cross back over the top of the foot, then loop under the outer ankle — forming the figure-eight.
- Continue the figure-eight pattern, each pass overlapping the previous by about half the bandage width.
- Extend the wrap up the lower leg (3–4 inches above the ankle) to anchor the bandage and increase support.
- Secure with clips, medical tape, or by tucking the end under the last loop.
- Re-check CSM — wiggle the toes; confirm sensation and color. If the patient reports numbness, tingling, or the toes turn blue or white, the wrap is too tight — remove and rewrap.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

### Cravat Bandage for Ankle Sprain

A cravat (folded triangular bandage) can be used as a figure-eight ankle wrap when elastic bandage is not available. Fold the triangular bandage lengthwise to the desired width. Apply with the same figure-eight pattern as the elastic wrap.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
An ankle that cannot bear any weight, has significant deformity, or has point tenderness directly on the bone may be fractured, not just sprained. Apply the bandage for comfort, but immobilize and seek medical evaluation rather than treating it as a simple sprain.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Requirement 8d3

{{< drg/requirement number="8d3" >}}
Elastic wrap and cravat bandages for wrist sprain or hand injury.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Wrist sprains and hand injuries require bandaging that protects the wrist joint and metacarpals while preserving as much finger function as possible. The wrist wrap technique differs slightly from the ankle wrap because the anatomy is different.

### Elastic Wrap for Wrist Sprain

{{< drg/checklist title="Wrist Elastic Wrap — Step by Step" subtitle="Keep the wrist in a slightly extended (neutral) position" >}}
- Check CSM in the fingers before you begin.
- Position the wrist in a neutral, slightly extended position (as if holding a can of soda). This is the position of function.
- Start at the palm: make one or two anchor wraps around the hand just below the knuckles.
- Angle the bandage across the back of the hand and around the wrist in a figure-eight pattern.
- Continue wrapping, overlapping each layer by half, working up the forearm 3–4 inches past the wrist joint.
- Leave the thumb free unless the thumb itself is injured.
- Secure with clips or tape.
- Re-check CSM — all five fingers should have normal sensation, color, and movement. The patient should be able to wiggle their fingers.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

### Cravat Bandage for Wrist or Hand

A cravat (folded triangular bandage) wrapped in a figure-eight around the hand and wrist provides moderate support and can secure a dressing over a wound. The same anatomical principles apply: anchor below the knuckles, wrap the wrist, extend up the forearm, and keep the thumb free if possible.

### For Hand Injuries with Wounds

If the hand injury involves a wound (laceration, puncture) in addition to a sprain or fracture:
1. Cover the wound with a clean dressing first.
2. Apply the elastic or cravat wrap over the dressing.
3. Do not wrap tightly enough to impair circulation — injured hands swell significantly.

{{< drg/tip >}}
When demonstrating bandages to your counselor, narrate every step. "I'm starting at the palm with an anchor wrap — I'm keeping the wrist slightly extended — I'm checking that the fingers are free and I can see their color." Narrating shows that your technique is deliberate, not just imitation.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

Bandaging is a hands-on skill — reading about technique is useful, but proficiency comes only from practice. Before your counselor meeting, apply each of these bandages multiple times on a partner and ask them to report honestly on comfort and CSM. A bandage that the patient says is cutting off their circulation or feels loose is not ready for demonstration.
