{{< drg/requirement number="8e" >}}
Demonstrate the proper procedures for handling and splinting of suspected closed or open fractures or dislocations of the:
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Splinting immobilizes an injured bone or joint to prevent movement that could worsen the injury, damage blood vessels and nerves, or increase pain. It is one of the most important field first-aid skills for outdoor emergencies because fractures and dislocations happen at camp, on trails, and during high-adventure activities — often far from professional medical care.

**The golden rule of splinting:** Splint the injury as you find it. Do not attempt to straighten an angulated fracture or reduce a dislocation in the field (unless trained and there is no pulse distal to the injury). Immobilize in the position of comfort.

**Before and after every splint application, check and document CSM:**
- **Circulation:** Is there a distal pulse? Is the skin warm and pink?
- **Sensation:** Does the patient feel normal touch below the injury?
- **Movement:** Can they wiggle their fingers or toes?

Checking CSM both before and after application shows your counselor that you understand the risk of creating or worsening a neurovascular injury.

## Requirement 8e1

{{< drg/requirement number="8e1" >}}
Finger and toe
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Finger and toe fractures or dislocations are among the most common injuries in sport and outdoor activity. The preferred field technique is **buddy taping** — securing the injured digit to an adjacent uninjured one, which acts as a natural splint.

### When to Buddy Tape

- Suspected fracture of a finger or toe (pain, swelling, tenderness on the bone, difficulty moving)
- Suspected dislocation of a finger joint that is stable (the joint is displaced but there is no open wound and circulation is intact)

### Buddy Taping — Step by Step

{{< drg/checklist title="Buddy Taping a Finger or Toe" subtitle="Use medical tape, cloth tape, or improvised strips of fabric" >}}
- Check CSM in the injured digit before you begin.
- If there is a wound, cover it with a clean dressing before taping.
- Place a small pad (folded gauze, foam) between the injured digit and the buddy digit to prevent skin maceration.
- Tape the injured digit to the adjacent digit at two points: one tape strip above the injured joint and one below — never directly over the joint.
- Tape snugly but not so tightly that circulation is cut off.
- Re-check CSM after application — the fingernail or toenail should return to pink color within 2 seconds of pressing.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

### When Not to Buddy Tape

- Open fracture (bone through skin) — cover the wound with a clean dressing, do not apply pressure, and seek immediate medical care.
- Severe deformity with loss of CSM — buddy taping alone is insufficient; the patient needs urgent medical care.

{{< drg/tip >}}
For a dislocated finger, do not attempt to pull it back into position in the field unless you have received specific training. Splint it in the position found with buddy taping or a padded rigid splint alongside the finger, and have the patient evaluated medically.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Requirement 8e2

{{< drg/requirement number="8e2" >}}
Forearm or wrist
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Forearm and wrist fractures are common after falls on an outstretched hand (a reflex reaction that concentrates the impact in the most breakable area). The goal is to immobilize the wrist joint and both forearm bones (radius and ulna) in a neutral position of function.

### Position of Function

The wrist should be splinted in a **slightly extended, neutral position** — as though the hand is about to shake hands or hold a glass. This preserves the best functional position if swelling causes stiffness during healing.

### Rigid Splint for Forearm or Wrist — Step by Step

{{< drg/checklist title="Forearm Splint Application" subtitle="A rigid splint plus padding plus securing wrap" >}}
- Check CSM in all five fingers before you begin.
- Gather a rigid splint material: SAM splint (moldable aluminum), rolled magazine or newspaper, sticks from the campsite, or any firm flat object long enough to span from the palm to mid-forearm.
- Pad the splint material (folded cloth, extra bandage) to prevent pressure points.
- Position the arm with wrist slightly extended, thumb pointing upward.
- Place the padded splint along the palm side of the forearm, extending from the palm to 2–3 inches past the wrist.
- Secure with elastic wrap, cravat bandages, or improvised strips of cloth — tie above the wrist, at the wrist, and at mid-forearm. Never tie directly over the fracture site.
- Apply an arm sling to support the weight of the splinted arm against the body.
- Re-check CSM — confirm all five fingers have sensation and normal color; the patient can wiggle their fingers.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
For an open (compound) forearm fracture: cover the wound and any visible bone with a clean dressing. Do not push bone back. Apply a padded splint loosely around the wound and arrange immediate medical transport. Do not attempt to clean or probe the wound.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Requirement 8e3

{{< drg/requirement number="8e3" >}}
Upper leg
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A suspected femur (thigh bone) fracture is one of the most serious limb injuries because:

1. The femur is the largest, strongest bone in the body — it takes significant force to break it.
2. A femoral fracture can cause internal bleeding of 1–2 liters into the thigh, potentially causing hemorrhagic shock.
3. Major blood vessels and the sciatic nerve run alongside the femur.

Upper leg fractures demand rapid stabilization and urgent medical evacuation.

### Signs Specific to Femur Fracture

- Severe pain in the mid-thigh or groin area
- The leg may be shortened and externally rotated (rolled outward)
- Rapid swelling of the thigh
- Signs of shock (pale, cool, clammy skin; rapid weak pulse; altered mental status) due to internal bleeding

### Upper Leg Splint — Step by Step

In a field setting without a traction splint, the goal is to immobilize the hip, knee, and entire femur:

{{< drg/checklist title="Upper Leg Splint for Femur Fracture" subtitle="Use long rigid materials that extend from groin to foot" >}}
- Check CSM in the foot and ankle before you begin (dorsalis pedis pulse, sensation in the foot, ability to wiggle toes).
- Do not attempt to straighten the leg if it is angulated — splint in position found.
- Assemble splint materials: two long rigid supports (padded boards, tent poles, trekking poles) — one for the inner leg, one for the outer leg. Outer splint must reach from the armpit to beyond the foot; inner splint from the groin to beyond the foot.
- Pad around any bony prominences (hip, knee, ankle).
- Slide securing materials (cravats, strips of cloth) under the leg at several intervals before positioning the splints.
- Position the padded splints alongside the leg.
- Tie securing strips at mid-thigh, above the knee, below the knee, at the lower leg, and above the ankle. Never tie over the fracture site.
- Tie the feet together to prevent rotation.
- Re-check CSM after securing.
- Treat for shock: keep the patient warm, monitor vitals, and activate emergency evacuation.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
A suspected femur fracture is a priority evacuation — not a "wait and see" situation. Even if the patient seems stable, internal bleeding into the thigh can worsen rapidly. Call for emergency assistance and keep the patient as still as possible during transport.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Requirement 8e4

{{< drg/requirement number="8e4" >}}
Lower leg or ankle
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Lower leg fractures (tibia and fibula) and ankle fractures or dislocations are common results of falls, trips, and twisting injuries. Compartment syndrome — a dangerous pressure buildup in the leg's closed muscle compartments — is a recognized complication of tibial fractures.

### Signs of Lower Leg Fracture or Ankle Fracture/Dislocation

- Pain and point tenderness over the bone
- Swelling, bruising
- Deformity of the lower leg or ankle (ankle fractures often produce dramatic deformity)
- Inability to bear weight
- Crepitus with any movement

### Lower Leg Splint — Step by Step

{{< drg/checklist title="Lower Leg and Ankle Splint" subtitle="Immobilize from mid-thigh to the foot" >}}
- Check CSM in the foot before you begin: dorsalis pedis pulse (top of the foot), sensation (ask if the toes feel normal), movement (wiggle toes).
- Do not move the ankle if it is dislocated — splint in position found.
- Pad a rigid splint (padded board, SAM splint, rolled foam pad, improvised from camping gear) to cover from mid-thigh to several inches beyond the foot.
- For ankle splint with a SAM splint or improvised material: form a U-shape (stirrup) under the heel and up both sides of the lower leg, or use a single posterior (back of leg) splint.
- Secure with elastic wrap, cravats, or cloth strips — tie above the knee, mid-lower leg, and above the ankle.
- Keep the ankle at 90 degrees (foot perpendicular to the leg — neutral position) if possible without forcing a deformity.
- Tie or pad the foot to prevent it from dropping.
- Re-check CSM after securing. Confirm the posterior tibial and dorsalis pedis pulses are present and toes are warm.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Watch for compartment syndrome after any lower leg fracture: increasing pain despite splinting, severe tightness in the leg, pain when passively stretching the toes, and loss of CSM. These are surgical emergencies — a tight splint must be loosened and emergency evacuation initiated immediately.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

{{< drg/tip >}}
When demonstrating any splint for your counselor, talk through your CSM check before and after. "Before I apply this splint, I'm checking — I can feel the dorsal foot pulse, the patient reports normal sensation in all toes, and they can wiggle their toes. After application — pulse is still present, sensation unchanged, toes are warm and pink." This is exactly what a trained rescuer does and it is exactly what demonstrates competence to a counselor.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

All four splinting techniques in this requirement share the same core logic: immobilize the injury and the joints above and below it, pad to protect bony prominences and open wounds, secure without cutting off circulation, and document CSM before and after. The specific materials and securing points change by location, but the principles are the same. Master the process, and you can improvise when the ideal equipment is not available — which is often the case in the field.
