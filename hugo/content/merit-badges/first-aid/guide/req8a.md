{{< drg/requirement number="8a" >}}
Explain the similarities and differences in a strain, a muscle tear, a tendon rupture, a sprain, a dislocation, a simple fracture, and a compound fracture.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Muscle and bone injuries are among the most common emergencies a Scout will encounter on the trail, at camp, or during activity. Before you can treat any of these injuries, you need to understand what each one is — because they share many of the same symptoms (pain, swelling, limited movement) while involving very different tissues and carrying very different risks. This page explains the seven injury types you need to know and how to tell them apart.

## The Seven Injury Types

### Strain

A **strain** is a stretch or minor tearing of muscle fibers or the tendons that attach muscle to bone. Strains happen when a muscle is overloaded — pulled beyond its capacity, usually by sudden movement or excessive force.

**Common causes:** Lifting something too heavy, a sudden sprint or jump, poor warm-up before exercise.

**What is damaged:** Muscle fibers and/or their tendon attachments — mild to moderate disruption of the tissue.

**Symptoms:** Local pain and tenderness, muscle spasm or cramping, swelling, limited range of motion, possible bruising after 24–48 hours.

---

### Muscle Tear

A **muscle tear** (also called a muscle strain Grade II–III) is a more severe injury than a mild strain — actual muscle fibers are significantly or completely torn. In a complete rupture, the muscle belly itself is split in two.

**Common causes:** High-force activities, explosive movement, a direct blow to a tensed muscle.

**What is damaged:** A significant portion (partial tear) or the full cross-section (complete tear) of the muscle.

**Symptoms:** Sudden, sharp pain at the moment of injury; immediate loss of strength; rapid swelling and bruising; a palpable gap or "bunching" of muscle tissue in complete tears; marked inability to use the affected area.

**Key difference from strain:** A strain is a stretch with minimal fiber disruption; a tear involves significant structural failure of the muscle itself.

---

### Tendon Rupture

A **tendon rupture** is a partial or complete tear of a tendon — the tough, cord-like connective tissue that attaches muscle to bone. Tendons transmit the force generated by muscles to move the skeleton.

**Common causes:** Sudden explosive force (jumping, sprinting, changing direction quickly), chronic overuse weakening the tendon, or direct trauma.

**What is damaged:** The tendon itself, not the muscle belly. The Achilles tendon (back of the heel) and the patellar tendon (kneecap) are common sites.

**Symptoms:** A sudden "snap" or "pop" sensation and sound at the moment of rupture; immediate severe pain; the ability to feel a gap in the tendon; dramatic loss of function (a person with a ruptured Achilles cannot push off with that foot).

**Key difference from muscle tear:** A tendon rupture affects the cord connecting muscle to bone, not the muscle itself. The "snap" sensation and complete functional loss in one specific movement are telltale signs.

---

### Sprain

A **sprain** is a stretch or tear of a **ligament** — the fibrous tissue that connects bone to bone and stabilizes joints. Sprains happen when a joint is forced beyond its normal range of motion.

**Common causes:** Rolling an ankle, falling on an outstretched hand, twisting a knee.

**What is damaged:** Ligaments around a joint — not muscle tissue.

**Symptoms:** Pain and tenderness over the joint (not over muscle belly), swelling, bruising, joint instability or looseness, limited range of motion.

**Key difference from strain:** A strain involves muscle or tendon; a sprain involves ligament. Strains typically cause pain along the muscle belly or where it meets the tendon. Sprains cause pain directly over a joint.

---

### Dislocation

A **dislocation** is the displacement of a bone from its normal position within a joint — the joint surfaces are no longer in contact with each other. It is a structural disruption of the joint itself.

**Common causes:** A fall onto an outstretched hand (shoulder dislocation), a direct blow to a joint, extreme force applied to a limb.

**What is damaged:** The joint capsule, surrounding ligaments, and sometimes blood vessels or nerves near the joint. The bones themselves are not necessarily broken.

**Symptoms:** Obvious deformity of the joint (it looks "wrong"); severe pain; complete inability to move the joint normally; swelling; numbness or tingling if nerves are involved.

**Key difference from fracture:** A dislocation is a joint problem — the bones are in the wrong position relative to each other but not broken. A fracture is a break in the bone itself. (However, fractures and dislocations can occur together.)

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Never attempt to put a dislocated joint back into place in the field unless you have specific training to do so. Incorrect reduction attempts can damage blood vessels, nerves, and surrounding soft tissue. Immobilize the joint in the position found and transport for medical care.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

---

### Simple (Closed) Fracture

A **simple fracture** (also called a closed fracture) is a complete or partial break in a bone where the skin remains intact. The bone is broken but there is no external wound.

**Common causes:** Falls, direct impact, overuse (stress fractures), twisting forces.

**What is damaged:** The bone itself — it may be cracked, broken into two pieces, or shattered (comminuted). Surrounding soft tissue may also be damaged.

**Symptoms:** Pain and point tenderness directly over the bone; swelling; bruising; deformity (abnormal angle or shortening of a limb); crepitus (a grinding or crackling sensation if the ends move); inability or extreme difficulty using the injured area.

---

### Compound (Open) Fracture

A **compound fracture** (also called an open fracture) is a bone break in which the bone has penetrated or is protruding through the skin, creating an open wound that connects to the fracture site.

**What is damaged:** The bone plus the overlying skin and soft tissue. The open wound creates a direct path for bacteria to reach the bone, making infection a serious risk.

**Symptoms:** All signs of a simple fracture, plus an open wound near the injury site. Bone may or may not be visible. There is often significant bleeding.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
A compound fracture is a more serious emergency than a simple fracture because of the high risk of infection and major blood loss. Cover the wound with a clean dressing, control bleeding, immobilize the limb, and call for advanced medical care. Do not attempt to push bone back under the skin or clean the wound deeply.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## How to Tell Them Apart: A Summary

| Injury | Tissue Involved | Skin Intact | Typical Location of Pain | Key Feature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Strain | Muscle / tendon attachment | Yes | Along muscle belly or muscle-tendon junction | Weakness, spasm |
| Muscle tear | Muscle fibers | Yes | Along muscle belly | Palpable gap; severe strength loss |
| Tendon rupture | Tendon | Yes | Along tendon (e.g., back of heel) | "Pop" sound; complete loss of one movement |
| Sprain | Ligament | Yes | Over a joint | Joint instability |
| Dislocation | Joint (displaced bones) | Yes | Over a joint | Obvious deformity; joint looks wrong |
| Simple fracture | Bone | Yes | Over bone | Point tenderness on bone; deformity |
| Compound fracture | Bone + skin | **No** | Over bone + wound | Open wound; bone may protrude |

In the field, you often cannot make a definitive diagnosis — only imaging can confirm a fracture versus a severe sprain. The practical takeaway: **treat an uncertain injury as the more serious option.** If you are not sure whether an injury is a sprain or a fracture, treat it as a fracture until proven otherwise.
