First aid is what you do in the critical moments before a doctor, paramedic, or rescue team takes over. A Scout who knows first aid can slow bleeding, protect an airway, calm a frightened patient, and keep a bad situation from getting worse. This merit badge teaches you how to notice what matters, act with purpose, and care for people at home, on the trail, at camp, and in your community.

## Then and Now

### Then — Neighbor-to-Neighbor Care

Long before ambulances carried advanced equipment, first aid depended on whoever was nearby. Families, neighbors, soldiers, sailors, and outdoorspeople learned practical skills like stopping bleeding, splinting broken bones, and carrying injured people to safety because help might be hours away. In the early 1900s, organized first-aid instruction spread through groups like the American Red Cross, hospitals, and civic organizations, turning life-saving knowledge into something ordinary people could learn instead of leaving it only to doctors.

Those early lessons focused on being ready, staying calm, and doing the most good with limited tools. That is still the heart of first aid today.

### Now — Fast Decisions, Better Outcomes

Modern first aid works alongside 911 systems, emergency departments, AEDs, epinephrine auto-injectors, and wilderness rescue teams. But even with all that support, the first few minutes still matter most. A responder who recognizes shock early, cools a heat illness quickly, protects the spine after a fall, or calls for help with clear information can change the outcome.

Today, first aid is also broader than cuts and broken bones. Scouts learn about mental health crises, environmental exposure, allergic reactions, and how to prevent emergencies before they happen. Good first aid is not just treatment. It is awareness, prevention, communication, and leadership.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Many emergency response systems talk about the "chain of survival" or "chain of care." That chain begins long before the ambulance arrives — with the person on scene who notices the problem, calls for help, and starts the right first-aid steps.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Get Ready!

You do not need to be fearless to be a good first-aid responder. You need to be observant, prepared, and willing to take the next right step. As you work through this guide, picture real situations: a fall on a hike, a swimmer in trouble, a bee sting at camp, a friend who suddenly feels sick. The more clearly you can imagine the scene, the more confidently you will respond.

## Kinds of First Aid

First aid is not one skill. It is a whole toolkit of ways to keep someone safe until more help arrives.

### Emergency Scene First Aid

This is the first response at the scene of an accident or illness. You size up the area, protect yourself, figure out what happened, check the patient, and decide what needs to happen next. Scene first aid is about priorities: danger first, life threats next, details after that.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Never rush straight to the patient without checking the scene. Traffic, fire, unstable trees, electrical hazards, ice, chemicals, or a panicked crowd can turn one victim into two. Your first job is to avoid becoming part of the emergency.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Everyday Home and Community First Aid

A lot of first aid happens in ordinary places: kitchens, playgrounds, sports fields, schools, parking lots, and meeting rooms. These emergencies may look smaller than wilderness accidents, but the same principles apply. Notice the problem, keep the patient safe, use simple treatment, and know when to call for professional help.

### Outdoor and Wilderness First Aid

In the outdoors, help may be delayed and evacuation may take time. That changes the way you think. You still handle life-threatening problems first, but you also think about weather, terrain, hydration, warmth, communication, and whether the group can move safely. Wilderness first aid depends heavily on prevention and good judgment.

### Environmental and Activity-Related First Aid

Scouts hike, swim, climb, camp in hot weather, and camp in cold weather. That means first aid includes heat illness, hypothermia, altitude sickness, bites, stings, sun exposure, and injuries caused by activity or equipment. These situations often build slowly, which makes early recognition especially important.

### Psychological First Aid and Crisis Response

Not every emergency bleeds. Some involve panic, overwhelming stress, disorientation, or signs that a person may be a danger to themselves or others. Good first aid includes listening, reducing immediate danger, staying with the person, and getting help from a trusted adult or emergency services when needed.

{{< drg/tip >}}
When you study first aid, ask yourself the same three questions every time: What is dangerous right now? What problem matters most? What can I do safely with the tools and training I have? Those questions keep you focused under stress.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

First aid starts with the first few minutes of every emergency: checking the scene, sizing up the patient, and making smart decisions under pressure.
