Using checklists provided in the First Aid merit badge pamphlet or ones approved by your counselor, do the following:
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Requirement 2b has two distinct actions: **assemble** a personal kit and **demonstrate** every item in it (2b1), then **inspect** a larger kit and **discuss** what you find (2b2). The first task proves you can build and explain a kit from scratch. The second task proves you know what a well-maintained, properly stocked kit looks like — and what to do when one falls short of that standard. Work through them in order since your personal kit knowledge from 2b1 will help you evaluate the larger kit in 2b2.

## Requirement 2b1

{{< drg/requirement number="2b1" >}}
Assemble a personal first-aid kit for hiking and backpacking. Demonstrate the proper use of each item in your first-aid kit to your counselor.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A personal first-aid kit for hiking and backpacking must balance two competing demands: it needs to cover the injuries most likely to happen on the trail, and it needs to be light and compact enough that you will actually carry it. A kit that stays in your pack because it is too heavy defeats its entire purpose.

### What Goes In a Personal Trail Kit

Use the official BSA Personal, Family, and Troop First Aid Kit Checklist as your starting point. A solid personal kit for hiking typically includes:

{{< drg/checklist title="Personal Hiking & Backpacking First-Aid Kit" subtitle="Build from this list — your counselor may approve additions or substitutions" >}}
- **Adhesive bandages** — Assorted sizes (small, medium, large, and knuckle); cover minor cuts and abrasions.
- **Sterile gauze pads** — At least two 3×3 or 4×4 inch pads; for covering and absorbing blood from larger wounds.
- **Rolled gauze (conforming bandage)** — Holds gauze pads in place and can secure splints.
- **Medical adhesive tape** — Holds dressings; can also be used to prevent blisters if applied before a hot spot develops.
- **Elastic (ACE-style) bandage** — For compressing and supporting sprained ankles and knees.
- **Antiseptic wipes or solution** — For cleaning wounds before dressing; reduces infection risk.
- **Antibiotic ointment** — Apply to cleaned wounds before covering; slows bacterial growth.
- **Blister treatment** — Moleskin, blister pads, or a gel blister bandage; do not wait until a blister fully forms.
- **Non-latex disposable gloves** — At least two pairs; wear whenever you contact body fluids.
- **CPR face shield or pocket mask** — Creates a barrier during rescue breathing.
- **Tweezers** — For removing splinters, thorns, or tick mouthparts.
- **Small scissors or trauma shears** — For cutting tape, clothing, or bandages.
- **Safety pins** — Can secure a sling, close a torn bandage, or improvise other uses.
- **Instant cold pack** — For reducing swelling on sprains and bruises; useful where ice is unavailable.
- **First-aid instruction card or booklet** — A reference you can check under stress.
- **Personal medications** — Any prescription medications you carry (epinephrine auto-injector, inhaler, etc.), clearly labeled.
- **Small pen and notepad** — For recording times of injury, treatment given, and patient information.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/tip >}}
Pack your kit in a waterproof stuff sack or a brightly colored zip pouch. Waterproof keeps everything usable even if your pack gets caught in the rain or dropped in a stream. Bright color means you can find it instantly under pressure — not after digging through your entire pack while someone is hurt.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### How to Demonstrate Each Item

"Demonstrating" for your counselor means more than pulling each item out and naming it. For every item, explain:

1. **What it is** — Name the item and any variations (e.g., "This is a 3×3 sterile gauze pad; it comes in other sizes too").
2. **What it is used for** — What type of injury or situation calls for it.
3. **How to use it correctly** — Walk through the steps. Physically show the technique on yourself or a practice partner where appropriate.
4. **What to watch out for** — Common mistakes, when not to use it, or signs that the treatment is working or not.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Practice Demonstrations for Counselor Sign-Off" >}}
- **Adhesive bandage** — Clean the wound first; dry the skin; apply without touching the sterile pad; press edges firmly.
- **Gauze pad with tape** — Center the pad over the wound; secure all four edges with tape; do not tape so tightly that circulation is impaired.
- **Elastic bandage** — Start below the injury and wrap upward in overlapping figure-eights; check capillary refill after application.
- **Moleskin / blister pad** — Identify the hot spot before it blisters; cut the moleskin into a donut shape with the hot spot at the center; press firmly.
- **Gloves** — Don before any patient contact; remove using the inside-out technique (see Req 1c); dispose of properly.
- **Tweezers** — Grasp a splinter as close to the skin as possible; pull in the direction it entered; clean the site afterward.
- **Instant cold pack** — Activate by squeezing to break the inner bag; wrap in a cloth before applying to skin (never place directly on bare skin for extended periods — risk of ice burn).
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

### Keeping the Kit Ready

A first-aid kit that has been raided for band-aids and never restocked is not a first-aid kit — it is a box. After every trip:

- Replace any items used, expired, or damaged.
- Check that sealed packages are intact (no tears, no moisture inside).
- Confirm that personal medications (if carried) have not expired.
- Return the kit to your pack before the next trip.

## Requirement 2b2

{{< drg/requirement number="2b2" >}}
With your counselor, inspect a unit, home, vehicle, or camp first-aid kit and discuss your findings.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Inspecting a kit is a systematic process — not a quick glance inside the bag. A thorough inspection checks whether the kit has everything it should have, whether everything in it is in usable condition, and whether the kit is stored where it can actually be reached when needed.

### How to Conduct the Inspection

Work through the kit methodically, item by item. Do not just look at what is there — look for what is missing and what should not be there.

{{< drg/checklist title="First-Aid Kit Inspection Checklist" subtitle="Use this alongside the official BSA kit checklist for the kit type you are inspecting" >}}
- **Completeness** — Compare contents against a standard checklist for that kit type (unit, home, vehicle, or camp). Note any missing items.
- **Expiration dates** — Check every item that has one: antiseptic wipes, ointments, medications, instant cold packs, and some bandages.
- **Packaging integrity** — Look for tears, punctures, water damage, or resealed packages. A sterile dressing that has been opened is no longer sterile — replace it.
- **Quantity** — Are there enough of the commonly used items (adhesive bandages, gloves, gauze) for the expected number of users?
- **Organization** — Can you find items quickly? Is the kit organized so that the most-used items are accessible without digging?
- **Storage location** — Is the kit in a place that is known to everyone who might need it, protected from extremes of heat and cold, and not locked away when unlocking it in an emergency would cause a deadly delay?
- **Kit size appropriateness** — Does the kit match the setting? A personal hiking kit should not be expected to serve an entire troop campout.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

### Discussing Your Findings

Your counselor will want more than a list of what was there. Be ready to talk through:

- **What was correct** — What items were present, in-date, and well-organized? This part matters too; it shows you understand what a good kit looks like, not just what a bad one looks like.
- **What was deficient** — Items missing, expired, opened without replacement, or in poor condition. Explain not just what was wrong, but why it matters. ("The only pair of gloves in the kit was torn. That means the first aider would have to make direct contact with the patient's blood or choose not to treat them.")
- **What you would recommend** — If you were in charge of maintaining this kit, what would you do next? Restock specific items? Relocate the kit? Reorganize the layout?

{{< drg/tip >}}
Different kit types have different standards. A vehicle first-aid kit lives in a hot car and may need more frequent expiration checks than a kit stored in a cool, dry cabinet at home. A camp kit serving 50 Scouts needs far more supplies than a personal day-hike kit. When discussing your findings, account for the specific context of the kit you inspected.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
After the inspection, make sure the kit is restocked and returned to its proper location before you finish your session. An incomplete kit left open or out of place after an inspection has failed its purpose — someone may need it before it is fixed.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### The Bigger Picture

Requirement 2b teaches a habit that goes far beyond this merit badge: checking your gear before you need it. A Scout who regularly inspects a first-aid kit — notices the expired antiseptic, replaces the gloves that got used last campout, adds a blister kit after a hike with hot spots — is building a practice of preparedness that will serve them for the rest of their life.

The goal of first aid preparation is to make sure that when an emergency happens, your kit is ready, your knowledge is ready, and you are ready. Requirements 2a and 2b are how you get there.
