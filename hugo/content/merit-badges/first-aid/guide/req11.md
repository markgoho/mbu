{{< drg/requirement number="11" >}}
Heat- and Cold-Related Conditions. Describe the symptoms and signs of, show first aid for, and explain prevention of these conditions associated with exertion and/or heat or cold exposure:
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

The body works hard to keep its core temperature in a narrow range. Push it too far in either direction — through exertion, environment, or improper hydration — and the results range from painful muscle cramps to life-threatening emergencies. Each condition in this requirement represents a step on that spectrum. Work through the dehydration and fluid balance topics first (11a), then follow the heat-illness progression from cramps (11b) through exhaustion (11c) to heat stroke (11d), before tackling the cold-exposure emergencies in 11e and 11f.

## Requirement 11a

{{< drg/requirement number="11a" >}}
Dehydration and over-hydration
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Water is the foundation of every other function in this requirement. Get fluid balance wrong and you set the stage for cramps, heat illness, or — in rare cases — a dangerous drop in blood sodium.

### Dehydration

**Describe — Symptoms and Signs:**
- Thirst (a late sign — you are already mildly dehydrated before you feel thirsty)
- Dark yellow or amber urine
- Headache, dizziness, or lightheadedness
- Muscle cramps
- Fatigue and reduced physical performance
- Dry mouth, decreased urine output
- In severe cases: confusion, rapid heartbeat, sunken eyes, no tears

**Show — First Aid for Dehydration:**
1. Move the person to shade or a cool area and have them rest.
2. Give cool water or an electrolyte drink in small sips. Do not force large amounts rapidly.
3. For mild to moderate dehydration, oral rehydration is usually sufficient.
4. If the person is confused, unresponsive, or cannot drink safely, call 911.
5. Monitor urine output — pale yellow urine indicates good rehydration.

**Explain — Prevention:**
- Drink before you are thirsty — drink consistently throughout the day, especially before and during activity.
- Aim for pale yellow urine as a hydration guide.
- Increase fluid intake in hot weather, at altitude, or when exercising vigorously.
- Eat foods with natural water content (fruits, vegetables) on long hikes or campouts.

### Over-Hydration (Hyponatremia)

**Describe — Symptoms and Signs:**

Over-hydration (drinking more water than the kidneys can process) dilutes sodium in the blood, causing hyponatremia — a dangerous condition sometimes seen in endurance athletes or hikers who drink large amounts of plain water without taking in electrolytes.

- Nausea, vomiting
- Headache
- Confusion or altered mental status
- Muscle weakness or cramping
- In severe cases: seizures, loss of consciousness

**Show — First Aid for Over-Hydration:**
1. Stop fluid intake immediately.
2. If the person is confused or has a seizure, call 911 — this is a medical emergency.
3. Do not give plain water. If the person is alert and a doctor recommends it, small amounts of a salty snack or electrolyte solution may help.
4. Keep the person calm and still.

**Explain — Prevention:**
- Do not drink more than about one liter of water per hour during strenuous activity.
- Replace electrolytes (sodium especially) during extended exercise — sports drinks, salty snacks, or electrolyte tablets.
- Recognize that "drink as much water as possible" is not always the right advice.

## Requirement 11b

{{< drg/requirement number="11b" >}}
Heat cramps and muscle pain after exertion
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

### Heat Cramps

**Describe — Symptoms and Signs:**
- Painful muscle spasms, usually in the legs, arms, or abdomen
- Occurs during or after heavy exercise in heat
- The skin may be moist and cool
- No fever or mental confusion (those point to a more serious condition)

**Show — First Aid for Heat Cramps:**
1. Move to a cool area and rest.
2. Gently stretch and massage the cramping muscle.
3. Drink water or a sports drink with electrolytes — avoid plain water only, as sodium loss contributes to cramps.
4. Rest until cramping stops before resuming activity.
5. If cramps persist or spread, the person develops nausea, or they seem disoriented, suspect heat exhaustion and treat accordingly.

**Explain — Prevention:**
- Acclimatize to heat gradually over 7–14 days before intense activity.
- Stay well hydrated with electrolyte-containing fluids during exercise.
- Eat salty foods or carry salt tablets for extended activity in heat.

### Exertional Muscle Damage (Rhabdomyolysis)

Severe, prolonged overexertion can cause muscle fibers to break down and release proteins into the bloodstream. Symptoms include extreme muscle pain, weakness, dark brown or cola-colored urine, and decreased urine output. This is a medical emergency — call 911 and stop all activity immediately.

## Requirement 11c

{{< drg/requirement number="11c" >}}
Heat exhaustion
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Heat exhaustion is a serious heat-related illness. The body's cooling system is overwhelmed, but core temperature is not yet critically elevated. Without quick treatment, heat exhaustion can progress to heat stroke.

**Describe — Symptoms and Signs:**
- Heavy sweating
- Cool, pale, moist skin
- Rapid, weak pulse
- Nausea or vomiting
- Muscle cramps
- Tiredness, weakness
- Dizziness or fainting
- Headache
- Temperature up to 104°F (40°C)
- The person is still conscious and able to follow commands

**Show — First Aid for Heat Exhaustion:**
1. Move the person to a cool location — shade, air-conditioned building, or near a fan.
2. Loosen or remove tight clothing.
3. Apply cool, wet cloths to the skin, particularly the neck, armpits, and groin (where large blood vessels are close to the surface).
4. Have the person drink cool water or an electrolyte drink — small sips if nauseated.
5. Have the person lie down with their legs slightly elevated to help blood flow to the brain.
6. Monitor closely. If symptoms do not improve within 15 minutes, or if the person loses consciousness, vomits repeatedly, or seems confused, call 911 immediately — this may be heat stroke.

**Explain — Prevention:**
- Drink fluids consistently before and during activity in the heat.
- Take rest breaks in shade.
- Wear light, loose, light-colored clothing.
- Avoid strenuous activity during the hottest part of the day (10 a.m.–4 p.m.).
- Acclimatize gradually to hot environments.

## Requirement 11d

{{< drg/requirement number="11d" >}}
Heat stroke
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Heat stroke is a life-threatening emergency. The body's cooling mechanism has failed completely, and the core temperature has risen to a dangerous level.

**Describe — Symptoms and Signs:**

Heat stroke has two forms:

- **Classic heat stroke** (non-exertional): Often affects elderly or very young individuals in hot environments. Skin may be **hot and dry** because sweating has stopped.
- **Exertional heat stroke**: Occurs in healthy, active individuals pushing hard in heat. Skin may still be **sweaty**.

Both forms share:
- Core body temperature above 104°F (40°C)
- Altered mental status — confusion, agitation, slurred speech, seizures, or unconsciousness
- Rapid, strong pulse progressing to weak pulse
- Possible nausea and vomiting

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Heat stroke is a 911 emergency. Call immediately and begin cooling aggressively while waiting for help. Every minute the core temperature stays elevated increases the risk of permanent brain damage, organ failure, and death.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

**Show — First Aid for Heat Stroke:**
1. **Call 911.**
2. Move the person to the coolest available location.
3. **Cool aggressively and rapidly.** The most effective method available:
   - Immerse in cold water or an ice bath (if available and the person is conscious)
   - Apply ice packs to neck, armpits, and groin
   - Spray with cool water and fan vigorously
   - Cover with cool, wet sheets
4. Do not give fluids by mouth if the person is confused or unconscious.
5. Continue cooling until emergency personnel arrive or core temperature drops to 101°F (38.3°C).

**Explain — Prevention:**

Same as heat exhaustion prevention, with added emphasis:
- Never leave a person or pet in a closed vehicle in warm weather.
- Recognize that certain medications (diuretics, antihistamines, antipsychotics) and alcohol increase heat stroke risk.
- Buddy system: watch each other for early signs of heat illness.

## Requirement 11e

{{< drg/requirement number="11e" >}}
Chest pains associated with cold exposure
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Cold temperatures, combined with physical exertion like shoveling snow or hiking in winter, can trigger a cardiac event in people with or without known heart disease. Cold causes blood vessels to constrict, the heart to work harder, and — in susceptible individuals — can precipitate a heart attack.

**Describe — Symptoms and Signs:**
- Chest pain, pressure, tightness, or squeezing — often described as "an elephant sitting on the chest"
- Pain that radiates to the left arm, jaw, neck, or back
- Shortness of breath
- Sweating, nausea, or lightheadedness
- Pale, gray, or ashen skin

**Show — First Aid for Chest Pains in Cold Exposure:**
1. **Call 911 immediately.** Chest pain in a cold environment is a cardiac emergency until proven otherwise.
2. Stop all activity. Have the person sit or lie down in the most comfortable position — often sitting with knees bent.
3. Keep the person warm — cold makes cardiac stress worse.
4. Loosen tight clothing around the neck and chest.
5. If the person has prescribed nitroglycerin and is not taking blood pressure medication, assist with it.
6. If the person is prescribed aspirin and is not allergic, one adult aspirin (325 mg) chewed (not swallowed whole) may be advised while waiting for EMS.
7. If the person becomes unconscious and stops breathing, begin CPR.

**Explain — Prevention:**
- Warm up gradually before vigorous outdoor work in cold weather.
- Dress in layers to keep the core warm.
- Take breaks frequently during heavy exertion like shoveling.
- Know your heart health and be aware of risk factors (age, blood pressure, family history).
- Use a snow blower or ask for help with heavy snow removal if you have cardiac risk factors.

## Requirement 11f

{{< drg/requirement number="11f" >}}
Hypothermia
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Hypothermia occurs when the body loses heat faster than it can produce it and core temperature drops below 95°F (35°C). It can happen in temperatures above freezing if conditions include wind, rain, or wet clothing.

**Describe — Symptoms and Signs:**

A useful memory tool is the "Umbles" — as hypothermia progresses, a person begins to **stumble, mumble, fumble, and grumble** before becoming unconscious.

| Stage | Core Temp | Signs |
|---|---|---|
| Mild | 90–95°F (32–35°C) | Shivering, poor coordination, slurred speech, confusion |
| Moderate | 82–90°F (28–32°C) | Shivering stops, severe confusion, muscle stiffness, drowsiness |
| Severe | Below 82°F (28°C) | Unconsciousness, no shivering, very slow pulse and breathing |

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Shivering is a good sign — it means the body is still fighting to rewarm itself. When shivering stops but the person is still cold and confused, that is a warning sign that hypothermia is worsening.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

**Show — First Aid for Hypothermia:**
1. **Call 911** for moderate or severe hypothermia.
2. Move the person out of the cold environment. Protect from wind, rain, and ground contact.
3. Remove any wet clothing gently — rough handling can trigger cardiac arrhythmia in moderate to severe cases.
4. Insulate the person from the ground up (sleeping bag, blankets, dry clothing).
5. Cover the head — a significant amount of heat is lost through the head.
6. Apply warm packs (not hot) to the neck, armpits, and groin if available. Never apply directly to skin without wrapping.
7. For mild hypothermia in a conscious person who can swallow safely: give warm (not hot) sweet drinks.
8. Handle gently — avoid rough movement that can cause a dangerous heart rhythm.
9. If the person is unconscious and not breathing, begin CPR. A hypothermic cardiac arrest may be reversible with hospital treatment.

**Explain — Prevention:**

Use the C.O.L.D. principle:

- **C — Clean:** Keep insulating layers clean and dry. Wet or dirty insulation is poor insulation.
- **O — Overheating:** Avoid sweating by adjusting layers. Wet clothing from sweat is just as dangerous as wet clothing from rain.
- **L — Layers:** Dress in moisture-wicking base layers, insulating mid-layers, and a waterproof outer shell.
- **D — Dry:** Protect all layers from moisture — rain, snow, stream crossings, and sweat.
