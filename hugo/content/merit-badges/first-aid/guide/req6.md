{{< drg/requirement number="6" >}}
Loss of Consciousness. Describe the symptoms and signs of, show first aid for, and explain prevention of these conditions causing loss of consciousness:
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Loss of consciousness means the brain has temporarily lost normal function — either because it is not getting enough oxygen, fuel, or blood, or because it is being disrupted by chemical or electrical signals it cannot manage. The seven sub-requirements in this section cover the most common causes a Scout is likely to encounter. Each has a distinct mechanism, a recognizable set of warning signs, and a first-aid response that you can actually perform on scene. Learn to recognize them early, because the window between warning signs and full unresponsiveness is often short.

## Requirement 6a

{{< drg/requirement number="6a" >}}
Fainting
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Fainting (syncope) is a brief, sudden loss of consciousness caused by a temporary drop in blood flow to the brain. It is rarely dangerous on its own, but a fainting person can fall and be injured. It can also be the first sign of something more serious.

### Why It Happens

The most common type is **vasovagal syncope** — the nervous system overreacts to a trigger (pain, fear, seeing blood, prolonged standing in heat, sudden emotional stress) by slowing the heart and dilating blood vessels in the legs. Blood pools away from the brain and the person goes down.

Other causes include dehydration, prolonged standing without movement, overheating, and getting up too quickly from a chair or bed (orthostatic hypotension).

### Symptoms and Signs

Watch for the warning sequence that often precedes full fainting:

- Sudden pallor (face goes pale or grayish)
- Lightheadedness or dizziness
- Nausea
- Sweating (cool, clammy skin)
- Narrowing or "tunneling" of vision
- Ringing in the ears
- Muscle weakness or feeling unsteady

Full fainting: the person goes limp and falls. Unconsciousness is brief — usually seconds to a couple of minutes — and the person typically recovers on their own.

### First Aid

1. If the person warns you they feel faint, have them sit or lie down immediately before they fall.
2. If they are already on the ground, lay them flat on their back.
3. Raise their legs 6–12 inches to help blood return to the brain — unless you suspect a head or spinal injury.
4. Loosen any tight clothing around the neck or waist.
5. If outdoors in heat, move them to shade.
6. Do not give anything to eat or drink until they are fully alert.
7. Once conscious, have them rise slowly — sit first, then stand — to avoid fainting again.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Call 911 if the person does not regain consciousness within one to two minutes, if they fainted without obvious cause (especially during exercise), if they are older, if they have a history of heart problems, or if they are injured in the fall. Fainting during physical exertion or in a person over 40 without a clear trigger warrants immediate medical evaluation.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Prevention

- Stay hydrated, especially in heat or during exercise.
- Avoid standing still for long periods; flex the calf muscles to pump blood upward.
- If you know your own triggers (like watching injections), warn the person with you and sit down before the trigger occurs.
- Rise slowly from lying or sitting positions, especially in the morning.

## Requirement 6b

{{< drg/requirement number="6b" >}}
Hypoglycemia
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Hypoglycemia means abnormally low blood sugar (glucose). The brain depends entirely on glucose — when the supply drops, brain function deteriorates rapidly. Severe hypoglycemia is a medical emergency.

### Who Is at Risk

Hypoglycemia most commonly affects people with diabetes who have taken too much insulin, missed a meal, or done unexpected exercise. It can also happen to anyone who has not eaten in a long time, is physically exhausted, or has consumed alcohol without food.

### Symptoms and Signs

The symptoms progress as blood glucose falls:

**Mild to moderate:**
- Shakiness, trembling
- Sweating (often heavy)
- Pounding or rapid heart rate
- Hunger
- Pale, clammy skin
- Headache
- Anxiety, irritability, or confusion
- Difficulty concentrating

**Severe:**
- Slurred speech (can resemble intoxication)
- Unsteady gait
- Seizure-like activity
- Loss of consciousness

{{< drg/tip >}}
A Scout with diabetes who becomes confused, shaky, or sweaty should be treated for hypoglycemia first. The symptoms of low blood sugar can look like many other things, but giving a small amount of sugar to a conscious person with diabetes is almost always safe even if you are unsure of the diagnosis.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### First Aid

**If the person is conscious and can swallow:**
1. Give 15–20 grams of fast-acting carbohydrate: 4 glucose tablets, 4 oz of juice or regular (not diet) soda, or a tablespoon of honey.
2. Wait 15 minutes and reassess. If still symptomatic, repeat.
3. Once symptoms resolve, give a more substantial snack (crackers with peanut butter, a sandwich) to sustain blood sugar.
4. Have the person rest and monitor them.

**If the person is unconscious or cannot swallow:**
- Do not attempt to give anything by mouth — choking and aspiration risk.
- Call 911 immediately.
- Place in recovery position if breathing.
- If you have been trained to use glucagon and a kit is available, use it per training.

### Prevention

- Scouts with diabetes should carry fast-acting glucose (glucose tablets, juice pouches) at all times during Scouting activities.
- Adults leading the activity should know which Scouts have diabetes and where their supplies are.
- Regular meals and snacks during high-activity events reduce risk.

## Requirement 6c

{{< drg/requirement number="6c" >}}
Seizure
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A seizure is a sudden, abnormal burst of electrical activity in the brain. It can cause a wide range of symptoms depending on what part of the brain is affected — from a brief staring spell to full-body convulsions with loss of consciousness.

### Types You May Encounter

**Generalized tonic-clonic seizure (grand mal):** The most dramatic type. The person becomes rigid (tonic phase), then goes into repetitive jerking movements (clonic phase). They are unconscious during the seizure and disoriented afterward.

**Absence seizure:** A brief stare or "check out" lasting a few seconds. The person may look like they are daydreaming and may not realize it happened.

**Focal/partial seizure:** Affects only part of the body or one area of the brain. The person may remain conscious but confused, with repetitive movements of one limb or odd sensations.

### Symptoms and Signs

- Sudden loss of consciousness (in generalized seizures)
- Stiffening of the body, followed by rhythmic jerking
- Eyes rolling back or turning to one side
- Frothing at the mouth
- Temporary loss of bladder or bowel control
- Confusion and fatigue after the seizure (postictal state)
- Aura beforehand (unusual smell, taste, visual change, or feeling of déjà vu)

### First Aid

{{< drg/checklist title="Seizure First Aid" subtitle="Focus on protecting the person — you cannot stop the seizure itself" >}}
- **Stay calm.** Seizures look frightening but most end on their own within 1–3 minutes.
- **Ease them to the ground** if they are standing, and clear the area of hard or sharp objects.
- **Protect the head** — cushion it with your hands, a folded jacket, or anything soft.
- **Turn them on their side** (recovery position) once convulsions stop to keep the airway clear and prevent choking on saliva or vomit.
- **Do not restrain them.** You cannot stop the movement by holding them; you may cause injury trying.
- **Do not put anything in their mouth.** People cannot swallow their tongue — this is a myth. Objects placed in the mouth can break teeth and injure both the patient and the responder.
- **Time the seizure** from start to finish.
- **Stay with them** through the postictal confusion period; reassure them calmly.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Call 911 if: the seizure lasts more than 5 minutes; the person does not regain consciousness after the seizure; a second seizure follows closely; the person is injured; the person is pregnant; the person is in water; or this is their first known seizure. A seizure lasting more than 5 minutes (status epilepticus) is a life-threatening emergency.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Prevention

- People with known epilepsy should carry their prescribed medications and wear medical ID jewelry.
- Adequate sleep, avoiding excessive heat, and not skipping medications reduce seizure risk in people with epilepsy.
- There is often no way to prevent a first seizure — focus on fast, correct response.

## Requirement 6d

{{< drg/requirement number="6d" >}}
Drug overdose and alcohol poisoning
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Both drug overdose and alcohol poisoning are emergencies caused by a toxic amount of a substance in the body. They can cause unconsciousness, respiratory depression, and death. You do not need to know exactly what substance is involved to respond correctly.

### Drug Overdose

**Opioid overdose** is the most common life-threatening drug overdose in the United States. Opioids (heroin, fentanyl, prescription painkillers like oxycodone) slow breathing — the primary cause of death is respiratory failure.

**Signs of opioid overdose:**
- Unresponsive or very difficult to wake
- Slow, shallow, or stopped breathing (fewer than one breath every 5 seconds)
- Gurgling or snoring sounds (not normal sleep sounds)
- Blue or gray lips, fingernails, or skin (cyanosis)
- Pinpoint (very small) pupils
- Limp body

**First Aid for Opioid Overdose:**
1. Call 911 immediately.
2. Try to wake the person — sternal rub (firm rubbing on the breastbone with knuckles) may produce a response.
3. If they are not breathing or barely breathing, give rescue breaths if trained, or begin CPR if no pulse.
4. **Naloxone (Narcan):** If available and you are trained to use it, administer naloxone. It reverses opioid effects within minutes. Most states allow bystander use; many Scout leaders carry it at high-adventure events.
5. Place in recovery position if breathing.
6. Stay until EMS arrives — naloxone wears off in 30–90 minutes and the overdose may return.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="Naloxone Access" >}}
Naloxone (brand name Narcan) is available without a prescription at most pharmacies in the United States. It is safe, effective, and can be the difference between life and death during an opioid overdose. Many BSA councils encourage units attending high-adventure camps or large gatherings to carry it.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

### Alcohol Poisoning

Alcohol poisoning occurs when someone drinks a large amount of alcohol in a short time, raising blood alcohol to a level that depresses the central nervous system. It can cause loss of consciousness, vomiting while unconscious (with aspiration), and respiratory arrest.

**Signs of alcohol poisoning:**
- Confusion, stupor, or unresponsiveness
- Vomiting (especially when unconscious)
- Seizures
- Slow, irregular, or stopped breathing
- Pale, bluish, or very cold/clammy skin
- Strong smell of alcohol

**First Aid:**
1. Call 911 — alcohol poisoning is a medical emergency.
2. Keep the person awake if possible.
3. If they are unconscious, place them in the recovery position (on their side) so they cannot choke on vomit.
4. Never leave an unconscious or semiconscious person alone.
5. Do not give them coffee, water, or food to "sober them up" — none of these reverse alcohol's effects.
6. Do not let them "sleep it off" without monitoring — conditions can worsen rapidly.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Never assume a person who is unconscious after drinking is just "sleeping it off." Blood alcohol can continue to rise after a person stops drinking, and respiratory arrest can occur. Monitor breathing constantly and be ready to perform CPR.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Prevention

- Education, awareness, and honest conversation are the primary prevention tools for substance misuse among young people.
- Know who in your group may have prescription opioid medications and where they are stored.
- At events, adult supervision and clear expectations reduce risk.

## Requirement 6e

{{< drg/requirement number="6e" >}}
Underwater hypoxic blackout
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Underwater hypoxic blackout (also called shallow-water blackout) is a sudden, silent loss of consciousness that occurs underwater, usually without warning. It is one of the most dangerous aquatic emergencies because the victim gives no distress signal before going under — and it kills experienced swimmers.

### How It Happens

The mechanism involves **hyperventilation before a breath-hold dive**. When a swimmer hyperventilates (takes many rapid, deep breaths), they lower the carbon dioxide level in the blood well below normal. Carbon dioxide — not oxygen — is the primary trigger that tells your brain to breathe. With CO₂ artificially low, the swimmer can hold their breath far longer than normal. But oxygen still depletes as they swim. When oxygen falls to a critical level, the brain shuts off without the usual overwhelming urge to breathe. The swimmer blacks out underwater, often still on the way up, and drowns.

### Warning Signs

There are **no reliable warning signs.** That is what makes this emergency uniquely dangerous. The swimmer may appear to be resting or gliding underwater. Bystanders often do not realize anything is wrong.

### First Aid

1. Identify the victim immediately — they will appear motionless underwater.
2. Get them out of the water as quickly as possible.
3. Call 911.
4. Begin rescue breathing or CPR immediately if they are not breathing.
5. Even if they respond quickly, seek emergency medical evaluation — secondary drowning is possible.

### Prevention

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Never hyperventilate before a breath-hold dive or underwater swimming challenge. This practice dramatically raises the risk of sudden blackout. This rule applies to swimming pools, open water, and any aquatic setting. Never swim alone and never leave a breath-hold swimmer unobserved — even for a moment.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

At Scouting aquatic events, be alert for "contests" involving how long a person can hold their breath underwater. These are prohibited for good reason.

## Requirement 6f

{{< drg/requirement number="6f" >}}
Cold water shock and drowning
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Cold water and drowning are separate but related dangers. Cold water shock can cause loss of consciousness before drowning begins; drowning itself deprives the brain of oxygen and leads to unconsciousness. Both can occur extremely quickly.

### Cold Water Shock

When the body is suddenly immersed in cold water (below about 60°F / 15°C), it triggers an involuntary gasp reflex. If the person's head is submerged at that moment, they inhale water. This is followed by hyperventilation and loss of muscular control. Swimming ability can be lost within minutes.

**Signs:**
- Sudden gasp or inability to breathe normally after entering cold water
- Rapid, uncontrollable hyperventilation
- Muscle cramping
- Loss of swimming ability
- Confusion and loss of consciousness

### Drowning

Drowning occurs when water enters the airway and prevents normal breathing. It can happen silently and quickly. Drowning is not the thrashing, calling-for-help scene often depicted in movies. An actively drowning person is typically upright in the water, heads tilted back, unable to speak, with arms pressing down on the water rather than waving.

### First Aid

1. Call for help and activate the aquatic emergency plan.
2. Reach, throw, don't go — attempt a rescue without entering the water if possible (use a rope, pole, or flotation device).
3. If entry is necessary, trained lifesavers only — panicking drowning victims can push rescuers underwater.
4. Once out of the water: check breathing.
5. If not breathing, begin CPR immediately — drowning victims of any age respond to CPR even after extended submersion in cold water.
6. All drowning victims should be evaluated by EMS even if they appear to recover fully — secondary drowning (delayed pulmonary edema) can occur hours later.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Anyone removed from the water after a drowning incident must be evaluated by a physician, even if they seem fine. Fluid in the lungs can cause life-threatening pulmonary edema hours after the event. Do not send a recovered near-drowning patient home without medical clearance.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Prevention

- Wear a properly fitted life jacket on open water — it keeps your head above water even if cold water shock incapacitates you.
- Never swim alone.
- Learn to recognize pre-drowning behavior in others.
- At Scouting aquatic events, buddy systems and trained water safety supervision are required.

## Requirement 6g

{{< drg/requirement number="6g" >}}
Lightning strike and electric shock
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Both lightning and household or industrial electrical current can cause cardiac arrest, respiratory arrest, and severe internal and external injuries. The key first-aid rule for both is one that surprises many people: it is safe to touch a lightning strike victim immediately, but an electrocution victim may still be in contact with a live current.

### Lightning Strike

Lightning delivers an enormous burst of electrical energy through the body. Unlike electrocution from a power source, the current does not continue — the victim is not "charged." Injuries include cardiac arrest, respiratory arrest, burns (entry and exit wounds), blast injuries from the shockwave, and neurological effects.

**Signs:**
- Person found on the ground unresponsive, often near a tree or in an open area
- Burns on the skin (may be feathering/Lichtenberg patterns)
- Clothing may be damaged or shoes blown off
- Ruptured eardrums (bleeding from ears)
- Temporary blindness or hearing loss

**First Aid:**
1. Ensure your own safety — confirm the storm is passing and the area is clear of further strike risk.
2. Begin CPR immediately if the victim is pulseless or not breathing — lightning victims respond well to CPR.
3. Treat for shock and burns.
4. Call 911.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Contrary to the "treat the worst patient last" triage principle, in a lightning mass casualty event, prioritize the patients who are pulseless or not breathing — they are the ones most likely to survive with CPR, because lightning arrest is often due to cardiac arrhythmia that can be corrected.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Electric Shock (Household and High Voltage)

Unlike lightning, a person in contact with an ongoing electrical source is still receiving current. Touching them directly connects you to the circuit.

**First Aid:**
1. **Do not touch the victim** until you are certain the power is off.
2. Call 911 immediately.
3. Turn off the power at the breaker or unplug the source. Do not attempt to move the wire with a stick — this is not reliable.
4. Once power is confirmed off, assess and begin CPR if needed.
5. Do not move the patient unless there is immediate danger — electrical injuries can damage the spine.
6. Treat burns and monitor for shock.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
High-voltage lines (downed power lines, electrical substations) require a minimum safe distance and professional response. Do not approach. Call 911 and keep others away.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Prevention

- Follow the 30/30 rule: if the time between lightning flash and thunder is less than 30 seconds, seek shelter immediately; stay inside for 30 minutes after the last thunder.
- Avoid tall isolated trees, hilltops, open fields, and bodies of water during electrical storms.
- Use surge protectors; do not use damaged electrical cords.

Requirement 6 asks you to describe, show, and explain prevention for seven distinct causes of loss of consciousness. What connects them is this: each one has a recognizable warning window — a moment where recognition and action can prevent full unconsciousness or death. The Scout who knows what pallor and clammy skin mean, who understands why you never hyperventilate before a breath-hold dive, and who will actually put an unconscious person in the recovery position is the one who makes a difference.
