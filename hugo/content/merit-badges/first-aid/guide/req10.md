{{< drg/requirement number="10" >}}
Moving a Patient. Do the following:
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Moving an injured person is almost always the wrong first instinct. Every unnecessary move risks turning a manageable injury into a catastrophic one. This group of requirements teaches you to think clearly before you touch an injured person, choose the right carry for the situation, and demonstrate the techniques that EMS and rescue professionals use every day. Master the decision-making in 10a before practicing the carries in 10b through 10e.

## Requirement 10a

{{< drg/requirement number="10a" >}}
Describe the conditions under which an injured person should and should not be moved.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

### When You Should NOT Move an Injured Person

As a general rule, keep an injured person where they are and wait for professional help. Do not move the patient if:

- There is any possibility of a head, neck, or spinal injury and the patient is stable
- The patient is breathing and has no immediately life-threatening bleeding
- Moving the person requires passing through an unsafe area
- You do not have enough helpers to move them safely

### When You SHOULD Move an Injured Person

There are specific situations where staying put is more dangerous than moving. You must move the patient when:

- **Immediate life threat at the scene:** The patient is in or near a burning building, rising floodwater, collapsed structure, traffic, or other danger that cannot be controlled.
- **You cannot give essential care where they are:** A patient who needs CPR face-down on an uneven slope must be moved to perform compressions effectively.
- **Evacuation is the only option:** In a wilderness setting where help cannot reach you and the patient's condition is worsening, a careful evacuation may be necessary.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Even when you must move someone, the decision should take only seconds — not minutes. Scan the scene: Is the danger immediate and unavoidable? Is there a safer spot within reach? Do you have enough people to do this safely? Answer those three questions before you touch the patient.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Requirement 10b

{{< drg/requirement number="10b" >}}
If a sick or an injured person must be moved, tell how you would decide the best method. Demonstrate these methods.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Choosing the right carry depends on four factors: **the injury**, **the patient's ability to help**, **the terrain**, and **how many helpers you have**.

### Decision Framework

| Factor | Considerations |
|---|---|
| **Injury type** | Spinal injury → minimize rotation; ankle/foot → no weight-bearing; unconscious → two-rescuer carry |
| **Can the patient assist?** | Alert + can bear some weight → walking assist; unconscious or unable → full carry or stretcher |
| **Terrain** | Stairs or narrow passage → cradle or firefighter carry; open flat ground → multiple options |
| **Number of helpers** | One rescuer → drag or firefighter carry; two → two-person carry; four or more → stretcher |

### Common Carry Methods

**Pack-strap carry (one rescuer):** The patient puts their arms over your shoulders from behind; you grip their wrists and lean forward to lift their weight onto your back. Good for a conscious patient who cannot walk but has no spinal injury.

**Firefighter carry (one rescuer):** Drape the patient over one shoulder, face down, balancing their torso on your shoulder and holding their wrist with one hand. Used for unconscious patients when you must act alone quickly.

**Two-person arm carry:** One rescuer cradles the patient's back and the other cradles the legs. Both rescuers link arms under the patient. Suitable for short carries with a patient who cannot walk.

**Chair carry (two rescuers):** Rescuers face each other and grip the patient's crossed wrists with one hand each, while their other hands grip behind the patient's knees. Useful on stairs.

**Walking assist:** One or two rescuers support the patient under the arms, allowing the patient to bear some of their own weight. Best for a patient who is mobile but unsteady.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Before demonstrating any carry, explain your reasoning to your counselor. "I chose this carry because the patient is conscious, can help, has a suspected ankle fracture, and we have two rescuers on level ground." That kind of thinking shows you understand the decision, not just the technique.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Requirement 10c

{{< drg/requirement number="10c" >}}
By yourself and with a partner, demonstrate how to transport a person from a smoke-filled room.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Smoke incapacitates people quickly. A smoke-filled room is also disorienting and may contain structural hazards. The goal is to get the patient and yourself out as fast as possible, staying low where air is cleaner.

### Alone — The Drag

When you are by yourself and someone cannot walk or crawl:

**Clothes drag:** Grip the patient's shirt or jacket at the collar and shoulders, cross their arms over their chest, and drag them backward toward the exit while you crawl or crouch low. Keep the patient's head elevated slightly off the floor if possible.

**Blanket drag:** If a blanket or tarp is available, roll the patient onto it and pull the blanket — this reduces friction and protects the patient's head from dragging on debris.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Stay low. Smoke and toxic gases rise. Crawl if the smoke is thick — air near the floor is cooler and has more oxygen. If you cannot crawl through the space safely, do not enter. No rescue attempt is worth becoming a second victim.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### With a Partner — The Carry or Two-Person Drag

With a second rescuer, one person cradles the patient's upper body and the other lifts the legs. Move quickly and low toward the nearest safe exit.

### After the Room

Once outside or in a safe area:
- Place the patient flat and perform a rapid assessment
- Check airway, breathing, and circulation
- Call 911 if not already done
- Treat for smoke inhalation (fresh air, monitor breathing, be ready to give rescue breaths)

## Requirement 10d

{{< drg/requirement number="10d" >}}
By yourself and with a partner, demonstrate how to transport a person with a sprained ankle for at least 25 yards.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A sprained ankle is one of the most common hiking injuries. The patient is typically conscious and cooperative but cannot bear full weight on the injured foot.

### Alone — The Human Crutch

Stand on the patient's injured side. Have the patient put their arm around your shoulder. Put your arm around their waist. The patient hops on their uninjured foot while you match their pace and absorb some of their weight. This works well for short carries but is tiring for the rescuer over long distances.

### With a Partner — Two-Person Walking Assist

Both rescuers stand on either side of the patient. Each rescuer puts one arm around the patient's waist. The patient places an arm over each rescuer's shoulder. The patient can hop or take small steps on the uninjured foot while rescuers provide support and forward movement.

{{< drg/tip >}}
On the trail, a trekking pole adjusted to the right height can serve as a crutch for a patient with a sprained ankle, freeing your hands and reducing rescuer fatigue on longer carries. If the ankle is severely sprained, improvise a splint before the carry to reduce pain and further injury.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Requirement 10e

{{< drg/requirement number="10e" >}}
With helpers under your supervision, improvise a stretcher and move a presumably unconscious person for at least 25 yards.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

An improvised stretcher is used when a patient cannot be safely carried by hand and no commercial stretcher is available. You are in charge of this demonstration — brief your helpers, assign positions, and direct the move.

### Building an Improvised Stretcher

**Two-pole and jacket stretcher:**
1. Gather two straight poles or branches at least five feet long.
2. Lay two or three jackets on the ground with the zipper side down.
3. Thread the poles through the sleeves of each jacket (do not zip the jackets).
4. The poles should extend at least one foot beyond each jacket at both ends.
5. Test the stretcher by pressing down firmly before placing the patient on it.

**Tarp or blanket stretcher:**
1. Lay a tarp or large blanket flat on the ground.
2. Place one of the poles in the center of the tarp.
3. Fold one third of the tarp over the pole.
4. Place the second pole on top of the folded section.
5. Fold the remaining third of the tarp over the second pole.
6. When the patient's weight is applied, friction holds the layers together.

### Moving the Patient

1. **Brief your helpers** before touching the patient: "Person A holds the head, persons B and C hold the middle, person D holds the feet. On my count, we roll to load — ready?"
2. **Log-roll** the patient onto the stretcher using coordinated movement.
3. **Designate a leader** (you) to call steps and keep everyone moving at the same pace.
4. **Carry at knee height** — low enough to lower quickly if someone stumbles, high enough to avoid dragging.
5. **The person at the head** should be able to watch the patient's airway and breathing throughout the carry.

{{< drg/checklist title="Improvised Stretcher Checklist" subtitle="Verify before loading" >}}
- Poles are long enough to extend beyond the patient's head and feet.
- Stretcher supports the patient's full length without sagging excessively.
- Fabric is secured — no sections can slip free under weight.
- All carriers know their position and grip before the patient is loaded.
- A leader is designated to call movements.
- The path to the destination is clear of trip hazards.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}
