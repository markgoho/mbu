{{< drg/requirement number="8b" >}}
Describe the symptoms and signs of and first aid for a muscle strain, a muscle tear, and a tendon rupture.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Muscle strains, muscle tears, and tendon ruptures all involve the contractile machinery of movement — the muscles and the tendons that connect them to bone. They exist on a spectrum from mild to severe, but each has a recognizable pattern of symptoms, and the first-aid response follows consistent principles across all three.

## Muscle Strain

A strain is a stretch or mild-to-moderate disruption of muscle fibers. Think of it as the most common soft-tissue injury in sport and outdoor activity — the pulled calf, the stiff lower back after a heavy lift, the sore hamstring after a sprint.

### Symptoms and Signs

- **Pain during and after use** — typically a dull ache or burning sensation along the muscle belly; worse with contraction or stretch
- **Muscle stiffness and spasm** — the muscle may feel tight or rigid, especially after rest
- **Tenderness to palpation** — pressing directly on the injured area causes localized pain
- **Mild swelling** — may appear within hours
- **Bruising (ecchymosis)** — may develop 24–48 hours after injury as blood pools under the skin
- **Reduced strength and range of motion** — movement is limited by pain but not completely lost

### First Aid for a Muscle Strain

The standard first-aid framework for soft tissue injuries is **RICE** (Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation). Current evidence also supports the updated **PRICE** (Protection, Rest, Ice, Compression, Elevation) or **POLICE** (Protection, Optimal Loading, Ice, Compression, Elevation) frameworks used by sports medicine providers.

{{< drg/checklist title="RICE First Aid for Muscle Strain" subtitle="Begin within the first 24–48 hours of injury" >}}
- **Rest:** Stop the activity causing pain. Avoid using the injured muscle for the first 24–48 hours. Complete immobility is not always ideal — gentle, pain-free movement may help recovery after the acute phase.
- **Ice:** Apply a cold pack or ice wrapped in a cloth to the injured area for 15–20 minutes at a time, several times per day. Never apply ice directly to bare skin — this can cause a cold burn.
- **Compression:** Wrap the area with an elastic bandage (ACE wrap) to reduce swelling. Wrap firmly but not so tightly that circulation is impaired — check for numbness, tingling, or color change below the wrap.
- **Elevation:** Raise the injured limb above the level of the heart whenever possible to reduce swelling through gravity drainage.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

**Over-the-counter pain relief** (ibuprofen or naproxen, if not contraindicated) can reduce pain and inflammation. Follow dosing instructions and check for allergies or contraindications.

**Return to activity** should be gradual — resume only when pain-free at rest, then progress through pain-free movement, then light activity, then full activity.

## Muscle Tear

A muscle tear involves significant disruption of muscle fibers — ranging from a partial tear (a large portion of fibers broken) to a complete rupture (the muscle belly is split in two). It is a more serious injury than a strain.

### Symptoms and Signs

- **Sudden, severe pain** at the moment of injury — often described as a "pop" or "snap" felt within the muscle
- **Immediate, significant weakness** — far greater functional loss than a strain; the person may not be able to use the limb at all in a complete tear
- **Rapid swelling and bruising** — often appears within minutes to hours; can be dramatic
- **Palpable defect** — in a complete tear, you may feel a gap or a bunching of the muscle tissue where it has pulled apart; the muscle may roll up into a visible mass
- **Severe tenderness** along the muscle belly

### First Aid for a Muscle Tear

1. Stop all activity involving the injured muscle immediately.
2. Apply ice wrapped in cloth — 20 minutes on, 20 minutes off. Do not compress a suspected complete rupture tightly, as this may increase pressure in the injured area.
3. Immobilize and support the limb in a comfortable position.
4. Seek medical evaluation — partial tears often require imaging (MRI) and may need physical therapy; complete tears frequently require surgical repair.
5. Do not stretch or massage a freshly torn muscle — this can worsen bleeding and increase damage.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
A complete muscle tear is a medical injury, not a field-treatable problem. Immobilize the limb, manage pain, and ensure the patient is evaluated by a physician. Attempting to "walk it off" or returning to activity risks converting a repairable injury into a permanent disability.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Tendon Rupture

A tendon rupture is a partial or complete tear of a tendon — the dense, fibrous cord that transmits muscular force to bone. The Achilles tendon (at the back of the heel) and the patellar tendon (connecting the kneecap to the shin bone) are the most commonly ruptured tendons, but any tendon under high load can rupture.

### Symptoms and Signs

- **A sudden, sharp "snap" or "pop"** — often audible and almost always felt; many patients describe the sensation of being struck from behind even though no one was there
- **Immediate, severe localized pain** — at the site of the rupture, not diffusely through the muscle
- **Complete or near-complete loss of a specific movement** — for Achilles rupture, the person cannot plantar flex (point the foot downward) with the injured leg; they may not be able to bear weight
- **A palpable gap** — pressing along the tendon reveals an abnormal depression or hollow at the rupture site
- **Swelling and bruising** — appear quickly, centered over the tendon
- **Thompson test (Achilles):** Squeezing the calf of a person lying face-down should cause the foot to plantar flex; if the foot does not move, this suggests a complete Achilles rupture

### First Aid for a Tendon Rupture

1. Stop all weight-bearing or activity on the injured limb.
2. Immobilize the joint in a neutral, comfortable position.
3. Apply ice wrapped in cloth to reduce swelling.
4. Elevate the limb.
5. Do not attempt to bear weight or test range of motion — this can worsen the injury.
6. Arrange transport for medical evaluation — tendon ruptures typically require imaging, and many (especially complete ruptures) require surgical repair and months of rehabilitation.

{{< drg/tip >}}
When describing these three injuries to your counselor, the best approach is to talk through how they would present differently at the moment of injury: a strain feels like a pull and gets worse over hours; a muscle tear produces sudden sharp pain with immediate weakness; a tendon rupture produces a distinct "snap" or "pop" and sudden complete loss of one specific movement. These differences in onset and character of symptoms are what your counselor is testing.
{{< /drg/tip >}}
