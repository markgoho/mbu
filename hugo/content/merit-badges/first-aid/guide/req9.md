{{< drg/requirement number="9" >}}
Head and Spine Injuries. Do the following:
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Head and spine injuries are among the most serious emergencies you can encounter. Damage to the brain, spinal cord, or the nerves that travel through the neck and back can happen without any visible wound, and a second injury caused by moving the patient incorrectly can be just as devastating as the original trauma. Work through requirements 9a and 9b before attempting the hands-on demonstrations in 9c and 9d.

## Requirement 9a

{{< drg/requirement number="9a" >}}
Describe the symptoms and signs of, relationships between, possible complications of, and prevention of head, neck, and back injuries.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Head, neck, and back injuries are closely connected because the brain, brainstem, and spinal cord form one continuous structure. Damage at any level can affect function at or below that level.

### Symptoms and Signs

**Head injuries** can produce:

- Headache, especially one that worsens over time
- Confusion, disorientation, or unusual behavior
- Unequal pupils (one larger than the other)
- Loss of consciousness, even briefly
- Nausea or vomiting
- Blood or clear fluid from the nose or ears
- Bruising behind the ears (Battle's sign) or around both eyes (raccoon eyes)
- Slurred speech or difficulty understanding words

**Neck and back injuries** can produce:

- Pain or tenderness along the spine
- Numbness, tingling, or weakness in the arms or legs
- Loss of movement or sensation below the injury site
- Unusual posture, particularly arms held stiffly out or curled (decerebrate or decorticate posturing)
- Loss of bladder or bowel control

### Relationships Between These Injuries

A fall, motor vehicle collision, diving accident, or blow to the head often involves the neck and back as well. A head injury that causes unconsciousness means the spine should be protected, because the forces that knocked the person out were also transmitted down the neck and back. Never treat these injuries as isolated if the mechanism of injury was significant.

### Possible Complications

- Swelling inside the skull (intracranial pressure) can compress the brain and become life-threatening within minutes to hours
- Spinal cord compression or transection can cause permanent paralysis
- Airway obstruction in an unconscious patient with a head injury
- Secondary injury caused by moving the patient before the spine is stabilized

### Prevention

{{< drg/checklist title="Preventing Head and Spine Injuries" subtitle="Smart habits before the activity starts" >}}
- Wear a properly fitted helmet for cycling, climbing, contact sports, and motorized activities.
- Never dive into unknown or shallow water headfirst.
- Wear a seatbelt every time you ride in a vehicle.
- Use fall-arrest systems and proper harnesses when working or climbing at height.
- Warm up before physical activity to reduce the chance of sudden muscle failure.
- Follow the buddy system and stay alert to hazards in the environment.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

## Requirement 9b

{{< drg/requirement number="9b" >}}
Describe the symptoms and signs of and first aid for a concussion.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A concussion is a traumatic brain injury caused by a bump, blow, or jolt to the head or body that causes the brain to move rapidly back and forth inside the skull. It does not require a loss of consciousness to be serious.

### Symptoms and Signs of a Concussion

**Physical signs:**
- Headache or a feeling of pressure in the head
- Dizziness or balance problems
- Nausea or vomiting
- Sensitivity to light or noise
- Blurred or double vision
- Feeling slowed down or "in a fog"

**Cognitive signs:**
- Difficulty concentrating or remembering
- Confusion about what happened
- Answering questions slowly
- Seeming dazed or stunned

**Emotional signs:**
- Irritability or sadness that is out of character
- More emotional than usual

**Sleep-related signs:**
- Sleeping more or less than normal
- Trouble falling asleep

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
If you suspect a concussion, remove the person from activity immediately. The motto is "When in doubt, sit it out." Do not allow someone with a possible concussion to return to play, practice, or any strenuous activity the same day.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### First Aid for a Concussion

1. **Remove from activity.** The person must stop what they are doing right away.
2. **Do not leave them alone.** Watch for worsening symptoms.
3. **Call for professional medical evaluation.** A concussion must be evaluated by a health care provider before return to any physical activity.
4. **Rest.** Reduce mental and physical activity, including screen time, while symptoms are present.
5. **Call 911 immediately** if the person loses consciousness, has a seizure, has one pupil larger than the other, experiences extreme confusion or slurred speech, or vomits repeatedly.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
A second concussion before the brain has fully healed from the first one — called second-impact syndrome — can cause rapid, severe brain swelling and is potentially fatal. This is why doctors hold athletes and Scouts out of activity until all symptoms are gone.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

## Requirement 9c

{{< drg/requirement number="9c" >}}
Demonstrate first aid for an open head wound with a triangular or other bandage.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

An open head wound must be treated carefully: control bleeding without pressing hard on a skull that may be fractured, and bandage the wound to protect it from contamination.

### What to Watch For Before Bandaging

Before treating a head wound, quickly check for:

- Embedded objects or bone fragments — do **not** remove these; bandage around them
- Clear fluid (not blood) from the wound, which may indicate a skull fracture and cerebrospinal fluid leak
- Signs of deeper injury (see 9a and 9b above)

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Apply only gentle pressure to a head wound. If you feel a depression, spongy area, or suspect a skull fracture, do not press directly on the injury. Instead, build up a ring of bandage material around the wound and cover without applying pressure to the center.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Applying a Triangular Bandage to the Head

1. **Fold a cravat** (hemmed edge) from the triangular bandage about two inches wide.
2. **Position the center** of the cravat at the front of the head, just above the eyebrows.
3. **Cross the two tails at the back** of the head just above the base of the skull.
4. **Bring the tails forward** and tie them at the forehead.
5. **Tuck in** or fold the loose knot so it does not press on the skull.
6. **Check the fit** — snug enough to hold the dressing in place, not so tight it restricts circulation or adds pressure to the wound.

{{< drg/tip >}}
If a triangular bandage is not available, improvise with a clean cloth, neckerchief, or piece of clothing. The goal is to hold a clean pad against the wound and keep it in place during transport.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Requirement 9d

{{< drg/requirement number="9d" >}}
Demonstrate first aid for someone with a suspected neck or back injury.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

When a mechanism of injury (fall, collision, sports impact) could have damaged the neck or back, your most important job is to keep the spine from moving until professional help arrives.

### The Golden Rule: Minimize Movement

Do not let the person get up. Do not let them twist, flex, or rotate the head or neck. Your one job until EMS arrives is to hold the spine still.

### Steps for Suspected Spinal Injury

1. **Call 911 immediately.** A suspected spinal injury is always a 911 call.
2. **Keep the person still.** Ask them not to move and speak calmly to reassure them.
3. **Manual stabilization.** If trained, place your hands on both sides of the head — do not pull or press — to hold the head in a neutral position (face forward, level). Maintain this until EMS takes over.
4. **Do not remove a helmet** unless you are specifically trained to do so and there is an airway emergency.
5. **Monitor airway and breathing.** If the person is unconscious and not breathing, rescue breathing or CPR takes priority over spinal precautions. An airway emergency overrides everything else.
6. **Keep the person warm** to prevent shock and hypothermia.
7. **Log-roll only if necessary.** If the person must be rolled (for vomiting or airway), use a team log-roll — three or four people keeping the head, shoulders, trunk, and legs aligned at all times.

### When Movement Cannot Be Avoided

If you are in a wilderness setting far from EMS and the patient must be moved (approaching danger, hypothermia, etc.), coordinate with your entire team, designate one person to control the head and neck, and keep the spine as aligned as possible throughout.
