{{< drg/requirement number="12" >}}
Mental Health Conditions. Describe the following:
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Not every first-aid emergency is physical. Scouts encounter situations that feel overwhelming — difficult challenges, frightening moments, and sometimes signs that a person may be in real danger. This requirement asks you to recognize those situations, understand how people naturally react to stress, and know the most important thing a first-aider can do in a mental health crisis: **get a trusted adult or professional involved quickly.** Requirements 12a and 12b build the foundation; 12c and 12d are the critical safety piece.

## Requirement 12a

{{< drg/requirement number="12a" >}}
Reactions associated with at least three stressful situations, such as mountain backpacking, rappelling, a ropes course, speaking before an audience, making a phone call to an adult, taking a swim test, missing home, lighting a match, trying out for a sports team, meeting someone for the first time, or other stressful circumstances.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Stress is the body's natural response to a perceived challenge or threat. When the brain senses danger or pressure, it triggers physical, emotional, and behavioral changes. These reactions are normal — they are designed to help us respond. The goal is to describe them accurately, not to judge them.

### Common Stress Reactions

**Physical reactions** are caused by the "fight-or-flight" response — a surge of adrenaline and cortisol preparing the body to act:

- Rapid or pounding heartbeat
- Fast, shallow breathing
- Sweating, trembling, or shaking hands
- Dry mouth or upset stomach
- Muscle tension, headache
- Feeling flushed or suddenly cold

**Emotional reactions** reflect the mind trying to process uncertainty or threat:

- Anxiety or nervousness
- Fear or dread
- Feeling overwhelmed or "frozen"
- Excitement that feels like anxiety (the body responds similarly to both)
- Irritability or frustration

**Behavioral reactions** are what you can observe in others:

- Avoiding or backing away from the challenge
- Becoming very quiet or withdrawn
- Talking fast, pacing, or fidgeting
- Crying or laughing inappropriately under pressure
- Becoming argumentative or irritable

### Three Example Situations

**Rappelling for the first time:** Heart pounds, palms sweat, legs feel shaky at the edge. The person may hesitate, look down repeatedly, or freeze partway through the lean-back. This is a completely normal response to height and perceived danger.

**Speaking in front of an audience:** Dry mouth, rapid heartbeat, and the feeling that everyone is watching. Some people speak too fast or lose their train of thought. The physical response is nearly identical to excitement.

**Missing home at camp (homesickness):** Sadness, low energy, difficulty focusing on activities, wanting to contact family. Some Scouts become quiet and withdraw. Homesickness is a normal emotional response to a new environment away from familiar support.

## Requirement 12b

{{< drg/requirement number="12b" >}}
The actions that you and others should take to prepare for and manage these situations.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

There are practical steps — before and during — that reduce the intensity of stress reactions and help people manage everyday high-pressure moments. These are not clinical interventions. They are preparation strategies and in-the-moment tools anyone can use.

### Before the Stressful Situation: Prepare

- **Know what to expect.** Uncertainty makes stress worse. Learn what the activity involves before you arrive — watch a video of rappelling, read about the ropes course, visit the venue in advance if possible.
- **Practice related skills.** Competence reduces anxiety. If you are nervous about speaking to a group, practice in front of a mirror or with one trusted friend first.
- **Get enough sleep.** A tired brain is less able to regulate emotions. Fatigue amplifies stress reactions.
- **Eat and stay hydrated.** Low blood sugar and dehydration worsen mood and physical stress responses.
- **Talk to someone you trust.** Naming your concern out loud — to a parent, Scoutmaster, or friend — often reduces its intensity.

### During the Stressful Situation: Manage

- **Breathe deliberately.** Slow, controlled breathing is the fastest way to reduce the physical stress response. A simple method: breathe in for 4 counts, hold for 4, breathe out for 4, hold for 4. Repeat.
- **Focus on the next small step.** Instead of thinking about the whole challenge, focus only on what comes next — one foot on the rappel device, the first sentence of your speech.
- **Stay physically grounded.** Notice what you can feel, hear, and see around you. This pulls attention away from anxious thoughts.

### How Others Can Help

- Stay calm yourself — calm is contagious.
- Acknowledge the person's feelings without dismissing them. "I can see this is hard" is more helpful than "Don't be nervous."
- Offer a specific, simple next step. "Take one breath and then step to the edge."
- Stay close and be patient — do not rush or push.
- If someone wants to stop an activity, honor that decision. A Scout who has reached their limit should not be pressured to continue.

## Requirement 12c

{{< drg/requirement number="12c" >}}
The indications that someone might be a danger to themselves or others.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This section is different from 12a and 12b. The behaviors described here are not normal stress reactions — they are warning signs that a person may be in a crisis that requires immediate help from a trusted adult or emergency services.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
If you observe any of the warning signs below, your job is not to fix the situation yourself. Your job is to stay with the person, keep them calm, and get a trusted adult or emergency help immediately. This is a first-aid response, not a counseling session.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Warning Signs of Danger to Self

- Talking about wanting to die, wanting to hurt themselves, or having no reason to live
- Saying things like "Everyone would be better off without me" or "I won't be a problem much longer"
- Giving away prized possessions for no apparent reason
- Sudden calmness or apparent relief after a period of deep depression (which can indicate a decision has been made)
- Withdrawal from friends, family, and activities they usually enjoy
- Dramatic change in mood, behavior, sleep, or eating habits
- Increased use of alcohol or drugs
- Expressing feelings of being trapped, hopeless, or a burden to others
- Searching for or collecting means to harm themselves

### Warning Signs of Danger to Others

- Making explicit or implied threats toward a specific person, group, or location
- Describing detailed plans to harm someone
- Expressing intense, unresolved rage or desire for revenge
- Sudden acquisition of weapons or items that could be used as weapons
- Extreme agitation, aggressive or erratic behavior that appears to be escalating
- Statements about "making someone pay" or "not caring what happens next"

{{< drg/tip >}}
Trust your instincts. If something a person says or does makes you genuinely worried about their safety or someone else's, take it seriously. It is always better to involve a trusted adult and be wrong than to stay quiet and be right.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Requirement 12d

{{< drg/requirement number="12d" >}}
The actions that you should take if you suspect that someone might be a danger to themselves or others.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

If you believe someone may be a danger to themselves or others, follow these steps. Your role is to bridge the gap until a trained adult or professional can take over — not to resolve the crisis yourself.

### What to Do

**1. Stay with the person.**
Do not leave them alone. A calm, caring presence matters. You do not need to have answers. Simply staying close signals that you care and that help is coming.

**2. Stay calm.**
Speak quietly and slowly. Avoid arguing, challenging, or dismissing what the person is saying. Do not minimize their feelings.

**3. Get a trusted adult immediately.**
Tell a parent, Scoutmaster, teacher, camp director, coach, or another nearby adult what you have observed and why you are concerned. Be direct: "I'm worried about [name]. They said something that scared me and I need an adult to come right now."

**4. Call 911 if there is immediate danger.**
If the person has a weapon, has already harmed themselves, or is in immediate physical danger, call 911 without delay. Do not wait to find an adult first.

**5. Do not keep it secret.**
A person in crisis may ask you not to tell anyone. Your first responsibility is their safety, not keeping a secret. Telling a trusted adult is the right thing to do even if the person is angry at first.

**6. Do not try to handle it alone.**
A Scout is not trained to manage a mental health crisis, and that is not a failure — it is honest self-awareness. Your job is to recognize the warning signs and get the right help. That is a complete and meaningful response.

### After You Get Help

- Stay available to support the adult or professional who steps in.
- Take care of yourself — witnessing or responding to a serious situation can be stressful. Talk to a trusted adult about how you are feeling afterward.

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The 988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline (call or text **988** in the United States) connects callers to trained crisis counselors 24 hours a day. If you or someone you know is struggling, this is a resource adults can call or direct someone toward for professional support.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}
