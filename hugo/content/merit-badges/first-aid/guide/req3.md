{{< drg/requirement number="3" >}}
Wounds with No External Bleeding. Describe the symptoms and signs of, show first aid for, and explain prevention of these wounds:
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Requirement 3 covers the widest variety of any requirement in this badge — seventeen different wound types, from bruises and burns to snake bites and frostbite. None of them produce significant external bleeding, which is exactly what makes them easy to underestimate. Internal damage, chemical injury, venom, and cold exposure can all be serious without a visible wound.

Every sub-requirement follows the same triple formula your counselor will test you on:

1. **Symptoms and signs** — What does the patient feel, and what can you see or detect?
2. **First aid** — What do you do, in what order?
3. **Prevention** — How do you keep this from happening in the first place?

Work through all seventeen sub-requirements. The ones most relevant to outdoor Scouting activities — burns, cold injuries, bites, stings — deserve extra attention.

## Requirement 3a

{{< drg/requirement number="3a" >}}
Closed wounds, such as a bruise (contusion) or a hematoma
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A closed wound is damage to tissue under unbroken skin. Blood leaks from damaged capillaries (bruise / contusion) or from larger vessels pooling into a pocket (hematoma). Because the skin is intact, the danger of infection is low — but a large hematoma can cause dangerous pressure or significant blood loss.

### Symptoms and Signs

- Discoloration of the skin — initially red or purple, shifting to blue-green, then yellow-brown as blood breaks down over days
- Swelling and firmness at the site (hematoma may feel like a lump)
- Tenderness and pain on touch or movement
- Possible limited range of motion if near a joint
- A hematoma over the skull or spine after head or back trauma is a red flag — it can indicate deeper injury

### First Aid

{{< drg/checklist title="First Aid — Contusion / Hematoma" subtitle="RICE is the core framework for closed soft-tissue injuries" >}}
- **R — Rest** the injured area; stop the activity that caused the injury.
- **I — Ice** (or instant cold pack wrapped in cloth): apply for 15–20 minutes at a time during the first 24–48 hours to reduce swelling and pain.
- **C — Compression**: apply a snug (not tight) elastic bandage to limit swelling; check circulation below the bandage.
- **E — Elevation**: raise the injured limb above heart level when possible to reduce swelling.
- For a **large or rapidly growing hematoma**, seek medical care — it may need to be drained.
- If the bruising is near the head, neck, or spine following trauma, evaluate for concussion or spinal injury and call 911 if neurological symptoms appear.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
A bruise that appears without known trauma, spreads rapidly, or is accompanied by unusual pain may signal a deeper fracture or internal bleeding. When in doubt, have a medical professional evaluate.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Prevention

- Wear appropriate protective gear (helmets, pads, shin guards) for contact sports and activities.
- Inspect campsites and paths to avoid collisions with rocks, stumps, and tent stakes.
- Use proper technique when lifting or carrying heavy loads.

## Requirement 3b

{{< drg/requirement number="3b" >}}
Superficial, partial thickness, and full thickness thermal (heat) burns or scalds
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Thermal burns result from contact with flames, hot surfaces, steam, or boiling liquids (scalds). Severity is described in three layers, each requiring different treatment.

| Depth | Former name | Appearance | Pain level |
|---|---|---|---|
| Superficial | First-degree | Red, dry, no blisters | High |
| Partial thickness | Second-degree | Red, wet, blisters form | Very high |
| Full thickness | Third-degree | White, brown, or charred; leathery | Low or none (nerve damage) |

### Symptoms and Signs

- **Superficial**: redness, dryness, pain at touch; confined to outer skin layer
- **Partial thickness**: intense redness, moist or weeping surface, blister formation, severe pain
- **Full thickness**: skin appears white, gray, brown, or charred; the surface may look waxy or leathery; little or no pain (nerves destroyed); edges may show partial-thickness burns with intense pain
- For any large burn: watch for signs of shock — pale skin, rapid pulse, altered consciousness

### First Aid

{{< drg/checklist title="First Aid — Thermal Burns" subtitle="Cool the burn, cover it, and get help for serious burns" >}}
- **Remove from heat source** without putting yourself at risk.
- **Cool the burn** with cool (not cold, not ice) running water for 10–20 minutes. This stops ongoing damage and reduces pain. Do NOT use ice — it worsens tissue injury.
- **Remove jewelry and tight clothing** near the burn before swelling begins.
- **Cover loosely** with a clean, non-fluffy dressing or cling wrap to protect from infection.
- **Do NOT** pop blisters, apply butter, oil, toothpaste, or any home remedy.
- **Superficial burns** limited to a small area: treat at home with cool water, cover, and OTC pain reliever.
- **Partial or full-thickness burns**, burns to the face, hands, feet, genitals, or joints, burns larger than the patient's palm, or any burn in a child or elderly person: **call 911 or go to the emergency room**.
- Monitor and treat for shock while waiting for help.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/tip >}}
The "rule of nines" is a quick way to estimate burn size in adults: each arm = 9%, each leg = 18%, front torso = 18%, back torso = 18%, head = 9%. Any burn covering more than roughly 10% of the body surface needs emergency care.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Prevention

- Keep pot handles turned inward on the stove; use oven mitts.
- Set water heater temperature to 120°F (49°C) or lower.
- Keep children away from campfire cooking areas and enforce "arm's length" rules at campfire rings.
- Never leave a campfire, stove, or candle unattended.

## Requirement 3c

{{< drg/requirement number="3c" >}}
Chemical burns
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Chemical burns occur when corrosive substances — acids (battery acid, pool chemicals) or alkalis (drain cleaner, oven cleaner, bleach) — contact skin or eyes. Alkali burns are often more severe because they penetrate deeper and continue to damage tissue long after initial contact.

### Symptoms and Signs

- Burning or stinging pain, sometimes delayed with certain chemicals
- Redness, swelling, and blistering at the contact site
- Color changes in skin — whitening, browning, or darkening
- Eye exposure: intense pain, tearing, inability to open the eye, blurred vision
- With inhaled chemical fumes: coughing, choking, difficulty breathing

### First Aid

{{< drg/checklist title="First Aid — Chemical Burns" subtitle="Flush early, flush long, and call Poison Control" >}}
- **Remove contaminated clothing and jewelry** while protecting yourself — wear gloves if available.
- **Flush with large amounts of cool running water** for at least 20 minutes continuously. Do not use a weak stream; volume and duration matter.
- For **eye exposure**: flush from the inner corner outward with clean water for at least 20 minutes; remove contact lenses if present. Do NOT rub the eye.
- **Do NOT** try to neutralize the chemical with an opposing substance — it can cause a heat reaction and worsen the burn.
- **Call Poison Control (1-800-222-1222)** for specific guidance on the chemical involved.
- Seek emergency care for all significant chemical burns, especially to the face, eyes, or large body areas.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

### Prevention

- Read labels before handling any chemical and follow all safety directions.
- Wear gloves, eye protection, and protective clothing when handling corrosive substances.
- Store household chemicals in original containers with labels intact, out of reach of children.
- Know the location of the nearest eyewash station in labs or workplaces.

## Requirement 3d

{{< drg/requirement number="3d" >}}
Electrical burns
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Electrical burns are deceptive: the external injury may look minor while severe damage runs the length of the current's path through the body. Electricity can cause cardiac arrhythmia, respiratory arrest, and deep internal tissue destruction even when the entry and exit wounds appear small.

### Symptoms and Signs

- Entry wound: small, often circular or charred area where current entered
- Exit wound: often larger and more destructive where current exited (commonly a foot or hand)
- Deep tissue destruction along the current path — not visible externally
- Possible loss of consciousness, confusion, or seizure
- Irregular heartbeat or cardiac arrest
- Muscle pain, spasm, or weakness
- Flash burns to the eyes from arc flash (similar to snow blindness)

### First Aid

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Do NOT touch the patient if they are still in contact with the electrical source. Turn off power at the circuit breaker or use a non-conductive object to separate the patient from the source. Only approach once the scene is confirmed safe.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

{{< drg/checklist title="First Aid — Electrical Burns" subtitle="Scene safety first — then treat as a trauma patient" >}}
- Confirm scene is safe and power is off before approaching.
- **Call 911 immediately** — all significant electrical burns require emergency care.
- Check airway, breathing, and pulse; be prepared to start CPR if the patient is unresponsive and not breathing.
- Treat any visible burns with cool water and a clean dressing.
- Keep the patient still and warm; monitor closely for deteriorating consciousness.
- Do not give anything by mouth.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

### Prevention

- Never use electrical equipment near water.
- Inspect cords and outlets regularly; replace frayed or damaged equipment.
- Keep kites, model rockets, and fishing rods away from power lines.
- In a thunderstorm: use the 30-30 rule (seek shelter when lightning is within 30 seconds of thunder; wait 30 minutes after the last rumble before going back outside).

## Requirement 3e

{{< drg/requirement number="3e" >}}
Sunburn
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Sunburn is UV radiation damage to the outer and deeper layers of skin. It is the most common environmental burn Scouts encounter — and the most preventable.

### Symptoms and Signs

- **Mild (superficial)**: redness, warmth, tenderness; appears 2–6 hours after exposure; peaks at 24 hours
- **Moderate**: intense redness, significant pain, possible swelling
- **Severe**: blistering, fever, chills, headache, nausea, and dehydration (sun poisoning)
- In children: any blistering is serious

### First Aid

{{< drg/checklist title="First Aid — Sunburn" subtitle="Cool, moisturize, hydrate" >}}
- **Get out of the sun** immediately and into shade or indoors.
- **Cool the skin**: apply cool (not cold) wet compresses or take a cool shower for 10–15 minutes.
- **Moisturize**: apply aloe vera gel or a fragrance-free lotion while skin is still damp; avoid petroleum-based products that trap heat.
- **Hydrate**: drink extra water to replace fluid lost through damaged skin.
- **Pain relief**: OTC ibuprofen or acetaminophen helps with pain and inflammation.
- **Do NOT pop blisters** — they protect the skin underneath.
- **Cover blistered skin** loosely with a non-stick dressing.
- **Seek medical care** if the patient has severe blistering over a large area, fever above 103°F (39.4°C), extreme pain, dizziness, or confusion.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

### Prevention

- Apply SPF 30 or higher broad-spectrum sunscreen 15–30 minutes before sun exposure; reapply every 2 hours and after swimming or sweating.
- Wear UPF-rated clothing, a wide-brimmed hat, and UV-blocking sunglasses.
- Seek shade between 10 a.m. and 4 p.m. when UV is strongest.
- Remember high-altitude and reflective surfaces (snow, water, sand) intensify UV exposure significantly.

## Requirement 3f

{{< drg/requirement number="3f" >}}
Snow blindness
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Snow blindness (photokeratitis) is a sunburn of the cornea — the clear front surface of the eye — caused by intense UV reflection off snow, ice, or water. It can occur even on overcast days.

### Symptoms and Signs

- Symptoms typically appear **4–12 hours after exposure** (like sunburn, delayed onset)
- Intense eye pain, burning, or a gritty "sand in the eye" sensation
- Excessive tearing, red eyes
- Sensitivity to light (photophobia)
- Blurred or temporarily reduced vision
- Swollen eyelids
- Temporary vision loss in severe cases

### First Aid

{{< drg/checklist title="First Aid — Snow Blindness" subtitle="Rest the eyes — UV damage heals on its own within 24–48 hours" >}}
- **Move the patient out of bright light** and into a dark or dimly lit shelter.
- **Remove contact lenses** if present.
- **Cover both eyes** with a dark, cool, damp cloth to reduce pain and light exposure — do not patch tightly.
- **Do NOT rub the eyes.**
- OTC pain relievers (ibuprofen or acetaminophen) can reduce pain and inflammation.
- Cold compresses may provide additional comfort.
- Most cases resolve within **24–48 hours** with rest.
- **Seek medical care** if vision does not improve, pain is severe, or vision loss is significant.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

### Prevention

- Wear **CE-certified or ANSI-rated sunglasses or glacier goggles** that block 99–100% of UVA and UVB rays whenever traveling on snow, ice, or open water.
- Even on cloudy days in snowy terrain, UV reflectance is high enough to cause snow blindness.
- Improvise eye protection with cardboard or bark with narrow slits cut across the eyes in a survival situation.

## Requirement 3g

{{< drg/requirement number="3g" >}}
Immersion foot, frostnip, frostbite, and ice burns
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

These are four distinct cold injuries, each representing a different degree and mechanism of tissue damage. Cold environment awareness and early recognition are what separate minor discomfort from permanent disability.

### Immersion Foot (Trench Foot)

Caused by prolonged exposure to **cold, wet conditions** (usually 32–60°F / 0–16°C) — not necessarily freezing. Tissue is damaged by reduced circulation from sustained cold and moisture, not ice crystal formation.

**Symptoms/Signs**: pale, cold, numb feet with swollen appearance; red and intensely painful when warmed; possible blistering; skin may look mottled or blotchy.

**First Aid**: Remove wet socks and footwear; gently dry and warm the feet (body heat, warm dry socks); do not rub; elevate; keep dry; seek medical evaluation for any blistering or significant swelling.

**Prevention**: Change into dry socks regularly on wet expeditions; treat any wet footwear quickly; waterproof boots for cold/wet environments.

### Frostnip

**Frostnip** is early, reversible cold injury — the skin surface cools significantly but tissue does not freeze.

**Symptoms/Signs**: pale, cold, slightly numb skin — usually on nose, ears, cheeks, chin, fingertips, toes; skin whitens but remains soft and pliable; full sensation returns with rewarming.

**First Aid**: Get indoors or out of the wind; rewarm affected skin gently with body heat (cup hands over ears or nose; place affected fingers in armpits); do not rub; avoid refreezing once rewarmed.

**Prevention**: Cover all exposed skin in cold, windy conditions; use the "buddy system" to watch each other's faces for early whitening.

### Frostbite

**Frostbite** occurs when tissue actually freezes — ice crystals form inside cells, rupturing them. It is a medical emergency and can result in permanent loss of tissue or limbs.

**Symptoms/Signs**:

| Degree | Appearance | Feeling |
|---|---|---|
| Superficial (surface) | White or grayish-yellow; firm on top but soft underneath | Numbness, aching, burning after rewarming |
| Deep | Hard, white, or mottled blue; wooden texture all the way through | No sensation at all |

**First Aid**:

{{< drg/checklist title="First Aid — Frostbite" subtitle="Do not rewarm in the field if refreezing is possible" >}}
- **Move the patient to a warm environment** and remove wet clothing and jewelry from affected areas.
- **Do NOT rewarm if there is any chance the tissue will refreeze** — refreezing causes far greater damage. Walking on a frostbitten foot to reach shelter is safer than thawing it in the field.
- If definitive shelter is available: rewarm in **warm water (99–102°F / 37–39°C)** — not hot — for 15–30 minutes, until the area becomes red and soft. This is painful.
- **Handle the affected area gently** — tissue is fragile. Do not rub, massage, or apply direct heat (fire, heating pad).
- Cover loosely with a sterile dressing; pad between toes/fingers.
- Give warm drinks if the patient is alert and can swallow safely.
- **Call 911 or evacuate** — deep frostbite requires hospital care.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

### Ice Burns

**Ice burns** occur when ice or very cold objects are held directly against skin — most commonly from misuse of ice packs. They look and behave like thermal burns.

**Symptoms/Signs**: redness, then white or gray discoloration; pain initially, then numbness; blistering in severe cases.

**First Aid**: Remove the cold object; treat as superficial frostbite (gently rewarm with body heat or warm water); cover loosely; seek care if blistering occurs.

**Prevention**: Always wrap ice packs in a cloth before applying; never apply ice directly to bare skin for more than a few minutes.

### General Cold Injury Prevention

- Layer clothing (moisture-wicking base, insulating middle, waterproof/windproof outer).
- Keep extremities covered and dry; carry extra socks and gloves.
- Fuel properly — dehydration and low blood sugar accelerate cold injuries.
- Recognize early numbness and act before it progresses.

## Requirement 3h

{{< drg/requirement number="3h" >}}
Abrasions, such as chafing and rope burns
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Abrasions are surface wounds caused by friction stripping away the outer layer(s) of skin. They are common outdoors and are usually minor — but they carry infection risk if dirty, and they can expand rapidly in high-friction activities.

### Symptoms and Signs

- **Chafing**: red, raw, burning skin at areas of repeated skin-on-skin or clothing-on-skin friction (inner thighs, underarms, nipples, shoulders under pack straps)
- **Rope burns**: linear or circular abrasion where rope slid rapidly across skin; redness, rawness, possible fluid weeping; can be surprisingly deep
- Both: stinging or burning pain; possible minor bleeding (oozing) from the raw surface; risk of swelling and infection if not cleaned

### First Aid

{{< drg/checklist title="First Aid — Abrasions" subtitle="Clean well, cover, and keep dry" >}}
- **Stop the activity** causing the friction.
- **Rinse the wound** with clean water; remove any visible debris gently.
- **Clean gently** with antiseptic wipes or mild soap and water.
- **Apply antibiotic ointment** to reduce infection risk.
- **Cover** with a non-stick dressing or adhesive bandage.
- For chafing: apply a skin-protection product (petroleum jelly, anti-chafe balm, or a blister bandage) once cleaned and covered.
- Monitor for signs of infection over the next 24–48 hours: increasing redness, warmth, swelling, pus, or fever.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

### Prevention

- Wear moisture-wicking, seamless, or padded garments at friction-prone areas.
- Apply anti-chafe products before long hikes or activities.
- Use proper rope-handling technique: grip firmly, control descents, wear leather or heavy-duty gloves when working with ropes.
- Pack hip belt and shoulder pads in a properly fitted backpack.

## Requirement 3i

{{< drg/requirement number="3i" >}}
Blisters on the hands, feet, buttocks, and shoulders
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A blister is a fluid-filled bubble that forms when friction separates the outer skin layers. It is the skin's natural shock absorber — but once it forms, it signals that the underlying tissue is being damaged and needs protection.

### Symptoms and Signs

- A "hot spot" — localized burning or redness — precedes blister formation
- Visible raised, fluid-filled bubble (clear or slightly yellowish)
- Tenderness and pain with pressure
- Possible surrounding redness and swelling
- An intact blister is best left alone; a burst blister exposes raw skin and increases infection risk

### First Aid

{{< drg/checklist title="First Aid — Blisters" subtitle="Intact blisters: protect. Torn blisters: clean and cover." >}}
- **Intact blister**: do not pop it. Clean the area, apply a donut-shaped moleskin pad (the hole centers over the blister to relieve pressure) or a blister-specific bandage, and return to activity if comfortable.
- **Burst or torn blister**: clean gently with antiseptic wipe; leave the skin flap in place as a natural dressing; apply antibiotic ointment; cover with a non-stick dressing and moleskin over it.
- Change the dressing daily or when wet; watch for infection.
- **For blisters on shoulders or buttocks** from pack or saddle friction: clean, cover with a non-stick dressing, and use gel padding or foam to reduce ongoing pressure.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/tip >}}
Catch hot spots early — before the blister forms. Stop as soon as you feel unusual friction or burning, apply moleskin or tape to the spot, and continue. Waiting until a full blister develops is more painful and harder to treat in the field.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Prevention

- Break in new boots before a long hike.
- Wear moisture-wicking socks (wool or synthetic); change into dry socks if feet get wet.
- Use liner socks under heavier hiking socks to redirect friction away from skin.
- Apply tape or blister prevention products to known hot spots before activity.
- Ensure packs are properly fitted; adjust shoulder and hip belts to prevent slipping.

## Requirement 3j

{{< drg/requirement number="3j" >}}
Puncture wounds from splinters, rope splinters, nails, and fish hooks
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Puncture wounds drive foreign material deep into skin on a narrow path. The entry point closes quickly, trapping bacteria — which is why puncture wounds carry a high infection risk despite looking minor.

### Symptoms and Signs

- Small entry point; often minor or no external bleeding
- Pain at point of penetration; tenderness when pressed
- Possible visible foreign object (splinter, hook); or object may be fully embedded
- Swelling and redness developing hours later
- If infected: increasing warmth, pus, streaking redness (red lines spreading from wound indicate spreading infection — a medical emergency), fever

### First Aid

**Splinters and small wooden/metal slivers:**

{{< drg/checklist title="First Aid — Splinters and Surface Punctures" subtitle="Remove if accessible; clean thoroughly" >}}
- Clean the site with antiseptic wipe or soap and water.
- Sterilize tweezers with alcohol or flame (let cool before use).
- Grasp the splinter as close to the skin as possible; pull out at the same angle it entered.
- Squeeze gently to encourage minor bleeding, which helps flush bacteria.
- Clean again; apply antibiotic ointment; cover with adhesive bandage.
- **Do not dig for deeply embedded objects** — seek medical care.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

**Nail or deep puncture wounds:**

- Clean the wound and surrounding skin thoroughly.
- Do not attempt to enlarge the wound.
- Cover with a clean dressing.
- Seek medical care — tetanus booster may be needed; deep punctures often require professional evaluation.

**Fish hooks:**

{{< drg/checklist title="First Aid — Fish Hook Removal" subtitle="Use the string-yank technique for shallow barbed hooks when medical care is not immediately available" >}}
- If the barb is visible and near the surface: push the hook through, cut off the barb with wire cutters, and back the hook out.
- **String-yank technique**: loop fishing line around the bend of the hook; press the hook shank down to disengage the barb; give a sharp tug along the axis of the hook.
- Flush well, apply antibiotic ointment, and cover.
- Seek medical care for hooks near the eye, deeply embedded hooks, or any sign of infection.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Any puncture wound that has not been fully cleaned, is in the foot, or involved a rusty or dirty object should be evaluated by a medical professional. A tetanus immunization up to date within 5 years (or 10 years for clean wounds) is important for puncture wound management.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Prevention

- Wear close-toed shoes and gloves when working with nails, tools, or sharp material.
- Use proper fishhook safety: crimp barbs for practice casts, keep hooks in containers, avoid risky casting angles around people.
- Use rope gloves when handling rough rope under load.

## Requirement 3k

{{< drg/requirement number="3k" >}}
Rash from poisonous plants
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Poison ivy, poison oak, and poison sumac all produce **urushiol** — an oily resin that causes an allergic contact dermatitis in most people. The rash itself is not contagious and does not spread, but urushiol can transfer from skin to clothes to gear and cause repeated re-exposure.

### Symptoms and Signs

- Rash typically appears **12–72 hours** after contact; up to a week in first-time exposures
- Intense itching — often the first symptom
- Red streaks or patches; bumps or swollen skin
- Blisters that weep clear fluid (fluid does NOT spread the rash — the blister fluid is not urushiol)
- Swelling, especially near the eyes and mouth
- Seek emergency care if rash involves the face, eyes, or throat, or if there is difficulty breathing (sign of severe systemic reaction)

### First Aid

{{< drg/checklist title="First Aid — Poisonous Plant Rash" subtitle="Wash immediately after any suspected exposure" >}}
- **Wash all exposed skin** with soap and cool water as soon as possible after contact — within 10 minutes ideally, but up to 2 hours can reduce severity. Scrub gently; do not scrub vigorously (can drive urushiol deeper).
- **Wash clothing, gear, and tools** that may have contacted the plant — urushiol stays active on surfaces for years.
- **Calamine lotion, hydrocortisone cream, or OTC antihistamines** reduce itching for most cases.
- **Cool compresses** provide temporary itch relief.
- **Do not scratch** — increases infection risk.
- Seek medical care for widespread rashes, severe blistering, rash on the face or genitals, or any difficulty breathing.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

### Prevention

- **Learn to identify** poison ivy ("leaves of three, let it be"), poison oak, and poison sumac in your region — patterns vary by season and geography.
- Wear long sleeves, long pants, and closed-toe shoes in areas where plants grow.
- Apply barrier creams (Ivy Block / bentoquatam) before exposure in high-risk areas.
- Wash all exposed skin and gear promptly after outdoor activities in suspect areas.
- **Never burn poison ivy** — inhaled smoke causes severe internal inflammation and can be life-threatening.

## Requirement 3l

{{< drg/requirement number="3l" >}}
Bug bites of chiggers, ticks, mosquitoes, and biting gnats
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Insect bites are the most frequent minor injury Scouts encounter outdoors. Most are just an annoyance — but ticks in particular carry serious disease risks that require prompt, correct removal.

### Symptoms and Signs

- **Chiggers**: intense itching appearing 3–6 hours after exposure; red welts, often clustered near waistbands, sock lines, or other tight areas; the bite site develops a raised red bump that persists for days
- **Ticks**: often painless at attachment; swelling, redness at bite site; possible "bull's-eye" rash (erythema migrans) days to weeks later — a classic early sign of Lyme disease; flu-like symptoms (fever, fatigue, joint pain) after tick exposure are a red flag
- **Mosquitoes**: immediate redness, localized swelling, itching; generally minor except in areas with mosquito-borne disease
- **Biting gnats**: immediate sharp pain; small red bump; intense itching that may persist for days

### First Aid

**Chigger bites**: Wash the area with soap and water to remove any remaining chiggers; apply calamine lotion or hydrocortisone cream; OTC antihistamines reduce itching; avoid scratching.

**Tick removal**:

{{< drg/checklist title="Correct Tick Removal" subtitle="Fine-tipped tweezers only — no heat, no petroleum jelly" >}}
- Use fine-tipped tweezers (not fingers) to grasp the tick as close to the skin surface as possible.
- Pull **upward with steady, even pressure** — do not twist or jerk (can cause mouthparts to break off).
- After removal, clean the bite area with antiseptic wipe or soap and water.
- **Do NOT** use petroleum jelly, nail polish, heat, or other methods — these can cause the tick to inject more secretions.
- Save the tick in a sealed bag with the date noted; it may help with disease identification if symptoms develop.
- Monitor for 30 days; seek medical care if a bull's-eye rash, fever, or joint pain appears.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

**Mosquito bites**: Wash; apply ice or cold compress for swelling; hydrocortisone cream or calamine for itch.

**Biting gnats**: Wash; apply cold compress; hydrocortisone cream or OTC antihistamine for relief.

### Prevention

- Apply EPA-registered insect repellent (DEET, picaridin, or IR3535) to exposed skin and clothing.
- Wear long sleeves, long pants (tucked into socks), and a hat in tick or gnat country.
- Conduct a full tick check on all body areas (scalp, behind knees, armpits, groin) within 2 hours of being outdoors.
- Treat gear and clothing with permethrin for added protection.

## Requirement 3m

{{< drg/requirement number="3m" >}}
Bee stings
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Most bee stings cause localized pain and swelling that resolves within a few hours. The danger is anaphylaxis — a severe, life-threatening allergic reaction that can occur in people with known or unknown bee venom allergy.

### Symptoms and Signs

**Normal local reaction** (most people):

- Immediate sharp pain at the sting site
- Redness, swelling, and itching limited to the sting area
- Symptoms resolve within a few hours

**Large local reaction** (some people):

- Swelling that expands significantly (more than 2 inches) over 24–48 hours
- More pronounced redness and itching; may persist for a week

**Anaphylaxis** — call 911 immediately if any of these appear:

- Hives, itching, or flushing spreading across the body
- Swelling of the throat, tongue, or lips
- Difficulty breathing, wheezing, or hoarse voice
- Nausea, vomiting, abdominal cramping
- Dizziness, rapid pulse, drop in blood pressure, fainting

### First Aid

{{< drg/checklist title="First Aid — Bee Sting" subtitle="Remove the stinger fast — the method matters less than the speed" >}}
- **Remove the stinger immediately** — scrape it sideways with a fingernail, credit card edge, or dull blade. Do NOT pinch or squeeze the stinger — that injects more venom.
- **Wash the area** with soap and water.
- Apply a **cold pack** wrapped in cloth for 15–20 minutes to reduce swelling and pain.
- OTC **antihistamine** (diphenhydramine/Benadryl) and **hydrocortisone cream** reduce itch and swelling.
- OTC pain reliever (ibuprofen or acetaminophen) for pain.
- **For anaphylaxis**: use the patient's epinephrine auto-injector (EpiPen) if available; call 911 immediately; position the patient lying flat with legs elevated (or sitting up if breathing is difficult); be ready to perform CPR.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Any Scout or leader known to have bee venom allergy should carry a prescribed epinephrine auto-injector at all outdoor activities. Make sure at least two adults know where it is and how to use it.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Prevention

- Avoid wearing bright floral clothing, strong perfumes, or scented products outdoors in bee season.
- Keep food and sweet drinks covered at outdoor meals.
- Stay calm and move slowly when bees are present — swatting provokes stinging.
- Know who in your group has allergies and where their epinephrine auto-injector is kept.

## Requirement 3n

{{< drg/requirement number="3n" >}}
Bites of spiders
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

The vast majority of spider bites in the US are harmless. Two spiders that require specific recognition and treatment are the **black widow** and the **brown recluse**.

### Symptoms and Signs

**Most spider bites** (harmless species):

- Minor redness, swelling, and itching at the bite site
- Resolves within a few hours to a day

**Black widow bite**:

- Small puncture mark; immediate sharp pain followed by dull ache
- Within 30–60 minutes: muscle cramps and spasms radiating from the bite (often severe in the abdomen, back, or chest)
- Sweating, nausea, vomiting, headache, high blood pressure
- Rarely fatal in healthy adults, but highly distressing; children and elderly are at greater risk

**Brown recluse bite**:

- Initial mild burning or stinging — may be overlooked
- Over 4–8 hours: redness, blistering, tissue death (necrosis) forming a deep ulcer at the bite
- Possible flu-like symptoms (fever, chills, body aches) in more severe reactions
- Tissue destruction can be extensive and slow-healing

### First Aid

{{< drg/checklist title="First Aid — Spider Bite" subtitle="Treat symptomatically; seek care if black widow or brown recluse is suspected" >}}
- **Wash the bite area** with soap and water.
- Apply a **cold pack** (wrapped in cloth) to reduce swelling and pain.
- Mark the edge of any redness with a pen and note the time — this helps track progression.
- **For black widow**: seek emergency care promptly; antivenom is available and effective.
- **For brown recluse**: seek medical care; there is no antivenom, but medical management reduces complications; photograph the bite area for tracking.
- Watch for signs of allergic reaction or anaphylaxis (rare).
- Capture or photograph the spider if it can be done safely — identification helps medical treatment.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

### Prevention

- Shake out footwear, clothing, and gear before putting them on when camping in spider territory.
- Wear gloves when moving logs, rocks, or stored wood.
- Inspect sleeping bags and tent corners before use.
- Black widows favor dark, sheltered areas (outhouses, wood piles); brown recluses like indoor clutter and undisturbed storage.

## Requirement 3o

{{< drg/requirement number="3o" >}}
Sting of a scorpion
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Scorpions are found across the American Southwest, as well as in parts of the Southeast and tropical regions. In the US, the **Arizona bark scorpion** (*Centruroides sculpturatus*) is the only species considered medically dangerous.

### Symptoms and Signs

**Most scorpion stings** (non-dangerous species):

- Immediate, intense pain at the sting site
- Local swelling, redness, and warmth
- Numbness or tingling near the site
- Symptoms typically resolve within a few hours

**Arizona bark scorpion** (serious toxin):

- Intense pain, burning, tingling at the sting site
- Numbness, muscle twitching, and uncontrolled eye movements
- Drooling, difficulty swallowing, or slurred speech
- Agitation, restlessness, unusual body movements
- Respiratory distress in severe cases
- Children are most at risk for severe reactions

### First Aid

{{< drg/checklist title="First Aid — Scorpion Sting" subtitle="Call Poison Control; emergency care for neurological symptoms" >}}
- **Wash the sting area** with soap and water.
- Apply a **cold pack** (wrapped in cloth) for pain relief.
- **Call Poison Control (1-800-222-1222)** for guidance — they can assess severity based on the species and symptoms.
- **Call 911** immediately if the patient (especially a child) shows neurological symptoms: muscle twitching, drooling, difficulty breathing, eye movement problems.
- Keep the patient calm and still.
- OTC pain reliever for mild stings.
- Antivenom (*Anascorp*) is available at emergency departments for severe reactions.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

### Prevention

- **Check shoes, clothing, and sleeping gear** before use when camping in scorpion territory — bark scorpions hide in dark, confined spaces.
- Do not walk barefoot outside at night in desert regions.
- Wear gloves when moving rocks, firewood, or debris.
- Caulk gaps in cabins or structures where scorpions may enter.

## Requirement 3p

{{< drg/requirement number="3p" >}}
Bite of a pet or wild mammal or human
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Animal and human bites carry an elevated infection risk from the bacteria naturally present in the mouth. The primary concerns are wound infection, **rabies** (from wild or unvaccinated animals), and **tetanus**.

### Symptoms and Signs

- Puncture marks, lacerations, or crushing injuries from teeth
- Immediate pain, swelling, and bleeding (even minor bites can be deep)
- Signs of infection developing within 12–72 hours: increasing redness, warmth, swelling, pus, red streaks radiating from the wound, fever
- **Rabies warning**: symptoms of rabies infection (agitation, confusion, hydrophobia) can appear weeks to months after a bite — by then treatment is often too late; prophylaxis must begin immediately after exposure

### First Aid

{{< drg/checklist title="First Aid — Animal or Human Bite" subtitle="Flush aggressively — depth of flushing reduces rabies and infection risk" >}}
- **Wash immediately and thoroughly** with soap and water for at least 5 minutes; flushing the wound vigorously is one of the most effective ways to reduce infection and rabies virus transmission.
- **Control any bleeding** with direct pressure.
- **Apply antibiotic ointment** and cover with a clean dressing.
- **Seek medical care for all bites** that break the skin — antibiotic prophylaxis and wound evaluation are typically recommended.
- **Report the animal**: for wild animals or unvaccinated domestic animals, medical professionals will assess rabies risk and initiate post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) if needed. PEP is very effective when started promptly.
- For confirmed or suspected rabies exposure: **do not wait for symptoms** — begin PEP immediately. Once symptoms appear, survival is rare.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Never approach a wild animal that appears unafraid of humans, is acting erratically, or is active during daylight hours when it should not be (e.g., a nocturnal animal seen in daytime) — these are possible signs of rabies. Contact local animal control or park rangers.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Prevention

- Do not approach, feed, or attempt to handle wild animals.
- Do not disturb a dog while it is eating, sleeping, or caring for pups.
- Keep pets current on rabies and other vaccinations.
- Teach younger Scouts to ask before petting any dog and to read animal body language.

## Requirement 3q

{{< drg/requirement number="3q" >}}
Bite of a venomous snake
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Venomous snakebite is a wilderness medical emergency. In North America, the primary venomous snakes are pit vipers (rattlesnakes, copperheads, cottonmouths/water moccasins) and coral snakes. Pit viper venom is primarily hemotoxic — it damages blood and tissue. Coral snake venom is primarily neurotoxic — it attacks the nervous system.

### Symptoms and Signs

**Pit viper bite (rattlesnake, copperhead, cottonmouth)**:

- One or two fang puncture marks
- Immediate intense pain and burning at the bite site
- Rapid swelling, bruising, and discoloration spreading from the bite (can be dramatic)
- Nausea, vomiting, dizziness, weakness
- Metallic or rubber taste in the mouth
- In severe envenomation: severe bleeding, blood pressure drop, shock

**Coral snake bite** (less common, but serious):

- Fang marks may be small or not visible
- Local pain is often mild initially; swelling may be minor
- Neurological symptoms appear hours later: double vision, drooping eyelids, slurred speech, difficulty swallowing, muscle weakness
- Can progress to respiratory failure without timely antivenom

**Dry bite**: Up to 25% of pit viper bites are "dry" — venom is not injected. Symptoms remain local and mild. This cannot be assumed at the scene.

### First Aid

{{< drg/checklist title="First Aid — Venomous Snake Bite" subtitle="Get to a hospital with antivenom — that is the definitive treatment" >}}
- **Keep the patient still and calm** — movement accelerates venom absorption.
- **Call 911** or evacuate immediately; antivenom is the only effective treatment and is available only at medical facilities.
- **Keep the bite area at or below heart level** while transporting.
- **Remove rings, watches, and tight clothing** near the bite before swelling makes them a tourniquet.
- **Note the time of the bite** and monitor and document symptoms and their progression.
- **Do NOT**: cut and suck the wound, apply ice, apply a tourniquet, or use electrical shock — these are ineffective and can cause additional harm.
- **Do NOT** try to capture or kill the snake — bring a photo if safe to do so. Many bites happen when people try to handle or kill the snake.
- Keep the patient hydrated with water if alert and stable.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Antivenom must be administered in a medical setting. In the US, call Poison Control (1-800-222-1222) en route to the hospital — they can advise the receiving facility on antivenom type and dosing. Time from bite to antivenom strongly affects outcome.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Prevention

- Watch where you place your hands and feet — never reach into crevices, under rocks, or into brush piles you cannot see into.
- Wear tall boots and long pants in snake habitat.
- Give any snake you encounter space and a path to retreat.
- Learn to identify the venomous snakes in your region before camping or hiking there.
- Most bites happen when people are trying to handle or kill snakes — leave them alone.
