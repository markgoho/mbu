{{< drg/requirement number="8c4" >}}
After gaining permission, collect, identify, press, mount, and label 10 different plants that are common in your area. Tell why voucher specimens are important for documentation of a field botanist's discoveries.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A pressed plant specimen is more than a souvenir. It is a record of what was found, where it was found, and when it was collected. That is why botanists call well-documented specimens **voucher specimens**—they support later study and prove that an identification was made from a real plant.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Never collect plants without permission, and never collect rare, protected, or unknown species just to fill your set. This requirement is for plants that are common in your area.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Basic Specimen Workflow

{{< drg/checklist title="From Field to Mounted Specimen" subtitle="Build a set your counselor can review clearly" >}}
- Collect a representative piece that shows important features.
- Write down location, date, habitat, and collector name immediately.
- Arrange the plant flat in newspaper or blotting paper.
- Put it in a press or under steady weight so it dries evenly.
- Mount the dried specimen on sturdy paper.
- Add a label with the common name, scientific name, location, date, and notes.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

## What Makes a Good Voucher Specimen

A good specimen shows the traits someone would need to identify it again later. Flowers, fruits, leaf arrangement, and stem details are especially helpful. A crushed leaf without a label is not a useful voucher.

Voucher specimens matter because they let other people verify what was found. They also become part of long-term records used in conservation, ecology, and range mapping. A specimen collected decades ago can still help scientists understand what used to grow in a place.
