{{< drg/requirement number="12" >}}
In deep water, show how to escape from a victim's grasp on your wrist. Repeat for front and rear holds about the head and shoulders.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

This is the requirement that could save your life as a rescuer. No matter how careful you are — no matter how well you use lead-and-wait and buoyant aids — there may come a moment when a panicking victim grabs you. If that happens, you need to know how to break free immediately, before they push you underwater.

### The Principle Behind Every Escape

Every escape technique is built on one simple fact: **a panicking victim wants to stay at the surface.** If you submerge, they will let go. Their survival instinct forces them to push away from anything that is going down and reach for anything that is going up.

This means your escape strategy always starts the same way:

1. **Take a deep breath.** You are about to go underwater.
2. **Tuck your chin.** Protect your airway.
3. **Submerge.** Push yourself down.
4. **Break the grip** using the specific technique for that hold.
5. **Swim away underwater** and resurface at a safe distance.
6. **Reassess and try again** — this time with a buoyant aid and better positioning.

### Wrist Grab Escape

A victim grabs your wrist as you extend your arm toward them.

**Technique:**
1. **Take a breath and submerge.** Go down.
2. **Rotate your wrist** toward the victim's thumb. The thumb is the weakest point in any grip.
3. **Pull sharply** in the direction of their thumb gap. Your wrist will slide free.
4. **Swim away underwater** — at least 6 feet — and resurface.

**Key detail:** Always turn toward the thumb. Even if a person has a crushing grip, the opening between their thumb and fingers is a natural weak point that you can exploit.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Practice the wrist escape on dry land first with a partner. Have them grab your wrist firmly, then practice the rotate-and-pull motion until it becomes automatic. The move needs to work on the first try — in the water, you may not get a second chance.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Front Head Hold Escape

A victim facing you wraps both arms around your head and shoulders, pressing you against their chest.

**Technique:**
1. **Take a breath and tuck your chin** against the victim's chest. This protects your airway.
2. **Submerge.** Push yourself downward by bending at the waist and driving your hips under the victim.
3. **Place both hands on the victim's hips** and push them away from you as you go deeper.
4. **Turn the victim** as you push — spin them so their back is toward you.
5. **Swim away underwater** behind them and resurface at a safe distance.
6. **If possible**, transition to a rear approach position for a safer rescue attempt.

### Rear Head Hold Escape

A victim behind you wraps both arms around your head and shoulders from the rear.

**Technique:**
1. **Take a breath and tuck your chin.** The victim's arms are around your neck from behind — tucking your chin protects your airway and creates space.
2. **Submerge.** Drive yourself downward.
3. **Grab the victim's elbow** (the arm that is highest or most constraining) with both hands.
4. **Push the arm up and over your head** while you duck under it.
5. **Spin to face the victim** as you come free. Keep hold of their arm.
6. **Push them away** and swim to a safe distance.
7. **Resurface** behind or to the side of the victim.

### After the Escape

Breaking free is only half the job. Once you have escaped:

- **Catch your breath.** You just performed an intense maneuver underwater. Take a moment.
- **Reassess the situation.** Is the victim still active? Have they tired out? Are they unconscious now?
- **Get a buoyant aid** if one is available. Do not approach again without one.
- **Use lead-and-wait** if the victim is still actively panicking.
- **Approach from the rear** if you must make contact again. The rear approach gives you the most control and the least chance of being grabbed.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
If a victim grabs you and you cannot break free, you are in serious danger. Do not try to out-muscle them. Go down — always go down. A panicking person will release anything that sinks. Your life depends on this reflex.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

### Common Mistakes

{{< drg/checklist title="Escape Pitfalls" subtitle="Errors that can get you trapped" >}}
- Trying to break free at the surface — you must submerge first
- Forgetting to take a breath before going under
- Not tucking your chin — leaving your throat exposed to compression
- Surfacing too close to the victim — they will grab you again
- Panicking yourself — stay calm, trust your training, and execute the technique
- Giving up after one failed attempt — try again, go deeper
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/be-prepared title="A Victim Grabs You and Won't Let Go" >}}
You are in the water and a panicking swimmer lunges at you, wrapping arms around your head from the front. Your head is being pushed underwater.

- **Do not fight.** Fighting at the surface wastes energy and keeps you in danger.
- **Take a breath** if you can, then **tuck your chin** and **submerge.**
- **Place your hands on the victim's hips** and push firmly downward and away.
- **Swim underwater** behind the victim and surface at least 10 feet away.
- **Shout for backup** if anyone else is nearby.
- **Do not approach again** without a buoyant aid between you and the victim.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Lifeguard training programs typically require rescuers to practice escape techniques blindfolded or with their eyes closed to simulate the disorientation of being grabbed and pulled underwater. The goal is to make the escape response completely automatic — a reflex, not a decision.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}
